## 2. Summary

A safety plan is a short, personal, written list of steps to follow when a crisis starts, made on a calmer day so it is
ready on a hard one. The Stanley–Brown version has six steps in a set order: notice your warning signs; do things on your
own that help; go to people and places that take your mind off it; ask family or friends for help; contact
professionals and crisis lines; and make your surroundings safer, especially by putting distance between yourself and
anything you could use to hurt yourself. Many versions add a final line: the thing most worth living for. The plan is
meant to be made *together* with a clinician or a trusted person, in your own words, and kept where you can reach it. In
a large study of veterans seen in emergency departments, safety planning plus follow-up phone calls was linked to about
45% fewer suicidal behaviours over six months and more people attending treatment. Keeping Watch already prints a
seven-question safety plan page at the back of every monthly book and shows a plan on the X4 after a long press of Back.
This doc sets out the method, the evidence, and small changes that would bring those closer to the evidence-based form.

## 3. History and origin

**Who created it.** Barbara Stanley was a psychologist and suicide researcher at Columbia University and the New York
State Psychiatric Institute. Gregory K. Brown is a clinical psychologist and Research Professor at the University of
Pennsylvania, known for cognitive therapy for suicide prevention. The official site credits both as developers.

**Timeline.**

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1990s–2000s | "No-suicide contracts" are common in practice despite no good evidence (McMyler and Pryjmachuk, 2008) |
| Mid-2000s | Stanley and Brown build a safety plan into Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy for Suicide Prevention (CBT-SP) for adolescents after a suicide attempt (published 2009) |
| 2008 | Stanley–Brown Safety Plan form first copyrighted; VA safety planning manual (veteran version) developed **(uncertain on exact manual date)** |
| 2009 | CBT-SP paper: the safety plan was delivered to 98% of 110 adolescents (Stanley et al., 2009) |
| 2012 | "Safety Planning Intervention: A brief intervention to mitigate suicide risk," *Cognitive and Behavioral Practice* (Stanley and Brown) |
| 2017 | Bryan et al. RCT: a related crisis response plan beat a "contract for safety" in soldiers |
| 2018 | Stanley et al., *JAMA Psychiatry*: SPI plus follow-up calls in 5 VA emergency departments vs 4 usual-care sites |
| 2021 | Nuij et al. meta-analysis; form updated |
| 2022 | 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline launches in the US (July 2022) |
| 2024 | Current English form dated 05-02-2024 |
| 2025 | 988's LGBTQ+ youth "press 3" option ends on 17 July 2025 |

**Cultural context.** Safety planning spread through the US Veterans Health Administration, then emergency departments,
schools and the Zero Suicide framework. It replaced "no-suicide contracts", where a person promised not to harm
themselves. Reviews found no evidence those contracts work and raised ethical concerns about coercion (McMyler and
Pryjmachuk, 2008). Safety planning moved the question from "promise you won't" to "here is what we'll do".

**Key quotes.** From the official site: "The Safety Planning Intervention is more than completing the Safety Plan form,"
and it is "a collaborative process between the clinician and the individual." From Means Matter (Harvard): "Many suicide
attempts occur with little planning during a short-term crisis."

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- **Crises pass.** Suicidal crises often rise and fall over a short time. A plan helps you get through the peak.
- **Prepare when calm.** Thinking is harder in a crisis, so decide the steps in advance.
- **Start with yourself, widen out.** Each step brings in more support: your own coping, then people and places, then
  help from friends and family, then professionals.
- **Your words, not a clinician's.** Warning signs and coping steps work better when they sound like you.
- **Specific and doable.** "Call Jo at 555-0100" beats "reach out"; "walk to the corner store" beats "exercise".
- **Plan for barriers.** For each step, ask "what might get in the way?" and solve it now.
- **Make the environment safer.** Distance from lethal means during a crisis saves lives.
- **Reachable.** A plan in a drawer at home doesn't help at work. Keep it where you'll be.
- **A living document.** Review and revise after use, after big life changes, and at follow-ups.
- **Not a contract.** Nobody is promising anything. It's a tool, not a test.

**The problem it solves.** In a crisis, people often can't recall what helps, who to call, or the crisis number, and
they may be alone with something dangerous. The plan answers those questions ahead of time.

## 5. Core components

| Step (Stanley–Brown order) | Question it answers | Keeping Watch paper page (current) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Warning signs | How will I know it's time to use this? | 1. Signs a hard time is starting |
| 2. Internal coping strategies | What can I do on my own? | 2. Things I can do on my own to feel a little better |
| 3. People and social settings that provide distraction | Where can I go, who can I be around? | 3. People or places that help me get my mind off it |
| 4. People I can ask for help | Who can I tell I'm struggling? | 4. People I can text for help |
| 5. Professionals and agencies to contact | Who do I call in a crisis? | 5. Professionals: therapist, prescriber, 988, crisis line |
| 6. Making the environment safe | How can I put distance between me and means? | 6. How I can make my space safer |
| (Final line) Reason for living | What is most worth living for? | 7. What matters to me, worth staying for |
| (Not on SB form) A text script | What can I send when talking is too hard? | "A text I can send…" box |

The intervention also includes tasks beyond the form: a brief review of the recent crisis, explaining how the plan works,
asking how likely the person is to use each step, solving barriers, deciding where to keep the plan, and follow-up
contact to review it (Stanley et al., 2018; official site).

### Step 1: Warning signs
Personal signs that a crisis may be starting: thoughts ("I'm a burden"), moods (numb, agitated), situations (a certain
anniversary, a fight, a sleepless week), or behaviour (isolating, not eating). Written in your own words. These should be
specific enough that you'd recognise them. Two to four signs is typical.

### Step 2: Internal coping strategies
Things you can do alone, without contacting anyone, to take your mind off things or lower distress: a shower, a walk, a
familiar show, a breathing exercise, holding ice, a playlist, drawing, a specific game. The test is "would I actually do
this when I feel this way?" Ask what could get in the way and fix it (for example, keep the playlist downloaded).

### Step 3: People and social settings for distraction
Places and people where you can be around others without talking about the crisis: a café, library, a friend's place,
a bus ride, a community group, an online game with friends. The goal is distraction and connection, not disclosure.

### Step 4: People I can ask for help
Family or friends you can tell "I'm having a hard time" and ask for support. Names and numbers. Many people prefer
texting; Keeping Watch's page already leans that way. At least two names, so one busy person doesn't end the plan.

### Step 5: Professionals and agencies
Therapist, prescriber, doctor, local crisis line, 988, Crisis Text Line, the nearest emergency department address, and
for trans people, Trans Lifeline (peer support, limited hours). Include numbers and hours. This is where people who
prefer text can list text options.

### Step 6: Making the environment safe (lethal means)
Talking through how to keep yourself safer by putting time and distance between yourself and anything you could use
to hurt yourself during a crisis. Covered briefly in the lethal-means subsection below.

### Reason for living
The official form ends with a line for the one thing most important and worth living for. It can be a person, a pet, a
plan, a value or a small thing. Keeping Watch's version: "What matters to me, worth staying for".

### Lethal-means counselling (brief)
Means Matter (Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health) summarises why this matters: many attempts happen with little
planning during a short crisis; the method used strongly affects whether someone survives; and about 90% of people who
survive an attempt do not go on to die by suicide later. Counselling means a calm, practical conversation about
**temporarily** reducing access, especially to firearms and to large amounts of medicine, for example:
- storing firearms locked and unloaded, or temporarily with a trusted person or outside the home;
- keeping only small quantities of medicine at hand, locking the rest, or asking someone to hold it; disposing of old
  medicine through a pharmacy take-back;
- deciding in advance who could help with this during a hard stretch.
An observational study of over 1.1 million emergency department patients found lower odds of suicide death within 30
days (adjusted odds ratio 0.62) at hospitals that routinely provided lethal-means counselling (Marcus et al., 2025).
This doc deliberately gives no method details.

### Follow-up
In the 2018 VA study, staff called patients at least twice after discharge (starting within 72 hours) to check in, review
and revise the plan, and support getting to treatment. In a journal system, the equivalent is a scheduled review with
a trusted person or clinician, and a "last reviewed" date on the page.

## 6. Setting it up

**Ideally with someone.** The intervention is designed to be done with a clinician or a trained person. If that's not
possible, a trusted friend can help, or you can fill it in yourself on a calm day and show it to your clinician later.

**First setup (20–45 minutes, on a steady day).**
1. Think about the last hard time: what happened first, what you noticed, what helped even a little.
2. **Warning signs:** write 2–4 in your own words.
3. **On my own:** list 3–5 things. For each, ask "would I do this at 2 a.m. when I feel like that?" Keep only the yeses.
4. **People and places for distraction:** 2–3, with addresses or times if it helps (for example, the library's hours).
5. **People to ask for help:** 2–3 names with numbers. Tell them, if you can, that they're on your plan and what you'd
   like from them ("just text with me").
6. **Professionals and lines:** your clinicians' numbers and hours, a local crisis line, 988, Crisis Text Line, the
   nearest emergency department, Trans Lifeline if relevant.
7. **Making my space safer:** one or two concrete actions and who could help. Do what you can now, while calm.
8. **Worth living for:** one line.
9. Write a **text you can send** when talking is too hard.
10. Decide **where the plan lives**: the back of the journal, a photo on your phone, a wallet card, the X4.
11. Write a **review date**.

**First day after.** Put the plan where you'll see it. If you listed people, send the one text: "I've put you on my
support list. Is it OK if I text you when things are rough?"

**First week.** Look at it once. Is anything unrealistic? Fix it. If you used it, note which step helped.

## 7. Daily practice

A safety plan isn't a daily practice. It is used **when a warning sign shows up**:

1. Notice the warning sign (step 1). Say to yourself, "this is on my plan".
2. Try step 2 for a set time, for example 15–20 minutes.
3. Still hard? Go to step 3: a place or people.
4. Still hard? Step 4: text or call someone on the list. Use the script if talking is too hard.
5. Still hard, or feeling unsafe? Step 5: 988 (call or text), Crisis Text Line, your crisis line, your clinician.
6. At any point, step 6: put distance between yourself and means.
7. If you are in immediate danger: 911 or the nearest emergency department.

You don't have to go in order if a later step fits better right now. Skipping to step 5 is always fine.

**Light daily touchpoints (optional).** Some people pair the plan with a daily mood check (Keeping Watch has a mood and
anxiety check-in) so warning signs are noticed earlier. Keep this gentle: a daily check is for noticing, not scoring.

## 8. Rhythms

- **After using it:** jot what helped and what didn't; update the plan within a few days.
- **Weekly (optional, 2 minutes):** glance at it so it's familiar. If mood tracking shows a run of hard days, reread it.
- **Monthly:** Keeping Watch prints a new safety plan page in every book. Copy forward what still fits, or rewrite it.
  Check numbers and hours (crisis lines change: 988's LGBTQ+ option ended in July 2025).
- **With your clinician:** bring it to appointments; review after any crisis, medicine change, move, or relationship
  change.
- **Yearly:** a full rewrite. Keep old versions in the Keeper if you want a record of what helped.
- **Contacts:** when a number changes, update the plan, the phone and the X4 file (`/kw/me.txt`) together.

## 10. Worked examples

All names, numbers and details are invented and generic.

**A completed plan (Sam, made with their therapist).**

```
last reviewed: 12 Oct
1 Warning signs
  - not sleeping two nights in a row
  - thinking "everyone would be better off"
  - cancelling plans and staying in bed after work
2 On my own
  - hot shower, then the comfort playlist (downloaded)
  - walk the loop around the block with headphones
  - cold water on my face, slow breathing for 2 minutes
3 People and places for distraction
  - the library (open till 8), sit by the window
  - Dana's game night, Thursdays (just show up)
4 People I can ask for help
  - Dana 555-0101 (text ok)
  - my sister Lee 555-0102 (call after 6)
5 Professionals and lines
  - therapist Dr R., 555-0110, Mon–Thu
  - county crisis line 555-0199, 24/7
  - 988 call or text · text HOME to 741741
  - Trans Lifeline (877) 565-8860, weekdays 10–6 Pacific
  - nearest ER: Main St hospital
6 Making my space safer
  - Lee keeps the extra medicine; I keep a week's worth
  - old prescriptions go to the pharmacy take-back on Saturday
Worth living for: my cat, Pepper, and seeing the lake in spring.
Text script: "Hey, I'm having a hard time. I'm not up for a call. Can you text with me for a bit?"
```

**Using it on a hard evening (a note written afterward).**

```
Tue 20 Oct. Signs: second bad night, the "burden" thought. Tried shower + playlist (20 min), a bit better.
Went to the library for an hour. Still low, texted Dana the script; we texted till 10. Slept.
Helped most: texting. Didn't help: breathing (too restless). Change: swap breathing for the walk.
```

**A week around it.** Mood check-ins on the day pages show three low days in a row (Sat–Mon). On Monday's day page, the
"Was hard" column says "sign #1 again". Sam rereads the plan Monday night, texts Lee Tuesday, and brings the plan to
Thursday's therapy session, where they add a second name to step 4.

**A month.** At "Closing the month", Sam copies the plan into the new book's safety plan page, updates Lee's new number,
and writes "reviewed 1 Nov" at the top. The X4 file is updated from the Wi-Fi page the same evening, so both match.

## 12. Community practice

- **What people do.** In peer and patient spaces, people describe keeping a photo of the plan on their phone lock screen
  or in favourites, a paper copy by the bed, and a card in the wallet. Many say the most-used parts are the list of
  small things to do alone and the named people to text. **(General observation, not a study.)**
- **Customisations.** Adding a "text script"; splitting "people" into "just be with me" and "I can tell them"; adding
  sensory tools (weighted blanket, earplugs); a "comfort box" of objects; a list of reasons for living with photos.
- **Aesthetic vs minimalist.** Some decorate their plan to make it inviting; others want it stark and fast to read. In a
  crisis, high contrast, large type and short lines are easier. Keeping Watch's X4 Support screen follows that.
- **What people dislike.** Being handed a blank form in an emergency department without help; plans that list only "call
  988"; generic coping ideas that don't fit them.

## 13. Official products and formats

- **Official form:** free download (English), version dated 05-02-2024, plus authorised translations

- **Apps:** MY3 (free, iOS and Android). Others exist.
- **Keeping Watch note:** the book's page uses its own wording and does not claim to be the official Stanley–Brown form.
  Keep it that way unless permission is obtained.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- A pen that works reliably; a pencil for numbers that change.
- The plan on sturdy paper (the back-matter page is removable along the frame).
- A wallet card (laminated or in a card sleeve).
- Phone: a photo of the plan; contacts saved with clear names; 988 and 741741 saved.
- Optional: a small "comfort kit" listed in step 2 (headphones, a textured object, a card with a breathing pattern).

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps.** MY3 lets people set three key contacts, a step-by-step plan and resources; free, English and Spanish.
  Research on safety planning apps is growing (for example co-design studies in 2025) but outcomes evidence for apps
  alone is limited **(uncertain)**. Ferguson et al. (2022) found most studied plans were made in person with a hard copy.
- **Phones.** A lock-screen photo is quick. A phone in crisis can also be a source of distress (messages, social media).
- **E-ink.** A PDF of the plan on a reader works offline and is calm to read. The X4 shows the plan after a long press of
  Back, with no network and no notifications.
- **Scanning.** A scan of the plan in a private folder is a backup. Personal plans should not go to shared or public
  destinations.
- **Lost versus gained.** Digital gains quick dialling and backup; it loses calm (notifications) and sometimes privacy.
  Paper gains calm and reach without battery; it can be left at home. The best answer is both: a paper page and a copy
  on a device you always carry.

## 16. Evidence and research

| Study | Design and sample | Finding | Strengths and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stanley et al., 2009, *J Am Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry* | CBT-SP feasibility, 110 adolescents after an attempt | Safety plan delivered to 98%; 72.4% completed 12+ sessions | Feasibility only; no control group |
| Stanley and Brown, 2012, *Cogn Behav Pract* | Description of the intervention | Defines the steps and process | Not an outcome study |
| Bryan et al., 2017, *J Affect Disord* | RCT, 97 soldiers; crisis response plan vs contract for safety | Attempts at 6 months: about 5% vs 19%; hazard ratio 0.24 (76% reduction); faster drop in ideation | Small; military; related method (CRP), not SB |
| Stanley et al., 2018, *JAMA Psychiatry* | Cohort comparison, 1,640 veterans, 9 VA emergency departments | Suicidal behaviour 3.03% vs 5.29% over 6 months (OR 0.56, about 45% fewer); outpatient attendance OR 2.06 | Not randomised; 88% men; excluded admitted patients; plan plus phone follow-up |
| Nuij et al., 2021, *Br J Psychiatry* | Meta-analysis, 6 studies, 3,536 participants | Relative risk of suicidal behaviour 0.57 (95% CI 0.41–0.80); number needed to treat 16; no significant effect on ideation | Few studies; mixed designs |
| Ferguson et al., 2022, *Arch Suicide Res* | Systematic review, 26 studies | Improvements in suicidality, depression, hopelessness, hospitalisation, treatment attendance; feasible and acceptable | Heterogeneous studies |
| McMyler and Pryjmachuk, 2008, *J Psychiatr Ment Health Nurs* | Review | No quantitative evidence that no-suicide contracts work; ethical concerns | Narrative review |
| Marcus et al., 2025, *AJPM Focus* | Observational, 1,148,546 ED patients | Routine lethal-means counselling hospitals: lower odds of suicide death at 30 days (AOR 0.62) and 180 days (AOR 0.77) | Observational; hospital-level exposure |

**Claimed versus supported.**
- *Supported:* safety planning-type interventions reduce suicidal behaviour (meta-analysis), and safety planning with
  follow-up increases treatment engagement.
- *Not supported:* that a plan reduces suicidal thoughts on its own (no significant effect in the meta-analysis).
- *Not studied well:* self-made plans with no clinician, and journal-integrated plans like Keeping Watch's. They are
  reasonable and low risk, but the evidence is for the clinician-guided intervention **(uncertain)**.

## 17. Benefits

- Fewer suicidal behaviours when used as a clinical intervention (**evidenced**: Stanley 2018; Nuij 2021).
- More people attend follow-up treatment (**evidenced**: Stanley 2018).
- Better than no-suicide contracts (**evidenced**: Bryan 2017 for CRP; McMyler 2008 on contracts).
- Lower suicide death where lethal-means counselling is routine (**evidenced**, observational: Marcus 2025).
- A sense of control and a clear "what next" in a crisis (**claimed**, consistent with qualitative findings in Ferguson 2022).
- Easier to ask for help using a prepared text (**claimed**).
- Helps others know how to help (**claimed**).

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---|---|
| Filled in during a crisis, rushed | Make it on a steady day; revise after |
| Generic steps ("exercise", "call someone") | Name the exact activity, person, number, place |
| Plan can't be found when needed | Paper + phone photo + wallet card + X4 |
| Only one contact, who is unavailable | At least two people in step 4, plus 24/7 lines in step 5 |
| Old numbers and hours | Check monthly; Keeping Watch reprints a page each book |
| "Call 988" is the only step | Fill steps 2–4 so there are earlier options |
| Lethal means skipped because it's uncomfortable | Keep it short and practical; involve a trusted person |
| Used as a promise or contract | It's a tool; not a pledge |
| Fear that calling means police | Know each line's policy; Trans Lifeline won't call emergency services without your explicit request |
| Plan never reviewed | Write a review date; bring it to appointments |
| The paper and X4 copies differ | Update both together; add "last reviewed" to both |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** short lines; one action per line; keep the plan in more than one place; the X4 long press is fast.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** concrete steps; sensory tools in step 2 (earplugs, dim light, pressure); text-first options
  (988 text, Crisis Text Line) for when speaking is hard; a pre-written script.
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** step 2 items that need no energy (lie down with a familiar audiobook); contacts who
  can come to you.
- **Depression:** keep it very short; the reason-for-living line visible; don't wait for the "right" moment to use it.
- **Anxiety:** plan for panic-type warning signs too; grounding steps.
- **Trauma:** choose words that don't echo the trauma; list safe people and places carefully; note which services
  might involve police, since that can be distressing (see Trans Lifeline's no-nonconsensual-rescue policy).
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** voice memo of the plan; icons; someone else can write it with you.
- **Low vision:** large type (letter size page), high contrast; X4 Support screen uses big type; phone accessibility.
- **Motor or hand pain:** fewer lines; a typed plan on the X4 via the Wi-Fi page; speed-dial contacts.
- **Deaf and hard of hearing:** 988 has a Deaf and hard of hearing option; text lines.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** Trans Lifeline (trans peers; weekdays 10–6 Pacific; will not call emergency services
  or police without an explicit request); The Trevor Project for LGBTQ+ people under 25 (call 1-866-488-7386, text START to
  678-678, chat); note that 988's LGBTQ+ "press 3" ended on 17 July 2025, but 988 itself still serves everyone. Include
  affirming people and places in steps 3–4.
- **Shift workers:** list who is awake at 2 a.m. and who at 2 p.m.; step 3 places open at odd hours; 24/7 lines.
- **Low income:** all listed lines are free; the plan needs only paper; include free places (library, parks, community
  centres).

## 21. Combining

- **Symptom tracking** ([symptom-tracking.md](symptom-tracking.md)): mood, sleep and anxiety logs can show warning signs
  early (for example two short nights, a Keeping Watch step 1 item).
- **Bullet Journal** ([bullet-journal.md](bullet-journal.md)): keep the plan as a fixed collection at the front or back;
  index it.
- **Gender euphoria and HRT log** ([gender-euphoria-and-hrt-log.md](gender-euphoria-and-hrt-log.md)): moments of euphoria
  can feed the "worth living for" line.
- **Five-year diary** ([five-year-diary.md](five-year-diary.md)): anniversaries of hard times can be warning signs to
  anticipate.
- **Stoic journaling** ([stoic-journaling.md](stoic-journaling.md)): evening reflection is a calm time to review the plan,
  not a crisis tool.

## 23. Glossary

- **988:** the US Suicide & Crisis Lifeline; call, text or chat, 24/7, free.
- **Active rescue:** a crisis service contacting emergency services to intervene.
- **Barrier:** anything that might stop a step working; solved ahead of time.
- **Crisis Response Plan (CRP):** a card-based plan from Bryan and Rudd; similar to SB.
- **Crisis Text Line:** text HOME to 741741, trained volunteer crisis counsellors, 24/7.
- **Distraction:** shifting attention away from distress for a while.
- **Internal coping:** things you do alone to lower distress.
- **Lethal means:** objects or substances that could be used for self-harm.
- **Lethal-means counselling:** a brief conversation about temporarily reducing access to lethal means.
- **Means Matter:** Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health project on means reduction.
- **MY3:** free safety planning app built around three contacts.
- **No-suicide contract:** a signed promise not to self-harm; not evidence-based.
- **Reason for living:** what is most important and worth living for.
- **Safety Planning Intervention (SPI):** the full process, not just the form.
- **SPI+:** SPI plus follow-up phone contact (the 2018 VA model).
- **Trans Lifeline:** trans-led peer support line.
- **Warning signs:** personal cues that a crisis may be starting.
- **WRAP:** Wellness Recovery Action Plan, a broader self-management plan.
- **Zero Suicide:** a health-system framework that includes safety planning.

## 24. FAQ

**Do I need a therapist to make one?** It's designed to be made with someone, and that's best. If you can't, a trusted
person can help, or you can make it yourself on a calm day and share it with a clinician later.

**When should I use it?** When you notice a warning sign from step 1. You don't need to wait until things are very bad.

**Do I have to go in order?** No. The order moves from self to others to professionals, but go straight to step 5 or
911 any time you need to.

**Is it a promise not to hurt myself?** No. It's not a contract. It's a list of things to try.

**Will a crisis line call the police?** It depends on the service. Trans Lifeline states it will not call emergency
services or law enforcement without your explicit request. 988 and other lines may involve emergency services in rare
situations of imminent risk **(uncertain on current details; check each service's policy)**. Texting and asking
directly about their policy is fine.

**What's the evidence that it works?** A meta-analysis of six studies found about 43% lower relative risk of suicidal
behaviour with safety planning-type interventions. A large VA study found about 45% fewer suicidal behaviours with safety
planning plus follow-up calls. It didn't clearly reduce suicidal thoughts on their own.

**How often should I update it?** After you use it, after big changes, and at least every few months. Keeping Watch
prompts a fresh page each month.

**Where should I keep it?** Where you'll be when things get hard: the journal, a phone photo, a wallet card, the X4.

**What do I write for "making my space safer" if I don't want to write details?** Just the action and who helps, for
example "Lee keeps the extra medicine". No details are needed.

**Is 988 still for LGBTQ+ people?** Yes, 988 serves everyone. The specialised LGBTQ+ youth option ("press 3") ended on
17 July 2025. The Trevor Project and Trans Lifeline remain.

### e. Don't adopt

- **Copying the official Stanley–Brown form verbatim or using its name** on the page without written permission; keep
  Keeping Watch's own wording.
- **Risk scores, "danger levels" or colour-coded risk.** Journals shouldn't assess risk; that's clinical work.
- **Automatic alerts or messages to contacts** from the X4. Out of scope (nothing leaves the device; no notifications),
  and consent matters.
- **Generative AI "crisis chat".** Out of scope and unsafe.
- **Streaks or badges for "safe days".** Adds pressure and shame.
- **Method details in lethal-means text.** Keep it general and practical.
- **"No-suicide contract" or pledge language.** Not evidence-based.
- **Scan codes or cloud upload of the filled-in plan by default.** It's private; route scans only to a private place.

## 26. Open questions

- Does a self-made plan (no clinician) help as much as the clinician-guided intervention? Not well studied.
- Do journal-integrated plans reviewed monthly outperform a single plan? No data found.
- Current details of 988's imminent-risk policy were not confirmed for this doc.
- The exact year of the first VA safety plan manual (commonly cited as 2008) was not verified: the VA PDF link tried
  returned "not found".
- The date of Barbara Stanley's death is not given on the official site.
- Whether "warning sign" check-ins on the X4 help people notice early or make them dwell is unknown.
- Outcome evidence for safety planning apps (MY3 and others) alone remains limited.
