# Prompt journaling

Journaling that starts from a question someone else wrote: a prompt book, a card deck, a question-a-day diary, a
therapist's worksheet, or the weekly exchange prompt in a Keeping Watch book. This doc covers where prompts come from,
what the research says about wording, and how to rotate them so they stay fresh.

## 1. At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Creator** | No single creator. Structured prompts go back to Ira Progoff's Intensive Journal (1966) and James Pennebaker's expressive writing studies (1986). Commercial "question a day" books took off with *Q&A a Day* (Potter, about 2010). |
| **Year** | 1966 (Progoff), 1986 (Pennebaker), 2010s (prompt books and decks), 2020s (apps and social media prompt lists) |
| **Type** | Writing practice |
| **Time per day** | 1 minute (one-line answer) to 20 minutes (expressive writing) |
| **Time per week** | 5–60 minutes |
| **Cost** | Free (lists online) to about US$15–30 for books and card decks; workshops more |
| **Formats and sizes** | Books (often 5-year, one question per date), card decks, printed prompts on a page, apps, worksheets |
| **Best for** | Blank-page freeze; people who want a nudge but not a system; couples, friends and families (shared prompts); trying new angles |
| **Not ideal for** | People who find others' questions intrusive; anyone in crisis given a heavy prompt with no exit; people who want to write whatever is on their mind (see [morning-pages.md](morning-pages.md)) |
| **Learning curve** | None to answer a prompt; real skill to *write* good ones |
| **Supplies** | Pen, paper, a source of prompts |
| **Official site** | None for the general practice. Related: Intensive Journal (<https://intensivejournal.org/>), Julia Cameron (<https://juliacameronlive.com/>) |
| **Evidence level** | **Some studies.** Strong literature on *specific* prompts (expressive writing, best possible self, gratitude, positive affect journaling), with small average effects. Almost none on prompt books or decks as products. |

## 2. Summary

Prompt journaling hands you a question so you don't have to start from nothing. It ranges from a one-line
"What can you smell right now?" in a five-year diary to Pennebaker's 20-minute instruction to write your deepest
thoughts about an upsetting experience. The research is about specific prompts, and it shows the *wording* matters:
prompts that mix feelings with making sense ("what did you learn?") helped more than feelings alone (Ullrich &
Lutgendorf 2002); concrete "how" questions are less likely to feed rumination than abstract "why" questions (Watkins
2008); and a little distance ("you", or your own name) helps people handle hard feelings (Kross et al. 2014). Good
prompts are open, specific, short, optional and safe to skip. Rotation keeps them fresh: fixed by date, by theme day,
by deck, or by bringing old questions back on a schedule. For Keeping Watch, the 52 weekly exchange prompts and the
daily "On this day" line are already prompt systems; they can grow into a themed rotation with a "pass" option and
a printable deck.

## 3. History and origin

### Who created it

Prompts are as old as teaching. For journaling, five strands matter:

- **Spiritual exercises.** Ignatius of Loyola's daily examen (1500s) is a fixed set of reflective questions.
  Quaker "queries" are questions read aloud for self-examination.
- **Ira Progoff** (1921–1998), a depth psychologist influenced by Carl Jung, created the Intensive Journal method in
  1966: a workbook of sections with structured exercises (for example, lists of "steppingstones" and written
  dialogues with people, work and the body). The Intensive Journal site describes it as "not journal keeping" but "an
  integrated system of writing exercises". See [progoff-intensive-journal.md](progoff-intensive-journal.md).
- **James W. Pennebaker** (University of Texas at Austin) ran the first expressive writing trial with Sandra Beall in
  1986: students wrote about a traumatic event for about 15 minutes on 4 days, and had fewer health-centre visits
  afterwards. It is the most-studied journaling prompt in psychology.
- **Creative writing teachers.** Natalie Goldberg's *Writing Down the Bones* (1986) popularised timed writing
  "practice" from a short prompt. Julia Cameron's *The Artist's Way* (1992) paired open morning pages with weekly
  tasks and "writing for guidance" (asking a question and writing the answer).
- **Gift publishing.** *Q&A a Day* (Potter Style, a Penguin Random House imprint; first edition about 2010) put one
  question at the top of each date page with five years of answer slots. Dozens of spin-offs followed (for moms, for
  couples, "for the soul"). Card decks and apps followed.

### Cultural context

Prompts moved from therapy rooms and workshops into gift shops. The 2010s gift books made prompts light and
nostalgic (a time capsule); the 2020s social feeds made them heavy and therapeutic ("shadow work", "inner child"),
often without the safety rails therapists use.

### Key quotes (short)

- Progoff (via the Intensive Journal site): the method is "not journal keeping, not introspective diary keeping, and it
  isn't writing therapy."
- Catron on the 36 questions, as reported by Sara Eckel: the experiment "wasn't scientific".
- Frattaroli (2006): experimental disclosure "is effective", with an average r-effect size of ".075", which is small.

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- **A question lowers the cost of starting.** Blank-page freeze is the most common reason people stop journaling.
- **The question shapes the answer.** "Why did this happen to me?" and "What helped, even a bit?" lead to different
  places. Prompt writers carry responsibility.
- **Open prompts invite; closed prompts record.** "How did the shift go?" opens; "Did you take meds?" records. Both
  have a place; check-ins are closed prompts.
- **Choice and safety come first** (trauma-informed practice): the writer can skip, change the question, or stop.
- **Freshness needs rotation.** The same prompt every day turns into a form.
- **Repeating on purpose** (same question each year) turns prompts into a mirror.

**Problem it solves:** "I don't know what to write" and "I only ever write about the same thing".

| Element | What it is |
|---|---|
| The prompt | The question or instruction |
| The frame | How long, how much space, what's allowed (lists, drawing, "pass") |
| The source | Book, deck, list, therapist, partner, the journal itself |
| The rotation | How the next prompt is chosen |
| The answer space | Lines, box, dots |
| The exit | Explicit permission to skip or swap |
| The return | When and how you revisit answers |

### The prompt
Types:

| Type | Example | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Closed | "Did you go outside?" | Tracking |
| Scaled | "Energy 1–5?" | Tracking |
| Open, light | "What can you smell right now?" | Warm-up, fun |
| Open, reflective | "What drained your spoons, and what refilled them?" | Weekly review |
| Open, deep | "Write about something you've never told anyone." | Only with safety rails |
| Sentence stem | "Today I noticed…" | Low effort, dyslexia-friendly |
| List | "Three sounds you liked." | Low spoons |
| Creative | "Draw your week as weather." | Non-verbal |
| Relational | "Ask me one question." | Exchange journals |
| Future | "What would make next week gentler?" | Planning |
| Distanced | "What would you tell a friend in this spot?" | Hard feelings |

### The frame
Pennebaker's frame (time, privacy, "don't worry about spelling") is part of why it works. A 1-line space says "keep it
short"; a full page says "go deep".

### The source
Therapists write prompts for a person; books write them for everyone. The narrower the audience, the better the fit.

### The rotation
See section 8 and the rotation table in section 11.

### The answer space
Match the space to the prompt weight. Heavy prompt, small box = frustration.

### The exit
A printed "pass" or "swap" option. Trauma-informed wording (section 19) treats it as essential.

### The return
Five-year books build it in (same date, same question). Otherwise a quarterly reread.

## 6. Setting it up

1. **Choose a job for the prompts.** Warm-up? Reflection? Connection with someone? Don't mix all three in one slot.
2. **Choose the source.** A book, a deck, or your own list of 20–50 prompts (section 22).
3. **Choose the rhythm.** Daily 1-line, or weekly longer. Weekly deep prompts are gentler than daily ones.
4. **Choose the rotation.** Fixed by date (no decisions), by weekday theme (predictable), or shuffle (novelty).
5. **Add an exit.** Write "pass is fine" at the top of your list.
6. **Mark heavy prompts** with a symbol so you can pick them on a good day only.

**First day:** one light prompt, 3–5 lines. **First week:** 3–5 light prompts, one reflective prompt at the weekly
review. At the end of the week, cross out prompts that didn't land.

## 7. Daily practice

1. **Read the prompt** (10 s). If it's wrong for today, swap or pass.
2. **Check the weight** (5 s). Heavy prompt on a low day? Swap.
3. **Answer plainly** (1–5 min). A list is fine. A drawing is fine.
4. **Optional: one line on how it felt** to answer.
5. **Stop at the space.** Overflow goes to the writing space, not a new obligation.

For expressive writing (a special case): 15–20 minutes, 3–4 days in a row, about one upsetting topic, stop if it gets
too much. See the expressive writing doc when it lands; the GGSC practice page summarises the protocol.

## 8. Rhythms

- **Daily:** one light prompt, or none.
- **Weekly:** one reflective prompt (the weekly review) and one relational prompt (the exchange spread).
- **Monthly:** a theme (the month theme page) that colours the prompts.
- **Quarterly:** repeat a small set of "mirror" questions ("What are you proud of?", "What would you drop?") and
  compare with last quarter.
- **Yearly:** the same 10 questions each New Year or birthday, answered fresh; compare. Retire prompts that never
  land; add 10 new.
- **Archiving:** keep a prompt log (which prompt, which date) so you don't repeat by accident.

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Who | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| Intensive Journal | Ira Progoff, 1966 | Sectioned workbook, structured exercises, workshops |
| Expressive writing | Pennebaker & Beall, 1986 | One heavy prompt, 15–20 min, 3–4 days |
| Writing practice | Natalie Goldberg, 1986 | Timed writing from a short prompt, "keep your hand moving" |
| Writing for guidance | Julia Cameron, *The Artist's Way* | Ask a question, write the answer |
| Best possible self | King 2001; Sheldon & Lyubomirsky 2006 | Write about your future self where things went well |
| Positive affect journaling | Smyth et al. 2018 | Prompts on positive events, gratitude, strengths, 12 weeks online |
| Question-a-day 5-year | *Q&A a Day*, Potter | Same question on the same date each year |
| 36 questions | Aron et al. 1997 | Escalating questions for two people |
| Prompt card decks | Many (conversation and journaling decks) | Draw a card; shuffle rotation |
| Worksheets | CBT, DBT therapists | Fixed fields (thought record, chain analysis) |
| Monthly prompt challenges | Social media (e.g., 30-day lists) | One prompt a day for a month; public |
| "Shadow work" prompt lists | Social media, loosely from Jung | Heavy prompts, often no safety rails |
| Exchange journals | Friends, couples, parents and teens | Shared book, alternating answers |

### Rotation systems

| System | How | Good | Bad |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed by date | One prompt per date (Q&A a Day) | No decisions; yearly mirror | Wrong prompt on a bad day |
| Fixed by week | One prompt per week (Keeping Watch exchange) | Calm pace; no repeats | Same issue |
| Weekday themes | Mon body, Tue outside… | Predictable; habit-friendly | Can feel rigid |
| Shuffle deck | Draw at random | Novelty | Heavy card on a low day |
| Weighted deck | Separate light and deep piles | Choose by capacity | Needs sorting |
| Spaced return | Answered prompts return after 1 month, 3 months, 1 year (like Leitner flashcard boxes) | Mirror effect; fewer prompts needed | Needs a log |
| Seasonal | Prompts tied to season or moon | Fits Keeping Watch's sky pages | Some find it forced |
| Choose-from-3 | Three offered, pick one | Choice (trauma-informed) | Takes space |

## 12. Community practice

- **What people do:** Reddit's r/Journaling regularly asks for prompts; the most-upvoted advice is to keep a personal
  list and to use prompts only on stuck days. Five-year Q&A books are popular gifts and often abandoned after a few
  months, then restarted on the same page the next year.
- **Social feeds:** "journal prompts for healing" and "shadow work prompts" lists spread on TikTok and Pinterest. Many
  are heavy (childhood, trauma) with no guidance. Therapists on the same platforms warn about this.
- **Customisations:** colour-coded prompt jars; prompt of the week on a sticky note; couples swapping prompts; teachers
  using a weekly prompt box.
- **Aesthetic camp:** decorated prompt pages with lettered questions.
- **Minimalist camp:** a plain list in the back of the notebook; tick when used.

## 13. Official products and formats

No official product for the method. Common products:

- **Five-year question books** (*Q&A a Day* and spin-offs): hardcover, about 368 pages, one date per page, five slots.
  About US$15–20.
- **Guided journals** on every topic: 100–200 pages, a prompt per page.
- **Card decks**: 50–150 cards, US$15–30.
- **Therapy worksheets**: free or from therapists.
- **Apps**: prompt-of-the-day, often subscription.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- Pen; a small notebook or index cards for your own list.
- Index cards (3×5 in) make a home-made deck; a rubber band splits light and deep piles.
- A pencil tick in the list's margin logs use.
- No stickers or stamps needed; a small icon per theme helps.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps:** Day One (prompts built in), Stoic, Reflectly-style apps; many use AI to generate prompts, which Keeping
  Watch's firmware scope rules out.
- **E-ink:** reMarkable or Boox templates with a prompt header; Kindle Scribe notebooks. A static prompt PDF works
  well; X4 can show a short fixed list.
- **Scanning:** a prompt page with a printed question and a scan zone maps cleanly; the question text is known from the
  build, so only the answer needs OCR.
- **Lost vs paper:** apps rotate for you and search answers, but tend to push daily reminders and AI. Paper keeps
  prompts private and fixed; the rotation has to be printed or logged.

## 16. Evidence and research

### Key studies

| Study | Sample | Prompt | Finding | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pennebaker & Beall 1986, *J Abnorm Psychol* | Undergraduates | Write about a traumatic event (facts, feelings, or both) vs trivial topics, 4 days | Facts + feelings group had fewer health-centre visits later but more short-term upset | Small; students |
| Pennebaker 1997, *Psychol Sci* | Review | Expressive writing | Health gains; language change (more cause and insight words) predicts benefit | Review |
| Frattaroli 2006, *Psychol Bull* | 146 randomized studies | Experimental disclosure | Average r = .075 (small); several moderators | Heterogeneous |
| Baikie & Wilhelm 2005, *Adv Psychiatr Treat* | Review | 15–20 min, 3–5 sessions | Benefits in clinical and non-clinical groups; suggests use in trauma care | Narrative review |
| Ullrich & Lutgendorf 2002, *Ann Behav Med* | 122 students, 1 month | Emotions only vs cognitions + emotions vs facts about media | Cognitions + emotions found more benefit in the event; emotions-only reported more illness symptoms | Students; self-report |
| Sheldon & Lyubomirsky 2006, *J Posit Psychol* | 67 undergraduates, 4 weeks | Best possible self vs gratitude vs day details | BPS best for mood; motivation predicted doing it | Small |
| Smyth et al. 2018, *JMIR Ment Health* | Medical patients with elevated anxiety | 12 weeks online positive affect journaling | Less distress, better well-being vs usual care (preliminary RCT) | Preliminary |
| Kross et al. 2014, *JPSP* | Several lab studies | Self-talk in first vs non-first person ("you", own name) | Distanced self-talk improved handling of social stress | Lab tasks, not journals |
| Watkins 2008, *Psychol Bull* | Review | Repetitive thought | Abstract, evaluative "why" thinking tends to be unhelpful; concrete, specific "how" thinking tends to be helpful | Review |
| Aron et al. 1997, *PSPB* | Student pairs | 36 escalating questions, 45 min | More closeness than small talk | Lab setting; closeness, not love |

### What this means for prompt wording

1. **Mix feeling with meaning.** "What did you feel, and what do you make of it now?" beats "Pour out your feelings".
2. **Prefer concrete "how" and "what" to abstract "why".** "What happened just before?" over "Why am I like this?".
3. **Offer distance.** "What would you tell a friend?" or "You got through it; how?".
4. **Escalate slowly.** Aron's sets go light to deep; so should a deck.
5. **Expect short-term upset** from heavy prompts; give an exit and a grounding line.
6. **Match the prompt to the goal.** Best possible self and gratitude lift mood; expressive writing processes events.

### Claimed vs supported

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Prompts help people start writing | **Claimed**, widely reported; not studied as such |
| Expressive writing prompts improve health | **Supported, small** (r ≈ .075) |
| Prompts mixing thinking and feeling beat feelings alone | **Some evidence** (one RCT) |
| Positive prompts (BPS, gratitude, PAJ) lift mood | **Supported, small** |
| Question-a-day books improve well-being | **Not studied** |
| "Shadow work" prompts heal trauma | **Not supported**; risk without support |
| AI-generated prompts are better | **Not studied**; out of scope anyway |

## 17. Benefits

- Beats blank-page freeze. **Claimed.**
- Specific prompts give small mood and health gains. **Evidenced.**
- Varied prompts widen what you notice. **Claimed.**
- Repeated prompts show change over time. **Claimed.**
- Shared prompts build closeness. **Evidenced** (Aron 1997, in the lab).
- Structured therapy prompts support treatment. **Evidenced** as part of therapy.

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---|---|
| Heavy prompt on a low day | Weight labels; a "pass" bubble; swap from the light pile |
| Prompts feel generic or twee | Write your own list; delete what doesn't land |
| Same answers every time | Rotate themes; add specificity ("today", "this week") |
| Rumination ("why am I like this") | Rewrite as "what" or "how"; add "what helped, even a bit" |
| Pressure to finish a 365-prompt book | Undated pages; skip freely |
| Shared prompt feels intrusive | Relational prompts should be answerable lightly; allow "ask me something else" |
| Social-media prompt lists re-open trauma | Keep trauma work with support; use grounding prompts instead |
| Prompts crowd out your own voice | Leave a free writing space beside the prompt |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** novelty helps; use shuffle or choose-from-3. Keep answers short. A deck is easier than a book.
- **Autism:** concrete, literal prompts ("What did you eat?") over vague ones ("How was your vibe?"). Predictable
  weekday themes can help. State expected length.
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** list and circle prompts; one-word answers count; "pass" always visible.
- **Depression:** avoid "why" and "best life" prompts on bad days; use "what helped even a bit", "one okay thing".
- **Anxiety:** avoid open-ended future prompts on high-anxiety days; use present-sense prompts (sound, smell).
- **Trauma:** follow the SAMHSA principles: safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration and
  mutuality; empowerment, voice and choice; cultural, historical and gender issues. In practice: say what a prompt is
  for, offer choice, never require disclosure, avoid "What's the worst…", pair heavy prompts with grounding, and keep
  trauma processing with a therapist.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** sentence stems, word banks to circle, drawing options, short prompts in plain words.
- **Low vision:** large print prompt; one per page on 8.5×11.
- **Motor or hand pain:** circle/tick prompts; voice notes.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** avoid prompts that assume a past gender or family ("as a little girl…");
  offer euphoria prompts ("a moment I felt like me"); make disclosure optional in exchange prompts.
- **Shift workers:** "today" and "tonight" confuse; use "this shift", "since you woke".
- **Low income:** free lists; avoid prompts that assume travel, shopping or money.

## 23. Glossary

- **Prompt:** a question or instruction that starts a piece of writing.
- **Open question:** can't be answered yes/no; invites description.
- **Closed question:** yes/no or a number; used for tracking.
- **Sentence stem:** the start of a sentence to finish.
- **Expressive writing:** Pennebaker's protocol of writing deepest thoughts and feelings about a stressful event.
- **Experimental disclosure:** research term for expressive writing and similar tasks.
- **Best possible self (BPS):** writing about a future where things went well.
- **Positive affect journaling (PAJ):** prompts focused on positive experiences.
- **Distanced self-talk:** addressing yourself as "you" or by name.
- **Rumination:** repetitive, abstract, evaluative thinking about distress.
- **Rotation:** how the next prompt is chosen.
- **Leitner boxes:** a flashcard method where items return at growing intervals; borrowed here for prompts.
- **Mirror question:** a prompt repeated on purpose to compare answers over time.
- **Exchange journal:** a shared notebook passed between people.
- **Trauma-informed:** designed to avoid re-traumatising; built on safety, choice and transparency.
- **Grounding:** a present-moment task (senses, breath) that steadies after hard material.

## 24. FAQ

**Are prompts "cheating"?** No. Most researched journaling uses prompts.

**How many prompts do I need?** About 50 for a weekly year; 20–30 if you let them return.

**Daily or weekly?** Light prompts daily if you like; reflective prompts weekly.

**What if a prompt upsets me?** Stop, ground yourself (name five things you can see), and pass. If it keeps happening,
talk to someone; heavy material is better with support.

**Should I answer honestly in a shared journal?** Answer what you're comfortable sharing. "Ask me something else" is a
valid answer.

**Are "shadow work" prompts safe?** Some are fine; many are heavy with no support. Treat trauma prompts as therapy
material.

**Why "how" and not "why"?** Abstract "why" questions tend to feed rumination; concrete "how" and "what" questions
tend to help problem-solving (Watkins 2008).

**Can I repeat prompts?** Yes. Repeating on purpose shows change. Log the date so you can compare.

**Do prompt books work?** They aren't studied as products. The prompts inside can use the same principles as studied
ones.

## 25. For Keeping Watch

Keeping Watch already has: **52 weekly exchange prompts** (`content/year.mjs`, one per week, no repeats, printed on
the exchange spread with a Reply page), the **weekly review** (fixed reflective prompts), **On this day** (one line of
history per day, a light prompt by another name), **month moon prompts** (astrology as a reflection prompt), **Fill-in
blanks**, **Words to circle**, and presets that are prompts ("A moment I felt like me", "Tomorrow's first step").
What's missing is theme and weight labels, a pass option, a way to return prompts, and user-editable prompt text.

### How the weekly exchange prompts could evolve

- **Tag each prompt** with a theme (people, outside, body, play, learn, hope) and weight (light, reflective). Print the
  theme as an icon next to "This week's prompt".
- **Alternate weights**: never two reflective prompts in a row; heavy prompts avoid known hard weeks (holidays).
- **Add a pass line** under the prompt: "or: ask me something else".
- **Return prompts**: a few prompts come back a year later marked with a small return icon, for a mirror effect.
- **Let Shelbee edit the list** in the editor (a text list option), keeping the default list.

### How On this day could evolve

- Keep it a light, factual line (history). Optionally offer a **prompt mode**: the fact plus a short open question
  ("What's something you've invented, even small?"). Fit: medium; it adds words.
- **Not** "one year ago you wrote…" in print: the book is built before you write, and scans are private.

### d. Proposed method layout: "Prompt journal"

1. `sky`
2. `care`
3. `prompt`: source list, n 3, pass on
4. `words`: Feeling
5. `review`: went well, was hard
6. `body`: lines

### e. Don't adopt

- **AI-generated or "personalised" prompts.** Firmware scope forbids AI; printed books don't need it.
- **Heavy trauma prompts in the default list.** Keep those for therapy.
- **365-day "must answer" books.** Guilt when skipped.
- **Prompt streaks or "prompts answered" badges.** Out of scope and pressuring.
- **"One year ago you wrote" on the device sleep screen.** Surfaces private text unasked; can hurt on a bad night.
- **Social "prompt challenges".** Public by nature; privacy rules.

## 26. Open questions

- No studies compare prompt books, decks and free journaling head to head.
- Best rotation (date, theme, shuffle, spaced return) is untested; recommendations here are design reasoning.
- Whether printed pass options reduce dropout: plausible, not studied.
- Effects of relational prompts in exchange journals outside the lab.
- How trauma-informed wording changes outcomes in self-help journals (the principles come from services, not
  journals).
