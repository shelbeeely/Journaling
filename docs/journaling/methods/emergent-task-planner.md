# Emergent Task Planner (ETP)

David Seah's one-page daily planning sheet: a time column, three important tasks with 15-minute bubbles, and a notes area for whatever the day throws at you.

## 2. Summary

The Emergent Task Planner is a single printed page for one day. Down the left side is a day grid where you write the hours you're awake or working. At the top right you name three important tasks, estimate each in 15-minute bubbles, and later fill the bubbles as you actually work. The rest of the page catches interruptions, extra to-dos and notes. Seah built it in 2006 after his more elaborate "Menu of the Day" form proved too heavy for real days. Its promise is modest: "Remembering What's Important as Stuff Happens." It doesn't ask you to plan every minute. It asks you to see how much time you really have, pick a few things that matter, notice what gets in the way, and learn from the gap between what you guessed and what happened. That makes it a gentle cousin of time blocking (see [time-block-planner.md](time-block-planner.md)) that fits days you don't fully control.

### Cultural context

The ETP came out of the mid-2000s productivity blog wave: Getting Things Done was everywhere, Lifehacker and 43 Folders were popular, and "hipster PDA" index cards and printable forms were a hobby. Seah's forms stood out for their design: clean grids, bubbles you fill like a test sheet, and a focus on how the form *feels* on a bad day. Bloggers such as Shawn Blanc (2014) wrote that the ETP matched the "Big Three" plan they already drew by hand.

### Why "emergent"

The word comes from the Emergent Task Timer. Seah wrote that he had "no idea how long these tasks were really going to take," so he'd "just do the tasks and see what patterns emerge from the doing." The planner keeps that spirit: you plan lightly, then let the real shape of the day show itself on the page.

### Key quotes

- Subtitle: "Remembering What's Important as Stuff Happens."
- "Its main purpose is to keep you grounded about your time and tasks."
- "Remember, it's a challenge to get even *one* thing done on purpose when working with other people!"
- On the 2017 bubble change: "focused work doesn't work well for me past about 90 minutes without a break."
- The 2017 redesign stresses that the form is "suggestive, not prescriptive."

## 4. Philosophy and principles

Seah names three principles on the ETP page:

1. **Focus.** A short list of important tasks makes it more likely you'll finish them.
2. **Assessment.** Estimating and then tracking time teaches you where your time goes, so you allocate it better next time.
3. **Time visualization.** A day grid shows how many hours are actually left, so you can test whether the plan is realistic.

Other beliefs, from his posts:

- **A form must match your mindset on a bad day.** The Menu of the Day failed because it "mixed planning with production" and asked for too much thinking while working. A daily form should need almost none.
- **Plans are suggestions.** The day will change. The sheet should hold what matters steady while letting everything else move.
- **Effort counts, not only completion.** The Task Progress Tracker calls itself an "I did list, not a tyrannical to-do list." The ETP's bubbles carry that idea: filled bubbles show work done even when the task isn't finished.
- **Other people make time unpredictable.** The notes area exists because interruptions are normal, not failure.

**The problem it solves:** a to-do list has no sense of time, and a full calendar has no room for chaos. The ETP puts a short list next to a picture of the day, so you can see the collision coming and choose.

### 5.1 Day grid

You write your own hours down the left edge ("hours of the day"). That matters: the grid fits a 6 a.m. start or a 2 p.m. shift equally. Then you block out time for tasks, usually by writing the task number in a circle on the grid and drawing a bracket or bar down the rows it covers. Seah's 2006 example overlaps two tasks at noon (a server backup running while he takes a plumber's call) to show "practical multitasking" of waiting time. The 2017 version widened the grid and replaced printed 15-minute boxes with small tick markers so it's easier to write in.

### 5.2 Three important tasks

The heart of the page. Originally "THREE MAJOR TASKS," reworded in 2017 to "THREE IMPORTANT TASKS." The 2006 form had nine numbered lines; later versions put three at the top, a "chore block" for 4–6, and more numbered rows in the notes area. The number three matches the popular "Most Important Tasks" (MITs) idea. Seah's own caution: getting even one done on purpose is hard when you work with other people.

### 5.3 Bubbles: estimate versus actual

Each bubble is 15 minutes. From 2017 each task row has 2 hours of bubbles (8); earlier versions had 4 hours (16).

How people use them (Seah's page plus user write-ups):

1. **Estimate.** Before starting, mark your guess. Common ways: draw a vertical line or slash after the last bubble you expect to need, or outline those bubbles lightly.
2. **Track.** Each time you finish 15 minutes on that task, fill one bubble. A kitchen timer or interval timer helps (this is the Emergent Task Timer habit).
3. **Compare.** At the end of the day you see at a glance whether the filled bubbles stop before, at, or past your line.
4. **Learn.** Over a week, patterns show up: "email always takes double," "writing tasks run 1.5× my guess."

A half-filled bubble can mean "about 7 minutes." Some people slash a bubble to show split attention, a trick from the Emergent Task Timer. Some users skip bubbles entirely; one coach wrote that she never used them because she isn't detail-oriented, and still found the sheet helpful.

### 5.4 Notes and interruptions

Called "What Else is Going On Today?" in the 2010 version (it had a dot pattern that divided users). In 2017 the rows were halved and the area split into four horizontal sections for overflow to-dos and notes. You write interruptions as they happen, with a time if you like. This is where "emergent" tasks land before you decide whether they bump anything.

### 5.5 End-of-day review

Step 5 of Seah's instructions: review what happened, then prioritize the remaining items for tomorrow. No printed box. Most users copy unfinished tasks onto the next sheet.

## 6. Setting it up

### First setup (15 minutes)

1. Download the free PDF (Letter or A4, B&W saves ink) or buy the Mini ETP if you want half-letter.
2. Print a week's worth (5–7 sheets). Use a laser printer if you can; Seah notes built-in PDF viewers in some browsers may drop the dotted lines, so print from a full PDF reader.
3. Decide where the sheet lives: clipboard, folder, desk mat. It has to stay in sight. One reviewer quit partly because she left the sheet on another floor.
4. Decide your hour range. Write it once on a sticky note so you don't think about it each morning.
5. Get a timer (phone, kitchen timer). Optional but it makes the bubbles honest.

### First day

1. The night before or first thing: write the date and your hours.
2. Write up to three important tasks. If you're unsure, pick one.
3. Mark an estimate on each task's bubble row.
4. Block them on the grid around fixed appointments.
5. Work. Fill a bubble per 15 minutes. Write interruptions in notes.
6. End of day: look at bubbles against estimates. Circle what's unfinished. Move it to tomorrow's sheet.

### First week

- Days 1–2: use only tasks and notes. Ignore the grid if it feels like too much.
- Days 3–4: add the grid.
- Day 5: add bubbles and estimates.
- End of week: lay the five sheets side by side. Count how many important tasks got done, how often estimates were short, and what kind of interruption showed up most.

## 7. Daily practice

| When | Step | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Evening before or morning | Date, hours, three tasks, estimates | 3–5 min |
| Morning | Block tasks on the grid around fixed events | 2–3 min |
| All day | Fill bubbles every 15 min on a task; log interruptions | seconds each |
| When the plan breaks | Look at the grid, redraw the rest of the day, drop or move a task | 1–2 min |
| End of day | Compare bubbles to estimates; circle leftovers; carry forward | 3–5 min |

**Order matters:** fixed things first (appointments, shift, bus), then important tasks into the biggest gaps, then chores into leftovers.

**Prompts to ask while planning:**
- What would make today feel like a good day if only one thing got done?
- How many hours are really free once meetings, travel and meals are out?
- Which task always takes longer than I think?

**Prompts at review:**
- Where did the time go that I didn't plan?
- Which estimate was furthest off, and why?
- What's the first step on tomorrow's top task?

## 8. Rhythms

- **Weekly:** Not built in. Many users spread the week's sheets out on Friday and note estimate accuracy (e.g., "planned 12 bubbles, did 18"). Seah's Concrete Goals Tracker is his weekly companion: points for concrete results, one bubble chart per week.
- **Monthly:** Optional. Tally important tasks done per week; note recurring interruptions to fix at the source.
- **Quarterly:** Use the Compact Calendar (a whole year on one sheet) to plan milestones; the ETP handles days.
- **Yearly:** The ETP Journal gives 365 pages in one PDF. Archive sheets in a binder by month if you want them; most people bin them.
- **Migration:** Daily. Unfinished tasks are rewritten on tomorrow's sheet, which is a natural filter: if you don't want to rewrite it, drop it.

## 12. Community practice

- **What people actually do:** most users treat it as a Big Three sheet with a timeline. The bubbles are the most loved and the most skipped feature. Bloggers describe planning the night before, keeping the sheet on the desk, and carrying tasks forward.
- **Common customisations:** hand-drawing it in a dot-grid notebook; using only the tasks and notes; using the grid as a time log (what happened) rather than a plan; colouring bubbles by type (deep work versus admin); adding a "tomorrow" line at the bottom; stapling a week together.
- **Why some quit:** one 2013 reviewer found that committing to tasks in writing made her rebel against them, and that the listed order felt restrictive when she worked in a different order. She moved to an app (Skedpal) that schedules for her.
- **Aesthetic versus minimalist:** the ETP sits firmly in the minimalist, functional camp. People who decorate usually do so with a colour for each task number. Printable-planner communities share restyled versions.
- **Digital crossover:** people use the form as a template in note apps, PDFs on tablets, or a timer app for the bubbles.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Paper:** ordinary 20–24 lb (75–90 gsm) copy paper is fine. For ink pens, 24 lb/90 gsm cuts show-through if you print double-sided.
- **Pen:** a fine-liner (0.3–0.5 mm) or a pencil. Pencil suits estimates you might erase.
- **Timer:** kitchen timer, watch, or phone interval timer for 15-minute bubble fills.
- **Clipboard or folder:** keeps it visible, which matters more than anything else.
- **Highlighter:** optional, to mark the three important tasks on the grid.
- **Ruler:** only if hand-drawing the form.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps and templates:** PDF on a tablet with a stylus (GoodNotes, Notability, Xodo); a template in a note app; the 30/30 timer app approach; any calendar plus a "top 3" note. Seah has published an online Emergent Task Timer (an early Flash version) in the past.
- **E-ink:** the PDF works well on reMarkable, Kindle Scribe and Boox as a daily template. Bubbles are easy to fill with a stylus. Dotted lines may render faintly; use the B&W or weight-adjusted file.
- **Scanning:** one page per day scans cleanly. Bubbles are machine-readable in principle (like a test sheet) if the scan is aligned.
- **What's lost with digital:** the sheet sitting in view all day; the tactile bubble fill; the end-of-day "whole page" glance.
- **What's gained:** automatic totals of estimate versus actual; no printing; history you can search.

## 16. Evidence and research

No study tests the ETP itself. Its parts connect to research:

| Study | Sample | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994), *J. Personality and Social Psychology* 67(3) | Study 1: 37 honours thesis students, plus further studies | Students predicted they'd finish in 33.9 days on average; they took 55.5. People plan from best-case scenarios and ignore past experience. | Classic, replicated. Lab and student samples. Supports why the ETP's estimate-then-track loop helps: it forces you to look at your past record. |
| Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38 | 94 independent tests | "If-then" plans that tie actions to times or situations improve goal attainment, d = .65. | Strong meta-analysis. The ETP's grid (task 1 at 9:00) is a light implementation intention, but the ETP doesn't test this directly. |
| Aeon, Faber & Panaccio (2021), *PLOS ONE* | 158 studies, 53,957 people | Time management relates moderately to job performance (r ≈ .25) and more to wellbeing; negatively to distress (r ≈ −.36). | Mostly cross-sectional, so no causation. Not specific to any tool. |
| Halder et al. (2026), arXiv preprint | 957 app users, 24,265 tasks, six weeks | About a quarter of tasks went straight from created to done; a third were abandoned; over three quarters of time-specific tasks were done after their scheduled time. | Preprint, one app, digital. Shows how normal it is for plans to slip, which is exactly what the ETP expects. |

**Claimed versus supported**

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Visualising the day shows whether a plan is realistic | Plausible; supported indirectly by planning-fallacy research |
| Tracking actual time improves future estimates | Plausible; the planning-fallacy work suggests people ignore their past, so a record helps. No ETP-specific trial. |
| A short list increases completion | Anecdotal; consistent with implementation-intention research |
| Logging interruptions reduces stress | Anecdotal |

## 17. Benefits

- Makes the day's real capacity visible. **(claimed)**
- Improves time estimates over weeks by comparing guess and actual. **(claimed; indirectly supported by planning-fallacy research)**
- Keeps a few priorities in view during chaos. **(claimed)**
- Specific when/what plans improve follow-through. **(evidenced for implementation intentions in general)**
- Counts effort, not only finished tasks, which is kinder on hard days. **(claimed)**
- Gives interruptions a place, so they don't vanish from memory. **(claimed)**
- Time-management habits in general go with better wellbeing and less distress. **(evidenced, correlational)**
- Free, printable, quick. **(fact)**

## 18. Pitfalls

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Sheet out of sight, forgotten | Clipboard on the desk or tucked in the front of the journal. Same spot every day. |
| Bubbles feel fiddly | Skip them for a week. Or fill only at task end ("that was about 3 bubbles"). |
| Listing three tasks triggers rebellion | Write them as options ("could do") or list one. Order doesn't matter; the numbers are labels, not a queue. |
| Estimates always wrong, feels like failure | That's the data. Note the ratio ("×1.5") and apply it next time. |
| Interruptions fill the notes, nothing important gets done | Look for the most common interruption and fix it upstream (batch calls, a sign on the door). |
| Planning takes too long | Cap at 5 minutes. Pick tasks first, grid second, estimates third. |
| Day controlled by others (shift, caregiving) | Use the grid as a log, not a plan. Keep only one important task. |
| Paper piles up | Recycle weekly after writing the tally line. |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** the bubbles externalise time, which helps with time blindness. Pair with a visible timer. Keep to one important task on bad days. Allow tasks in any order. Use colour or a sticker for the one that matters.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** the layout is predictable and quiet. Use the B&W version. The notes area can hold "what changed" so surprises are written down, not carried.
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** estimate in spoons as well as bubbles, or treat each bubble as "15 minutes of energy." Block rest on the grid first. A day with one bubble filled is still a record.
- **Depression and anxiety:** the "I did" framing helps. Fill bubbles for any effort, including getting dressed. Avoid reading a week of low totals as proof of anything; note context.
- **Trauma:** don't use the form to monitor yourself harshly. Skip tracking on hard days. The notes area can be private shorthand.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** use symbols or single words. Print larger (Letter instead of Mini). The bubbles need no writing.
- **Low vision:** print Letter at high contrast; thicken lines (the weight-adjusted mono file); use a thick felt pen.
- **Motor or hand pain:** tick bubbles with a single stroke instead of filling. Use a tablet and stylus, or a stamp.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** add care tasks (HRT dose, appointments) as important tasks when they matter that day; they count.
- **Shift workers:** write your own hours; the grid works for a 2 p.m. to 10:30 p.m. shift. Plan the off-shift part of the day only.
- **Low income:** free PDFs; hand-draw on any paper; print on the back of scrap sheets.

## 21. Combining

Sibling docs are written in parallel; if a file name differs when they land, fix the link.

- **With time blocking** ([time-block-planner.md](time-block-planner.md)): use the ETP's three tasks and bubbles as the input to a block plan; the ETP grid is a lighter block column.
- **With the Bullet Journal** ([bullet-journal.md](bullet-journal.md), sibling doc): the ETP replaces the daily log on busy days; bullets go in the notes area. Migrate leftovers as usual.
- **With Ivy Lee or MITs** ([ivy-lee.md](ivy-lee.md), sibling doc): the three important tasks are a short Ivy Lee list with time estimates.
- **With the Eisenhower matrix** ([eisenhower-matrix.md](eisenhower-matrix.md), sibling doc): pick the three from "important, not urgent."
- **With Seah's own forms:** Concrete Goals Tracker for the week's results, Task Progress Tracker for a project's effort, Compact Calendar for the year.
- **With a shutdown ritual:** end the ETP review by saying the day is closed (see the shutdown ritual in [time-block-planner.md](time-block-planner.md)).

## 23. Glossary

- **Bubble:** a small oval standing for 15 minutes; filled when the time is spent.
- **Chore block:** tasks 4–6 on the 2017 form; small jobs grouped together.
- **Compact Calendar:** Seah's one-page year calendar with no gaps between months.
- **Concrete Goals Tracker (CGT):** Seah's first Printable CEO form; points for concrete results, tracked weekly.
- **Day grid:** the time column on the left, with hours you write in.
- **Emergent:** tasks or patterns that appear during the day rather than being planned.
- **Emergent Task Timer (ETT):** a log form; fill a bubble for what you did each 15 minutes.
- **Estimate mark:** a line or slash in the bubble row showing your guess.
- **ETP 8511:** the 2017 Letter-size format name.
- **Long hour:** a 2017 grid feature carried over from the half-size notebook **(exact form unsure)**.
- **Menu of the Day:** Seah's 2006 predecessor form with E–A task sizes.
- **Mini ETP:** half-size (5.5×8.5 or A5) versions 0T7, 3T3, 5T0.
- **MIT:** Most Important Task.
- **Printable CEO:** Seah's series of printable productivity forms (2005 onward).
- **Task Progress Tracker (TPT):** per-project effort log; an "I did" list.
- **Three important tasks:** the core list; "three major tasks" before 2017.

## 24. FAQ

1. **Do I have to use all three tasks?** No. One is fine. Seah himself notes that getting even one done on purpose is hard.
2. **Do the tasks have to be done in order?** No. The numbers are labels for the grid, not a queue.
3. **What if I never use the bubbles?** The sheet still works as a focus page. You lose the estimate-learning part.
4. **How do I mark an estimate?** Draw a line after the bubble you expect to reach, or outline those bubbles lightly. Fill bubbles as you actually work.
5. **What if a task needs more than 2 hours?** Split it (Seah's own rule on the TPT: if 4 hours isn't enough, break it up), or continue on a notes line.
6. **Is it free?** The standard PDFs are free for personal use. The Mini ETP and the ETP Journal are paid. Businesses should ask Seah about licensing.
7. **Letter or Mini?** Letter if you have lots of interruptions to log or want room to write. Mini if it lives in a half-letter binder or you keep days small.
8. **Can I use it for shift work?** Yes. Write your own hours. On shift days, plan only the time off shift.
9. **How is it different from a time-block planner?** The ETP plans a few tasks and tracks time; a time-block planner assigns every work minute a job and re-plans when it breaks.
10. **Should I keep old sheets?** Keep a weekly tally line; the sheets themselves can go.

### d. Proposed method layout: "Emergent Task Planner"

1. Moon/sun/season (existing; small)
2. Events (existing, from your day)

4. Time blocks: blank hours on, step 30

### e. Don't adopt

- **Seah's forms verbatim.** They are his design and trademark ("Printable CEO," "Emergent Task Planner"). Borrow the idea (bubbles, grid, notes), not the artwork or names.
- **Colour-coded versions.** Books are black and white.
- **An on-device timer that beeps.** Notifications are out of scope for the X4.
- **E–A task sizes.** Seah dropped them himself; they add thinking.
- **Point scores (Concrete Goals Tracker).** Points and totals drift toward gamification and badges, which Keeping Watch avoids.
