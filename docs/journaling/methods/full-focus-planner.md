# Full Focus Planner (Michael Hyatt)

A reference for the Full Focus Planner: where it came from, how every part works (annual goals, quarterly Big 3, ideal week, weekly preview, Daily Big 3, daily rituals), how people bend it, how it compares with its sibling goal planners (PowerSheets and the SELF Journal), and what Journalwright Studio should take from it.

Checked against sources on 2026-09-28. Where a detail comes only from a secondary source or couldn't be confirmed, it's marked **(unconfirmed)** or **(secondary)**.

## 2. Summary

The Full Focus Planner is a paper planner that lasts one quarter (about 14 weeks of undated daily pages). It links three levels of goals. You set up to **8–10 annual goals**, choose **three per quarter**, choose a **Weekly Big 3** in a guided hour-long **Weekly Preview**, and then write a **Daily Big 3** each day: "the top three outcomes you need to accomplish for the day." Around that core sit an **Ideal Week** grid (front stage, back stage, off stage), four **daily rituals** (morning, workday startup, workday shutdown, evening), a status key for tasks (done, in progress, delegated, deferred, deleted) and a notes page opposite every day. It comes from Michael Hyatt's coaching business and his books *Living Forward*, *Your Best Year Ever* and *Free to Focus*. It's a tidy, printed version of ideas that are older than it: Ivy Lee's short ranked list, weekly review from Getting Things Done, time blocking and the "perfect week". What it adds is the **quarter** as the unit of paper and a fixed, printed ritual for every level.

## 3. History and origin

### Who created it

- **Michael Hyatt** studied at Baylor, co-founded the publisher Wolgemuth & Hyatt in 1986, joined Thomas Nelson in 1998, and was its CEO from 2005 to April 2011. He started blogging in 2004 and became known for leadership and productivity content.
- In **2012** he founded the company that became **Full Focus** (first called Michael Hyatt & Company). It sells coaching, courses, books and the planner.
- In **2021** he stepped down as CEO. His daughter **Megan Hyatt Miller** took over.
- He is an ordained deacon in the Antiochian Orthodox Church. His own ideal week (below) blocks time for church and vespers. The planner itself is secular.

### Cultural context

- **The 2010s paper-planner boom.** The Bullet Journal went public in 2013. The SELF Journal launched on Kickstarter in 2015. PowerSheets (from 2012) and the Passion Planner grew in the same years. Business owners who had moved everything to phones came back to paper for focus.
- **"High achiever" coaching culture.** The planner speaks to leaders, founders and executives. Language like "win the day", "high achievers" and "rubber meets the road" comes from that world.
- **Quarterly goals.** The 90-day or 12-week goal cycle (also in *The 12 Week Year* by Brian Moran and Michael Lennington, **not checked here**, and the SELF Journal's 13 weeks) was popular in business coaching. The planner's physical size is built around it.

### Key quotes from the creator

- On the Daily Big 3: "Your Daily Big 3 are the top three outcomes you need to accomplish for the day to make progress on your weekly and long-term goals and projects." (Full Focus blog, 2021)
- On the Ideal Week: "It's what your perfect week would look like if you could control 100% of what happens." It's "similar to a financial budget … you spend it on paper first." (Ideal Week lesson, 2019)
- On rituals: "Rituals are a form of self-automation." Rituals are "prescribed procedures for achieving specific results." (Daily Rituals lesson, 2019)
- On limits: for quarterly goals, "Stick to the limit of three or risk major burnout." (Full Focus System page)
- On the gap and the gain (borrowed from coach Dan Sullivan): list wins first, because focusing on "the gap" dings confidence. (Weekly Preview lesson)
- On the daily pages: "This is where the daily battle is won or lost." (Daily Pages lesson)
- On the rebrand: "At some point, I crossed the line where it was no longer about me. It's about us."

## 4. Philosophy and principles

**Stated goal:** connect yearly goals to what you do today, so they don't get lost in "the daily whirlwind".

**Core beliefs:**

1. **Fewer is more.** Three per quarter, three per week, three per day. "Most people sabotage their own goals by setting far too many."
2. **Proactive, not reactive.** "You can either live on purpose, according to a plan … or you can live by accident, reacting to the demands of others." (Ideal Week lesson)
3. **Plan on paper first.** Like a money budget, you decide where time goes before you spend it.
4. **Rest is part of performance.** "Rest is the secret weapon of high achievers." The Weekly Preview has a self-care planner and the Ideal Week has an off-stage row.
5. **Look at the gain, not the gap.** Start each review with wins.
6. **Execution is a learned skill.** The after-action review asks what to keep, improve, start and stop, every week.
7. **Rituals remove decisions.** Doing the same things in the same order saves willpower and "stops you from overthinking."
8. **The quarter is the right horizon.** Short enough to feel urgent (the planner prints a "weeks left in the quarter" widget), long enough for real progress.

**The problem it solves:** long, flat to-do lists where the urgent crowds out the important, and yearly goals that are forgotten by February.

**What it isn't:** it's not a journal for feelings, not a habit tracker at heart (though Habit goals exist), and not a bullet journal. There's no freeform logging system beyond the notes page.

### 5.1 Annual goals

- Write 8–10 goals for the year (the System page says 8; a reviewer's 2018 edition allowed up to 10).
- Goals follow Hyatt's **SMARTER** frame from *Your Best Year Ever*: specific, measurable, actionable, **risky**, time-keyed, exciting, relevant. "Risky" is his twist: a goal should sit just outside your comfort zone.
- Two types from the 2018 edition: **Achievement goals** (a target and a deadline) and **Habit goals** (a streak tracked daily).

### 5.2 Goal detail pages

For each goal: the goal statement, **key motivations** ("why does this matter?"), the **next step**, and how you'll **celebrate**. Reviewing motivations weekly is meant to keep the goal alive when interest drops.

### 5.3 Quarterly Big 3

Pick three of the annual goals to work on this quarter. The rest wait. This is the most cited rule in the system.

### 5.4 Daily rituals

Four rituals, each a short list of steps with estimated minutes and a total:

1. **Morning ritual:** "what you do when you first get up." Important but not urgent things.
2. **Workday startup ritual:** "what you do when you first arrive at the office." Often inbox, calendar, Daily Big 3.
3. **Workday shutdown ritual:** "what you do as you prepare to leave the office." Wrap up, plan tomorrow.
4. **Evening ritual:** "anything that sets you up for a restful night."

The lesson warns: "take into account your season of life … If you attempt to do too much, you're only setting yourself up for frustration." Rituals are re-done each quarter. The four ritual names are printed at the top of each daily page to tick.

### 5.5 Ideal Week

- A grid: days across the top, focus areas (for Hyatt: Self, Work, Family/Friends/Planning) down the side.
- Each day is given a **stage**: **front stage** (what you're paid for, visible to clients or boss), **back stage** (preparation, invisible work), **off stage** (not work; rest and people).
- If stages don't help, use **day themes** ("Mondays could be people days … Fridays could be planning days").
- Share it with family and teammates so they can help protect it.
- "It's not going to happen every week. In fact, it might not happen most weeks."

### 5.6 Daily pages

Two facing pages per day, with pages for 14 weeks so any quarter fits.

- **Left (agenda):** date, "weeks left in the quarter" widget, **Daily Big 3**, **Other tasks** with two narrow columns (status and sequence), and the status legend at the bottom.
- **Right top:** four ritual tick boxes. **Right column:** appointments ("an abbreviated list", even if you use a digital calendar, so you "fully own what you've scheduled").
- **Notes page:** a symbol column on the left and an optional time column on the right.

### 5.7 Daily Big 3

- "These tasks must be important." They can serve a quarterly goal but don't have to.
- The test: if you did these three and nothing else, would the day still count as a win?
- Why three: it forces choice. "Listing out only three tasks … requires more effort and discipline." (Blog, 2021)
- Unfinished ones get the deferred arrow and are swept up in the Weekly Preview. The official material doesn't prescribe carrying them to tomorrow automatically.

### 5.8 Task status key

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ✓ | Done |
| ⁄ (diagonal line) | Started, or waiting on someone ("if you call someone but have to leave a message") |
| ○ | Delegated; write the person's name in brackets |
| → | Deferred to another time |
| ✗ | Deleted: done by someone else or no longer relevant |

This is close to Bullet Journal migration marks, but applied to a pre-printed list.

### 5.9 Weekly Preview

Scheduled for Friday afternoon, Sunday evening or Monday morning. About an hour. Six steps (2019 lesson):

1. **Wins:** three to five from last week, found by scanning the daily pages.
2. **After-action review:** write last week's Weekly Big 3 with a percentage (partial credit allowed). Then **keep / improve / start / stop**.
3. **List sweep:** deferred tasks (arrows), delegated tasks (circles) and starred notes.
4. **Events, deadlines and tasks:** personal and professional. Review calendar, goals (and motivations) and projects.
5. **Weekly Big 3:** "the three biggest items you must accomplish this week."
6. **Self-Care Planner:** sleep, eat, move, connect, relax.

### 5.10 Quarterly Preview

At the end of a quarter: review the goals, celebrate wins, decide what carries over, choose the next Quarterly Big 3, and update rituals and ideal week in the new planner. The official lesson for this step couldn't be opened (404), so details beyond that are **(secondary)**.

### 5.11 Notes, index and extra pages

Notes opposite each day. About ten extra notes pages at the back ("continued from page …"), plus an index from the 2018 edition so old planners can be searched.

## 6. Setting it up

### First setup (60–120 minutes)

1. Write the quarter dates on the cover or first page.
2. Do the **life domain** check: rate each area 1–10.
3. List up to **8 annual goals**. Mark each Achievement or Habit.
4. Fill a **goal detail page** for each: motivations, next step, celebration.
5. Choose the **Quarterly Big 3**.
6. Write the four **rituals** with times. Keep them short.
7. Draw your **Ideal Week**: stage per day, then blocks.
8. Fill the **month grids** with known dates.
9. Do your first **Weekly Preview** (skip step 2, since there's no last week).

### First day

- Morning: tick the morning ritual, then open today's daily page.
- Write the date. Shade the weeks-left widget.
- Write the **Daily Big 3**, then other tasks.
- Copy today's appointments into the right column.
- During the day, mark status and take notes on the right page.
- At shutdown: mark statuses, glance at tomorrow.

### First week

- Days 1–3: just the daily page. Don't worry about the ideal week.
- Days 4–5: check the rituals against reality. Cut steps you skipped.
- End of week: first full Weekly Preview with wins and a real after-action review.
- Expect the ideal week to be wrong. Note what actually happened and redraw it next quarter.

## 7. Daily practice

Order of operations, with typical timing:

| When | Step | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Wake | Morning ritual (tick it on the daily page) | 15–60 min |
| Start of work | Workday startup ritual: check calendar, copy appointments, confirm the Daily Big 3 | 10–15 min |
| Work block 1 | Daily Big 3, item 1 | — |
| Through the day | Other tasks; mark status; notes page for meetings and ideas | — |
| End of work | Workday shutdown ritual: mark statuses, arrow the deferred, draft tomorrow's Big 3 | 10–15 min |
| Bed | Evening ritual | 15–30 min |

Prompts for the Daily Big 3:

- What would make today a win even if nothing else happened?
- Which of this week's Big 3 moves forward today?
- What's the one thing I'd regret not starting?

Many users draft the next day's Big 3 during the shutdown ritual, which makes it very close to the Ivy Lee method (see [ivy-lee-method.md](ivy-lee-method.md)).

## 12. Community practice

What users report on blogs, YouTube walkthroughs and reviews:

- **The Daily Big 3 is the part that sticks.** Many say it's the one habit they keep even after dropping the planner.
- **The Weekly Preview is the second.** People schedule it for Sunday evening or Friday afternoon.
- **The ideal week is often aspirational.** Rachel Held Evans' 2012 post ("a simple chart that explains why I am less successful than Mike Hyatt") is a famous joke about the gap between ideal and actual. Many users say they redraw it every quarter and hit it "some weeks".
- **Rituals get shortened.** People start with 10-step morning rituals and cut to three.
- **Digital calendar plus paper.** Most copy only a few appointments into the planner.
- **Customisation:** colour-coding goals, washi tabs for weeks, using the time column as a habit tracker, pasting printed calendars in.
- **Minimalist versus aesthetic:** smaller than the BuJo split. The planner is already designed, so most customisation is sticky tabs and pens, not drawn spreads.
- **Common complaint:** price (four books a year), the Monday–Friday assumption, and running out of note space.

## 16. Evidence and research

No controlled study of the Full Focus Planner exists. Its parts map onto better-studied ideas.

| Claim | Study | Sample and finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Writing specific plans frees the mind | Masicampo & Baumeister 2011, *JPSP* | Five lab studies of undergraduates (for example 73, 68 and 126). Thinking about unfinished tasks caused intrusive thoughts and worse reading and anagram performance; making a specific "how, when, where" plan removed the effect | Moderate. Lab, students, short time scale. Doesn't test planners |
| If-then plans help goals | Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* | Meta-analysis of 94 tests. Implementation intentions improved goal attainment, d = .65 | Fairly strong for "when X, I'll do Y". The Big 3 is less specific than an if-then plan unless you add a time |
| Listing tomorrow's tasks helps sleep | Scullin et al. 2018, *J. Exp. Psych.: General* | 57 healthy young adults in a sleep lab. Writing a to-do list for 5 minutes at bedtime led to falling asleep faster than writing about completed tasks; the more specific the list, the faster they fell asleep | Small single study, one night, healthy adults. Supports the shutdown ritual |
| People chase urgent over important | Zhu, Yang & Hsee 2018, *J. Consumer Research* | Five experiments: people pick urgent tasks with lower pay-offs | Moderate. Supports deliberately naming important tasks |
| We underestimate time | Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994, *JPSP* | Thesis students predicted 33.9 days; took 55.5 | Strong, much-replicated. Argues for three items, not ten |
| Unfinished tasks stick in memory (Zeigarnik) | Ghibellini & Meier 2025 meta-analysis | No memory advantage for unfinished tasks overall; a general urge to resume them (Ovsiankina) does hold | Weakens the popular "open loops nag you" story; the resumption urge is real |
| Written goals are 42% more likely | Often cited by goal planners (PowerSheets quotes it) | Traces to a small unpublished Dominican University study by Gail Matthews **(not verified here)** | Weak. Don't repeat as fact |

**Claimed versus supported:**

- *Supported in part:* limiting and naming the day's key tasks; making concrete plans; a to-do list before bed.
- *Plausible but untested:* the ideal week, the quarter as the best horizon, the four rituals, the weeks-left widget creating useful urgency.
- *Unsupported:* the "science-based" marketing for the planner as a whole.

## 17. Benefits

- Clear top priorities each day. **Evidenced in part** (plan making, urgency bias).
- Less evening rumination when tomorrow is planned. **Evidenced in part** (Scullin 2018, Masicampo 2011).
- Goals stay visible across the year. **Claimed.**
- Rest is planned, not left over. **Claimed.**
- Weekly learning loop (keep, improve, start, stop). **Claimed**; similar to after-action reviews used in the military and in teams.
- Confidence from listing wins. **Claimed**; loosely related to research on progress and small wins.
- A physical quarter creates a fresh start. **Claimed**; related to "fresh start effect" research (not reviewed here).

## 18. Pitfalls

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Big 3 are really Big 30 (three huge projects) | Write the next *visible* step, sized to one sitting |
| Other tasks list swallows the day | Cap at 6 lines; the rest go to the task manager |
| Ideal week never happens | Treat it as a default, not a test. Aim for one protected block, not a perfect grid |
| Rituals too long | Three steps each, under 15 minutes; season-of-life rule |
| Skipped Weekly Preview | Put it in the calendar; do a 10-minute version (wins, Big 3, self-care) |
| Planner abandoned mid-quarter | Undated pages: restart on today's date, no guilt, no back-fill |
| Cost of four books | Loose-leaf inserts, the Minimalist edition, or a printed template |
| Monday–Friday assumption | Label days by shift, not weekday; one ideal week per rota |
| "High achiever" pressure | Rename things: "Today's 3", "What mattered", "Rest plan" |
| Percent scores feel like grades | Use words: "moved / stuck / done" |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** Big 3 is a strong fit (short, visible). Put the Big 3 where you'll see it (sticky note, phone lock screen). Replace the hour-long preview with a 10-minute one. Use the status marks without the sequence column. Time estimates beside each Big 3 help with time blindness.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** the fixed structure helps. Rituals written as steps reduce transitions. Keep the ideal week as a predictable routine card. Avoid colour-coding overload.
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** make the Big 3 flexible: "1 must, 2 if I can". Add a spoons estimate beside each. Allow "rest" as a Big 3 item. Skip the percentage scoring.
- **Depression:** keep the wins step, drop the after-action review on bad weeks. A Big 3 can be "shower, eat, text someone".
- **Anxiety:** the shutdown ritual (write tomorrow down, then stop) is the most useful piece. Don't read goal pages late at night.
- **Trauma:** avoid "risky" goal language if it triggers pressure. Keep off-stage time as safety time, not a reward.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** icons for status; fewer words; wide lines; dictate the weekly preview.
- **Low vision:** the 6 × 9 print is small; use a large-print template, bold rules, a thick pen.
- **Motor or hand pain:** tick boxes over writing; pre-printed rituals so you only tick; the Pocket size is worse, not better.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** goal and self-care areas can hold transition tasks (appointments, name change paperwork, HRT refills) without them being "wins" to perform. The planner has no gendered content.
- **Shift workers:** label the ideal week by shift pattern. Rituals become "start of shift" and "end of shift". Undated pages help.
- **Low income:** the method is free. A notebook plus the Big 3, a weekly wins list and a self-care line does most of it.

## 24. FAQ

1. **Is it dated?** No. Pages are undated and cover 14 weeks, so any quarter or start day works.
2. **Why only three?** It forces a choice and fits what you can actually hold in mind and time. The limit is a design choice; there's no study behind "three" specifically.
3. **Do the Big 3 have to be goal tasks?** No. They must be important; they can be any project.
4. **What if I don't finish them?** Mark them deferred (→) and pick them up in the Weekly Preview or tomorrow's list.
5. **Can I use it without the goal pages?** Yes. Many people use only the daily pages and weekly preview.
6. **Is there an app?** No official one in 2026. Third-party templates exist.
7. **How is it different from a bullet journal?** It's pre-printed and goal-led. A bullet journal is blank and log-led.
8. **Does it work for shift work?** With changes: label days yourself and design an ideal week per rota.
9. **Is it religious?** The planner isn't. Hyatt's personal examples include church time.
10. **Is it worth the money?** The method is free. You pay for design, paper and the quarterly reset.
11. **What replaced PowerSheets for people who liked it?** Cultivate What Matters closes in early 2027. Full Focus and the SELF Journal are the nearest goal planners; a printed template can reproduce the Tending List.

### d. Proposed method layout: "Full Focus" (planned)

Ordered blocks for one click:

1. `sky` (Moon, sun and season): small date anchor
2. `checks` "Rituals": Morning, Start, Shut down, Evening
3. `top` "Today's 3": n 3
4. `tasks` "Other tasks": rows 5, status column on (or `bullets` with key strip until `tasks` exists)

6. `timeline` (Time blocks): from 7 to 21, every 1 hour; or `shift` on work days

### e. Don't adopt

- **"High achiever", "win the day", "battle" language.** Pressure framing. Use plain labels.
- **Percent scores in the after-action review.** Feels like grading. Use done / moved / stuck.
- **Weeks-left countdown.** Creates urgency by design; clashes with calm, low-stimulation pages.
- **SMARTER "risky" goals.** Fine for some; not a default for a care-first journal.
- **Front stage / back stage / off stage metaphor for everything.** Office-shaped. Shift work needs "work / recover / people" or just time bands.
- **Quarterly books.** The default book is monthly with a yearly Keeper; don't add a third cadence.
- **Colour-coded goals.** Print is black and white.
- **The "42% more likely" statistic.** Weak source; don't print it.
