# Symptom tracking (symptom, pain, medication, cycle and sleep diaries)

> **Not medical advice.** This is a research summary for designing journal pages. It does not diagnose, treat or replace
> a clinician. If a symptom is new, severe or frightening, contact a health professional or emergency services.

## 2. Summary

Symptom tracking means writing down, at the time or close to it, how your body and mind are doing: pain level and where
it is, headaches and what you took for them, medicines and doses, bleeding and cycle symptoms, hormone doses, and sleep.
The point is memory. By the time you see a clinician, the last month has blurred into "it's been bad", and a page of
short, dated entries replaces that blur with numbers you can both look at. Standard tools exist and are worth borrowing:
the 0–10 pain scale, the Consensus Sleep Diary, headache diaries used in migraine research, and daily premenstrual
ratings. Research shows these measures are reliable, that routine symptom reporting can improve care in some settings,
and that paper diaries are often back-filled from memory, which weakens them. The best personal version is small: a few
circles a day, a weekly look, and a one-page summary for appointments. For Keeping Watch, most of the pieces already exist
(Care check-in, Pain and Sleep times blocks, HRT & body care checks, X4 check-ins); the gaps are a body map, a PRN
("as needed") dose log, cycle or injection-day marks, and an appointment summary page.

## 3. History and origin

**Who created it.** Nobody owns symptom tracking. It comes from four clinical traditions that grew side by side:

- **Pain measurement.** The visual analogue scale (a 10 cm line) and numerical rating scales (0–10) spread through pain
  research from the 1970s onward. A 2011 systematic review of 54 studies found numerical scales had better compliance
  than line or word scales in 15 of 19 studies that reported it, and recommended the NRS for most settings
  (Hjermstad et al., 2011). In 2001 Farrar and colleagues, using daily diary data from 2,724 people in ten pain trials,
  showed that a drop of about **2 points or about 30%** on the 0–10 scale matched what patients called "much improved".
  In 2005 the IMMPACT group set core outcome domains for chronic pain trials, including pain intensity, physical and
  emotional functioning, and ratings of global improvement (Dworkin et al., 2005).
- **Headache diaries.** Headache medicine has long relied on diaries to count headache days and medication days. The
  International Headache Society's trial guidelines (first 1991, most recently updated for episodic migraine in 2020,
  Diener et al.) build their outcomes on diaries. The ICHD-3 criteria for medication-overuse headache depend on counting
  days of medicine use; for some cases the classification says "in almost all cases, this necessitates diary follow-up".
- **Sleep diaries.** Sleep clinics used many home-made sleep logs until an expert group published the **Consensus Sleep
  Diary** (Carney, Buysse, Ancoli-Israel, Edinger, Krystal, Lichstein, Morin, 2012). They gathered diaries from 25
  insomnia experts, rated and revised the items, and tested wording with six focus groups (47 people).
- **Menstrual charting.** Menstrual calendars are old; clinical daily rating forms such as the **Daily Record of
  Severity of Problems (DRSP)** were built for diagnosing premenstrual dysphoric disorder (Endicott, Nee and Harrison,
  2006). ACOG has called the menstrual cycle a "vital sign" (2015, reaffirmed 2021, per Vollmar et al., 2025).

**Cultural context.** Chronic illness and disability communities made self-tracking ordinary: spoon theory, "flare"
logs, and bringing a symptom list to appointments are standard advice in patient groups. The quantified-self movement
(around 2007 onward) pushed apps and wearables. After the 2022 *Dobbs* decision in the US, privacy of period and health
apps became a mainstream worry, which pushed some people back to paper.

**Key quotes.** There is no creator to quote. Two lines from the sources capture the method:

- "The goal is to record information that will be valuable to you and your doctor later, not to add another stressful
  task." (American Migraine Foundation, headache diary guidance)
- Morning sleep diary items should be completed "within one hour of getting out of bed." (Carney et al., 2012)

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- **Memory is unreliable; the page is not.** Recall of pain and sleep drifts toward the most recent and the worst days.
  Dated entries fix that. (Dietch and Taylor, 2021, found a retrospective sleep questionnaire had more bias than a daily
  diary.)
- **Measure what you will use.** Every item should answer a question you or your clinician actually has: "Is the new dose
  working?", "How many headache days this month?", "Do bad days follow short sleep?"
- **Same scale, same time.** A 0–10 score is only comparable with itself if you use it the same way each day.
- **Write it near the time.** Stone et al. (2002) showed that paper diaries filled in later look complete but are not.
- **Small beats complete.** Three circles every day beat a full page on good days only.
- **You own the data.** Paper keeps health data at home. Apps may not (see section 15).
- **Tracking serves care, not judgement.** A high pain number is information, not a failure.

**The problem it solves.** Appointments are short, symptoms fluctuate, and medicine changes are hard to judge from
memory. A symptom diary turns "I think it's worse since the dose change" into "headache days went from 9 to 4".

## 5. Core components

| Element | What it is | Typical time |
|---|---|---|
| Symptom rating | 0–10 or 0–3 score for each chosen symptom | 10 s each |
| Pain NRS | Pain now or average pain, 0 = none, 10 = worst imaginable | 5 s |
| PEG | Pain, Enjoyment interference, General activity interference, each 0–10 | 20 s |
| Body map | Front/back outline; shade where it hurts | 20 s |
| Headache/migraine log | Start, end, severity, features, acute medicine, relief | 1 min per attack |
| Medication log | Scheduled doses ticked; PRN doses with time, dose, reason, effect | 10 s per dose |
| Side-effect notes | New or changed effects, dated | as needed |
| Cycle log | Bleeding days, flow, cycle day, symptoms | 10 s |
| HRT log | Dose, route, injection or patch-change day, site, symptoms across the cycle | 10 s |
| Sleep diary | Consensus Sleep Diary items, done in the morning | 1 min |
| Triggers and context | Weather, food, stress, work shift, alcohol, caffeine | 20 s |
| Flare or event note | A few lines when something unusual happens | 1–3 min |
| Weekly review | Count days, spot patterns | 5–10 min |
| Appointment summary | One page: top 3 concerns, counts, changes, questions | 10–15 min |

### Symptom rating
Pick 1–5 symptoms. Name them in your own words ("brain fog", "joint pain", "nausea"). Use a short scale: 0–3 (none,
mild, moderate, severe) is quick and easy to print as bubbles; 0–10 matches clinical pain scales. Keep the scale fixed
for at least a month.

### Pain NRS
The 0–10 numerical rating scale is the most widely used pain intensity measure; Hjermstad et al. (2011) found it had
better compliance than visual analogue or verbal scales in most studies that compared them. Decide which question you
answer, and keep it: "pain right now", "average pain today" or "worst pain today". Farrar et al. (2001) found a 2-point
or 30% drop is what people typically feel as "much improved", which helps judge a new treatment. Keeping Watch's current
Pain preset uses a 6-step "none … bad" scale; that is fine for personal use but does not map to 0–10.

### PEG
Three 0–10 items: average **P**ain in the past week, interference with **E**njoyment of life, interference with **G**eneral
activity. Score = mean of the three (Krebs et al., 2009; 500 primary care patients plus 646 VA patients; good validity,
Cronbach's alpha 0.73–0.89). Useful weekly, because pain that interferes less is progress even if the number is the same.

### Body map
A front and back outline where you shade or mark pain. Clinical versions segment the body (the CHOIR Body Map has 74
regions, 36 front and 38 back). On paper, a small outline with a few letters (A = ache, B = burning, S = stabbing,
N = numb) works. Weekly is usually enough unless pain moves a lot.

### Headache and migraine log
Per attack: date, start time, end time, severity (0–3 or 0–10), features (one side, throbbing, light or sound
sensitivity, nausea, aura), acute medicine and time, relief after 2 hours, possible triggers. Per month: count headache
days and acute-medicine days. ICHD-3 defines medication-overuse headache as headache on 15 or more days a month in
someone with a pre-existing headache disorder, with regular overuse of acute medicine for more than 3 months, on
"10 or more or 15 or more days/month, depending on the medication". Counting medicine days is therefore one of the most
useful things a headache diary does. (Which threshold applies to which drug is for a clinician to judge.)

### Medication log
Two kinds of dose:
- **Scheduled** doses: a tick per dose. Keeping Watch's Care check-in already has a meds tick.
- **PRN (as needed)** doses: time, medicine, dose, why, and whether it helped. These matter for headache counting, for
  pain treatment reviews, and for not doubling a dose by mistake.
Also keep a **medicine list** (name, strength, how taken, prescriber, start date, why) in a stable place. Keeping Watch's
Keeper is the natural home. Note side effects with a date and the medicine you suspect.

### Cycle log
Bleeding days and flow (spotting, light, medium, heavy), cycle day, and chosen symptoms. FIGO's normal adult ranges, as
cited by Vollmar et al. (2025): cycles every 24–38 days, bleeding up to 8 days, and length varying by no more than 7–9
days. For suspected PMDD, clinicians use **two cycles** of daily ratings (the DRSP is a validated daily form; Endicott et
al., 2006). Many trans and non-binary people also track cycles, or the return or absence of cyclic symptoms on hormones.

### HRT log
Dose, route (pill, patch, gel, injection), time, and for injections or patches the day and site. Some people on
injections note how they feel across the injection interval (for example, low energy or mood before the next dose) to
discuss with a prescriber. **(Uncertain: the size and meaning of such "trough" symptoms varies by person and regimen;
treat it as a question to bring, not a conclusion.)** Lab dates and results belong in the Keeper. Clinical guidance
recommends clinical and lab monitoring every 3 months in the first year of hormone therapy and then once or twice a year
(Endocrine Society guideline, Hembree et al., 2017). The sibling doc
[gender-euphoria-and-hrt-log.md](gender-euphoria-and-hrt-log.md) covers the affirming side of this log.

### Sleep diary
The core Consensus Sleep Diary has nine items: time into bed; time you tried to sleep; how long it took to fall asleep;
how many times you woke; how long those wakings lasted in total; time of final waking; time you got out of bed; sleep
quality rating; and comments. Optional items add early waking, naps, alcohol, caffeine and sleep medicine. Fill it in
within an hour of getting up. AASM suggests two weeks, completed daily. Diaries tend to **overestimate** sleep time
compared with EEG or actigraphy (about 30–40 minutes on average in Dietch and Taylor, 2021) but are accurate on timing,
and they are the standard tool for insomnia treatment (CBT-I).

### Triggers and context
Only track suspected triggers you would act on. Common ones: sleep, stress, missed meals, alcohol, caffeine, weather,
screen time, cycle day, shift work. Keeping Watch already prints weather, air quality, work shift and sleep.

### Flare or event note
A short, dated note: what happened, what you tried, what helped, and whether you contacted anyone. These are the lines a
clinician most often wants.

### Weekly review
Count days (headache days, PRN doses, nights under 6 hours), glance for patterns, and write one line: "What should I
mention next appointment?"

### Appointment summary
One page to hand over or read from. See section 9.

## 6. Setting it up

**First setup (about 20 minutes).**
1. Write down **one question** you want answered. Examples: "Is the new preventive working?", "Is my pain getting worse?",
   "Do I sleep worse on work nights?"
2. Choose **up to five items** that answer it. More is not better.
3. Choose scales. Pain: 0–10. Other symptoms: 0–3. Sleep: times plus a 1–5 quality.
4. Choose **when**: a fixed moment such as the morning coffee (sleep) and the evening wind-down (pain, meds, symptoms).
5. Put the medicine list in the Keeper (or on the inside cover).
6. Mark your next appointment date in the calendar and put a summary page just before it.

**First day.** Fill in the items once in the evening, and the sleep items in the morning. Don't go back to fill in
yesterday from memory; leave a blank. Blanks are honest data.

**First week.** Keep going with the same items. At the end, spend 5 minutes counting. If an item never changed or you
never looked at it, drop it. If you kept forgetting, move the entry to a moment you already do daily (meds, brushing
teeth).

## 7. Daily practice

| When | What | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Within 1 hour of getting up | Sleep diary: bed, lights out, time to sleep, wakings, up, quality | 1 min |
| With each scheduled dose | Tick | 5 s |
| Each PRN dose, at the time | Time, dose, why | 10 s |
| During or after a headache/flare | Start time, severity, what you took; add end time and relief later | 30 s |
| Evening | Pain NRS (or chosen question), symptom ratings, cycle/HRT mark, one line of notes | 1–2 min |

**Order of operations.** Record *at the time* when you can (PRN doses, attack start). Everything else goes in one short
evening pass. If the day was bad, write only the number and one word. Low effort still counts.

**Prompts.**
- What was my pain today, 0–10?
- Did anything new happen in my body?
- What did I take, and did it help?
- What's one thing to remember for my next appointment?

## 8. Rhythms

- **Weekly (5–10 min):** count headache days, PRN doses, poor sleep nights; do a PEG; update the body map if pain moved;
  write one "mention at appointment" line.
- **Monthly (15 min):** totals onto the month page; compare with last month; cycle length; carry medicine changes to the
  Keeper medicine list. Keeping Watch's "Closing the month" page already totals meds taken and average sleep.
- **Before an appointment (15 min):** build the one-page summary (section 9). Bring the raw pages too.
- **Quarterly:** is each item still answering a question? Drop what isn't. Check lab dates for HRT.
- **Yearly:** archive the summaries in the Keeper. Keep the medicine history (start, stop, why) forever; it saves time
  with every new clinician.

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Who / where | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| Consensus Sleep Diary (core, CSD-M, CSD-E) | Carney et al., 2012 | Standard 9 items; morning or morning+evening versions |
| AASM two-week sleep diary | American Academy of Sleep Medicine | Printable grid with caffeine, alcohol, exercise, meds |
| Headache diary | IHS trial guidelines; American Migraine Foundation | Counts headache days and acute medicine days |
| Pain diary with NRS | Pain trials (Farrar 2001) | Daily 0–10, often morning and evening |
| PEG | Krebs et al., 2009 | Three items, weekly, adds function |
| Body map / pain drawing | CHOIR (Stanford) and many clinics | Location instead of intensity |
| DRSP | Endicott et al., 2006 | Daily premenstrual ratings over two cycles for PMDD |

| PRO questionnaires (PROMIS) | NIH, from 2004 | Validated short forms for pain, fatigue, sleep, mood |
| Flare journal | Chronic illness communities | Only on flare days; what triggered, what helped |
| Spoon log | Chronic illness communities (from Christine Miserandino's spoon theory) | Energy as a budget |

## 12. Community practice

- **What people actually do.** Chronic illness and migraine communities (patient forums, Reddit groups for chronic pain,
  migraine, EDS, POTS, endometriosis; YouTube "bullet journal chronic illness" videos) show the same pattern: a monthly
  grid of small boxes for pain, fatigue and meds, plus a flare log and an appointment page. People often say the
  appointment page is the most useful part. **(Community patterns summarised from general observation, not a study.)**
- **Common customisations.** Colour coding pain levels (in black-and-white print: fill density instead), a "good day" mark,
  a "questions for my doctor" collection, weather/barometric notes for migraine, a symptom "key" of letters.
- **Aesthetic vs minimalist.** The aesthetic camp draws body outlines and decorative trackers; the minimalist camp uses
  a grid of dots or a single line per day. For a tired or flaring body, minimalist wins: it survives bad days.
- **Burnout is common.** Many people describe stopping because tracking made them focus on symptoms. The fix they report
  is fewer items and tracking only on flare days.

## 13. Official products and formats

Not applicable as a single product. Related formats: free printable clinical diaries (AASM sleep diary; headache diaries
from migraine charities), commercial chronic illness planners, and apps (section 15). Clinical forms such as the DRSP and
PROMIS short forms have their own licensing terms; check before printing them in a sold book.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- Any pen that doesn't smear on a shaky day; a thicker grip helps hand pain.
- Paper: 80–100 gsm is plenty for ballpoint or gel. Bubbles and lines print cleanly in black and white.
- A clock or phone time for PRN doses (write the time, not "afternoon").
- Optional: a weekly pill organiser (reduces "did I take it?"), a small stamp of a body outline, removable sticky tabs
  for appointment pages.
- Avoid: colour-only coding (fails in B&W and for colour-blind readers).

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps.** Migraine apps (for example Migraine Buddy), period apps, pain apps and general trackers (Bearable, Daylio).
  Pros: timestamps, charts, reminders. Cons: privacy, subscriptions, phone fatigue.
- **Privacy.** In the US, HIPAA covers providers, insurers and their partners, not most consumer apps. HHS said in 2022
  that "in most cases, HIPAA rules do not protect the privacy or security of individuals' health information when they
  access or store it on personal cellphones or tablets" (reported by ProPublica, 2022). The FTC's 2021 final order against
  Flo Health found the app had shared users' health data with marketing and analytics firms including Facebook and
  Google despite privacy promises. Paper at home, or an offline device, avoids this class of risk.
- **E-ink.** reMarkable, Kindle Scribe and Boox can hold PDF trackers; handwriting stays local unless synced.
- **Scanning.** Keeping Watch pages scan to the owner's app; health pages should be routed to a private destination.
- **X4.** The Keeping Watch X4 already logs meds, meals, care, mood, anxiety, spoons and sleep offline, and exports a
  month log over its own hotspot. That is a strong privacy position: nothing leaves the device.
- **What's lost or gained.** Paper loses automatic timestamps (so back-filling is easier; Stone et al., 2002, measured
  real paper compliance of 11% against 90% reported) and charts. It gains privacy, zero setup and no battery.
  Mitigation on paper: write the actual time next to entries that matter (PRN, attack start), leave blanks rather than
  reconstructing.

## 16. Evidence and research

| Study | Sample | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Farrar et al., 2001, *Pain* | 2,724 people, 10 pregabalin trials | ~2 points or ~30% drop on 0–10 NRS = clinically important | Large; drug-trial populations |
| Hjermstad et al., 2011, *J Pain Symptom Manage* | Review of 54 studies | NRS had better compliance than VAS/VRS in 15 of 19 | Systematic review; anchors vary |
| Dworkin et al., 2005 (IMMPACT), *Pain* | Expert consensus | Core domains for chronic pain trials: pain, physical and emotional function, global improvement, symptoms/adverse events | Consensus, not a trial |
| Krebs et al., 2009, *J Gen Intern Med* | 500 + 646 patients | PEG valid and reliable (alpha 0.73–0.89) | Primary care and VA samples |
| Stone et al., 2002, *BMJ* | 80 chronic pain patients | Paper diaries: 90% reported vs 11% actual on-time compliance; electronic 94% | Small; lab-style monitoring; old tech |
| Carney et al., 2012, *Sleep* | Expert consensus + 47 focus group participants | Core 9-item Consensus Sleep Diary | Development paper |
| Maich et al., 2018, *Behav Sleep Med* | Insomnia and good sleepers | CSD valid, sensitive to CBT-I; 99.8% completion over 14 days | Research setting |
| Dietch and Taylor, 2021, *J Clin Sleep Med* | 80 community adults, 7 days | Diary overestimated sleep ~31–40 min vs actigraphy/EEG; timing accurate | Short, small |
| Endicott et al., 2006, *Arch Womens Ment Health* | 27 + 243 participants | DRSP reliable, valid, sensitive to treatment | PMDD diagnosis context |
| Basch et al., 2016, *J Clin Oncol* | 766 patients on chemotherapy, RCT | Weekly symptom self-report with nurse alerts: better quality of life, fewer ER visits (34% vs 41%) | Cancer, with clinical response built in |
| Basch et al., 2017, *JAMA* | Same trial, 7-year follow-up | Median survival 31.2 vs 26.0 months | One centre; mechanism is clinician response |
| ICHD-3 (2018) | Classification | MOH defined by headache days and medicine days per month | Diagnostic standard |

**Claimed versus supported.**
- *Supported:* standard scales and diaries measure symptoms reliably; daily prospective diaries beat retrospective
  recall; routine symptom reporting **that clinicians act on** improved outcomes in cancer care.
- *Claimed, weakly supported:* that tracking alone reduces symptoms. The Basch benefits came from nurses responding to
  alerts, not from writing itself.
- *Uncertain:* whether self-tracking raises health anxiety for some people. It is widely reported in communities; good
  trials are scarce **(uncertain)**.

## 17. Benefits

- Clearer appointments and better shared decisions (**evidenced**, indirectly: Basch trials; clinical guidance).
- Accurate counts of headache and medicine days, which affect diagnosis (**evidenced**: ICHD-3 relies on them).
- Judging whether a treatment works, using a 2-point / 30% rule of thumb (**evidenced**: Farrar 2001).
- Better sleep treatment (diaries are part of CBT-I) (**evidenced**).
- Spotting cycle-linked symptoms (**evidenced** for PMDD diagnosis with daily ratings).
- Catching side effects early (**claimed**, plausible).
- Feeling more in control, less "gaslit" by fuzzy memory (**claimed**, common in patient reports).
- Privacy on paper (**evidenced** in the sense that US law leaves most apps unprotected).

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---|---|
| Tracking too many things | Cap at five; drop anything unused after two weeks |
| Back-filling from memory | Leave blanks; write times for PRN and attack starts |
| Scales drift ("a 7 now is a 5 then") | Write your own anchors once ("7 = can't work") and keep them |
| Health anxiety grows | Track only on flare days, or weekly; stop and talk to a clinician if it feeds worry |
| Nobody ever reads it | Build the one-page summary before appointments |
| Bad days = no entries | One number and one word is a complete entry |
| Colour codes lost in print | Use fill density, letters and bubbles |
| App data shared | Prefer paper or offline device; read privacy policies |
| Tracking replaces care | Red-flag symptoms go to a clinician, not a diary |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** attach tracking to an existing habit (meds); bubbles not writing; one fixed spot on the page; the X4 button
  check-in suits "no pen nearby" moments.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** add sensory load to context (Keeping Watch has a Sensory load block); use concrete anchors
  rather than vague words; allow "don't know".
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** minimum entry = pain number; flare-only logs; weekly PEG instead of daily detail.
- **Depression and anxiety:** watch for rumination; keep a "what helped" line; if mood items are tracked, pair with the
  Support and Safety plan pages (see [safety-planning.md](safety-planning.md)).
- **Trauma:** body maps can be activating; make them optional, allow a word list instead; no body silhouettes with
  gendered shapes.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** circle, don't write; icons and letters; big bubbles.
- **Low vision:** letter size book, 44 px-equivalent bubbles (about 6 mm), high contrast; X4 large type.
- **Motor or hand pain:** fill bubbles with a single stroke; fewer items; voice notes if needed.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** neutral body outline; "bleeding" not "period" in labels; HRT dose and site log;
  cycle tracking for anyone who wants it; no assumptions about which body parts exist. Privacy matters more where laws
  are hostile.
- **Shift workers:** sleep diary by *sleep period*, not by night ("main sleep" and "nap"); record shift type (Keeping
  Watch's Work shift block).
- **Low income:** free printable forms; paper over subscriptions; bring pages instead of an app.

## 23. Glossary

- **Acute medicine:** taken to treat an attack (for example a painkiller or triptan).
- **Actigraphy:** a wrist device estimating sleep from movement.
- **Aura:** temporary visual or sensory symptoms before or during some migraines.
- **Back-filling (hoarding):** filling in diary entries later from memory.
- **Body map:** outline where pain locations are marked.
- **CBT-I:** cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia; uses sleep diaries.
- **Clinically important difference:** the smallest change patients notice as meaningful (about 2 points or 30% on 0–10 pain).
- **Consensus Sleep Diary (CSD):** standard sleep diary (Carney 2012); CSD-M morning, CSD-E evening + morning.
- **Cycle day:** day of the menstrual cycle, day 1 = first day of bleeding.
- **DRSP:** Daily Record of Severity of Problems, a daily premenstrual rating form.
- **Flare:** a period when a chronic condition gets worse.
- **HRT / GAHT:** hormone therapy; gender-affirming hormone therapy.
- **ICHD-3:** International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition.
- **MOH:** medication-overuse headache.
- **NRS:** numerical rating scale, usually 0–10.
- **PEG:** Pain, Enjoyment, General activity scale.
- **PMDD / PME:** premenstrual dysphoric disorder; premenstrual exacerbation of another condition.
- **PRN:** *pro re nata*, "as needed" medicine.
- **PRO:** patient-reported outcome.
- **PROMIS:** NIH's Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System.
- **Sleep efficiency:** time asleep ÷ time in bed.
- **Sleep onset latency:** minutes to fall asleep.
- **Trough:** the lowest drug level before the next dose.
- **VAS / VRS:** visual analogue scale (a line); verbal rating scale (words).

## 24. FAQ

**How many things should I track?** Up to five. Start with the one question you want answered.

**0–10 or 0–3?** Use 0–10 for pain (it matches clinical scales and the 2-point rule). Use 0–3 for other symptoms if it's
faster.

**What if I miss days?** Leave them blank. Don't reconstruct. Clinicians can work with gaps; they can't spot invented data.

**How long before an appointment should I start?** Two weeks minimum for sleep (AASM); a full month for headaches; two
cycles for premenstrual symptoms.

**Should I bring the diary or a summary?** Both. The summary for the conversation, the pages if they want detail.

**Does tracking make pain worse?** For some people, focus on symptoms feeds worry. If you notice that, track less often,
or only on flare days, and mention it to your clinician. **(Evidence is thin.)**

**Is a period or HRT app private?** Often not. In the US most apps are outside HIPAA; at least one major period app was
found by the FTC to have shared health data. Paper or an offline device is safer.

**My sleep diary says I slept less than my watch says. Which is right?** Neither exactly. Diaries tend to overestimate sleep
compared with EEG, and wearables vary. For insomnia treatment, the diary is the standard.

**I work nights. How do I fill in a sleep diary?** Log each main sleep period and naps with real times, whatever the clock
says, and mark the shift type.

**Can I change the scale partway?** Only at a clean break (a new month) and note it, or your trend becomes meaningless.

### e. Don't adopt

- **Reproducing licensed clinical forms verbatim** (DRSP, PROMIS short forms, BPI) in a sold book without checking terms.
  Use the ideas and plain wording instead.
- **Dose reminders, alarms or streaks on the X4.** Notifications and badges are out of scope and add pressure.
- **Scores, diagnoses or "you may have X" logic.** Keeping Watch records; clinicians interpret.
- **Fertility-awareness contraception charts.** Too high stakes for a general journal.
- **Scan codes on the Keeper medicine list.** The Keeper stays code-free.
- **Cloud sync of symptom data.** Keeps the privacy promise intact.

## 26. Open questions

- How often does self-tracking raise health anxiety, and for whom? Good trials are scarce.
- Do paper diaries with a written time stamp reduce back-filling enough? No study found.
- The best way to log sleep for rotating shift workers in the Consensus Sleep Diary format is not standardised.
- Whether tracking symptoms across an injection interval helps HRT dosing decisions is unclear; it's a conversation aid.
- The bridge spec exports a `scale` block as 1..steps, so an 11-step 0–10 pain scale would be stored as 1–11. Whether to add a zero-based option or shift on display is open until the bridge lands in `x4/tools/export_pack.py`.
- Licensing of the Consensus Sleep Diary for printing in a sold book was not confirmed.
