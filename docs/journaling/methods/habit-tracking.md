# Habit Tracking

A reference for habit tracking in journals: what habit research actually says (Lally's 66 days, Wood and Neal on context, Gollwitzer's if-then plans), the popular systems (James Clear's *Atomic Habits*, B.J. Fogg's *Tiny Habits*, "don't break the chain"), every common tracker design, the harm broken streaks can do, and how a printed journal can measure without shame.

Checked against sources on 2026-09-28. Where a detail couldn't be confirmed from a primary source, it's marked **(unconfirmed)**. Where a claim is popular but not tested, it's marked **(claimed)**.

## 2. Summary

A habit is a behaviour that runs on autopilot when a familiar context cues it. Research says habits form through **repetition in a stable context**, not through willpower, and that it takes longer than people think: in Lally's 2010 study, a median of 66 days, with a range of 18 to 254. Missing a single day didn't derail the process. Tracking helps because recording progress, especially on paper, improves goal attainment. Linking a new behaviour to a clear cue ("after I pour coffee, I take my meds") helps more than reminders, which can support repetition while slowing true habit formation. The popular books (*Atomic Habits*, *Tiny Habits*) package these ideas well but go beyond the evidence in places. The main design risk is the streak: an intact streak motivates, but a visibly broken one can lower engagement, especially when people blame themselves. Good trackers count what you did, allow partial credit, and make restarting the normal path.

## 3. History and origin

### Who and their background

- **Wendy Wood**, social psychologist (Duke, then University of Southern California). Leading researcher on habits in daily life; author of *Good Habits, Bad Habits* (2019).
- **David T. Neal**, psychologist and behaviour consultant, Wood's co-author on the 2007 *Psychological Review* model.
- **Phillippa Lally**, health psychologist at University College London (UCL), with **Jane Wardle** (UCL Health Behaviour Research Centre) and **Benjamin Gardner** (then UCL, later King's College London and Surrey). Ran the best-known real-world habit formation study.
- **Peter M. Gollwitzer**, motivation psychologist (New York University and University of Konstanz). Introduced implementation intentions.
- **B.J. Fogg**, behaviour scientist at Stanford since 1995, founder of the Behavior Design Lab. Author of *Tiny Habits* (January 2020).
- **James Clear**, writer and entrepreneur. Author of *Atomic Habits* (2018), one of the best-selling self-help books of its decade.

### Cultural context

The "21 days to form a habit" myth dominated self-help for decades. UCL's 2010 study, widely reported, replaced it with "about 66 days" and a large range. App stores then filled with streak-based trackers, borrowing game mechanics (streaks, badges, points). Paper journaling adopted the monthly grid. By the 2020s a backlash grew against streak anxiety, with apps adding "streak freezes" and writers promoting "never miss twice" or "rest days".

### Key quotes

- Gardner, Lally and Wardle: habits are "actions that are triggered automatically in response to contextual cues."
- Gollwitzer: the if-then format is "When situation x arises, I will perform response y."
- Clear: "A habit tracker is a simple way to measure whether you did a habit."
- Clear: "Never miss twice. ... Missing once is an accident. Missing twice is the start of a new habit."
- Fogg: "Behavior happens when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt come together at the same moment."

## 4. Philosophy and principles

**The problem it solves.** Good intentions fade; behaviour defaults to old patterns. Tracking makes repetition visible and gives a small reward for doing it.

**Research-based principles:**
1. **Same cue, same response.** Habits are context-response links. Do it at the same time and place, after the same event (Wood and Neal 2007; Gardner 2012).
2. **Repetition builds automaticity slowly and unevenly.** Early repetitions add the most; gains then flatten. Expect about 2–3 months, possibly much longer (Lally 2010; Singh 2024).
3. **A single miss doesn't reset you.** In Lally's study, missing one opportunity didn't materially change the curve.
4. **Plan the "when" and "where".** If-then plans have a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006).
5. **Event cues beat reminders.** Reminders help you repeat but can keep the habit dependent on the reminder (Stawarz 2015).
6. **Record progress physically.** Monitoring helps goals, more when written down (Harkin 2016).
7. **Start small and choose your own.** Self-chosen, simpler behaviours form faster (Singh 2024; Gardner 2012).

**Popular principles (claimed):**
8. **Make it obvious, attractive, easy, satisfying** (Clear's four laws).
9. **Anchor, tiny behaviour, celebrate** (Fogg).
10. **Never miss twice** (Clear).
11. **Identity**: each tick is "a vote for the type of person you want to become" (Clear) **(claimed)**.

## 5. Core components

| Element | Kind | What it's for |
|---|---|---|
| Habit list (3–5 items) | Section | What you're building |
| Cue / anchor | Plan | When and where it happens |
| Tiny version | Plan | The minimum that still counts |
| Mark (tick, dot, X, half-fill) | Tracker | Record doing it |
| Grid | Page type | Month at a glance |
| Daily dots | Day block | Mark on the day page |
| Streak / chain | Tracker style | Consecutive days |
| Totals | Tracker | "Days done this month" |
| Automaticity check | Rating | Is it becoming automatic? |
| Review | Ritual | Adjust monthly |
| Restart rule | Rule | What to do after a miss |

### Habit list
Pick 1–5. Clear suggests limiting manual tracking to your 3–4 most important habits, because tracking is itself a habit that takes effort.

### Cue / anchor
Write the context: "After I brush my teeth, I stretch." Fogg calls this an anchor; Clear calls it habit stacking; research calls it an implementation intention. It's the single most evidenced part.

### Tiny version
Fogg's rule: make the behaviour so small it's easy on a bad day (one push-up, floss one tooth). The tiny version lets a low day still count, which protects the chain and the self-story.

### Marks
- **Tick or X:** binary.
- **Dot:** fill a circle.
- **Half-fill:** partial credit, e.g. the tiny version, or "did some". Journalwright Studio's Habit dots use empty / half / full.
- **Slash then X:** slash = planned, X = done **(community convention)**.
- **Number:** minutes, glasses, pages.

### Grid
Habits down the side, days across (or the reverse). The monthly overview shows patterns, like weekends dropping off.

### Daily dots
A row of labelled circles on the day page. Easier to mark at the point of doing, but harder to see the month.

### Streak / chain
Consecutive days. Motivating while intact (Silverman and Barasch 2023). The risk: a broken streak that's shown prominently can lower engagement, more so if you blame yourself.

### Totals
"18 of 30 days" instead of "streak: 0". Totals never reset, so a miss costs one day, not the whole run.

### Automaticity check
The habit goal is automaticity, not frequency. Once a month, rate one line: "I do it without thinking" 1–5. This is based on the 4-item SRBAI (Gardner et al. 2012), whose items ask whether the behaviour is something you do automatically, without having to consciously remember, without thinking, and before you realise you're doing it **(item wording from memory; unconfirmed)**.

### Review
Monthly: keep, shrink, change the cue, or drop.

### Restart rule
A pre-written rule for after a miss: "Tiny version tomorrow." "Never miss twice" is the popular form; a gentler form is "next time, smaller".

## 6. Setting it up

### First setup (10 minutes)
1. **List candidates.** Write every habit you'd like. Circle 1–3.
2. **Shrink each.** Write the tiny version: "1 glass", "2 minutes outside", "1 stretch".
3. **Attach a cue.** "After [existing routine], I will [tiny habit]." Pick routines that happen every day, including work days and off days.
4. **Pick a mark.** Empty / half / full circles work well: half = tiny version.
5. **Choose where it lives.** Day page dots (mark at the moment) and/or a month grid (see patterns). Pick one to start.
6. **Write the restart rule** at the top: "Missed? Tiny version next time."

### First day
Do the tiny version right after the cue. Mark it immediately. If you enjoy a small celebration (Fogg's "Shine"), do it: a word, a fist pump.

### First week
- Mark each day. Expect misses.
- Day 3–4: check the cue. If you keep forgetting, the cue is wrong, not you. Choose a more reliable anchor.
- Day 7: count, don't judge. "5 of 7" is a good first week.

## 7. Daily practice

| When | Time | Steps |
|---|---|---|
| At the cue | seconds | Do the habit (tiny version counts) |
| Right after | 5 s | Mark it: full or half |
| Evening | 30 s | Glance; fill any you did but didn't mark; leave the rest empty (don't draw a failure mark) |
| Optional | 30 s | One note: what helped or got in the way |

**Order:** cue → behaviour → mark. Marking at the point of doing turns the mark into part of the routine and a small immediate reward.

**Don't mark misses.** An empty circle is enough. A red X on a miss is a failure signal that serves no purpose.

---

## 8. Rhythms

- **Weekly (2–5 min):** count done days. Look for the pattern: which days drop? Adjust the cue for those days (for example, a different anchor on shift days).
- **Monthly (10 min):** total each habit ("21/31"). Rate automaticity 1–5. Decide: keep, shrink, change cue, graduate (stop tracking because it's automatic) or drop. Start a fresh grid; a new month is a natural fresh start (Dai et al. 2014).
- **Quarterly:** most habits need 2–3 months. At about 10 weeks, ask: does it feel automatic yet? If not, it may need a more stable cue, not more effort.
- **Yearly:** list graduated habits (a quiet record of change). Archive grids.

**Graduation:** when a habit feels automatic (rated 4–5 for two months), stop tracking it. Tracking forever turns the tracker into the burden.

## 12. Community practice

**What people actually do** (r/bulletjournal, r/getdisciplined, r/ADHD, YouTube "plan with me" videos):
- Draw a new monthly grid every month, often with 8–15 habits at first, trimming to 3–5 by month two.
- Colour-code habit categories (in colour journals).
- Mix binary habits (meds) with numeric ones (glasses of water, pages read).
- Use a mood or sleep "year in pixels" as a companion.
- Leave rest days as a different mark (a dash) so they don't read as misses.
- Abandon trackers mid-month when a broken week makes the grid feel "ruined", then restart at the next month.

**Common customisations:** "minimum and target" levels; weekly rather than daily habits (3×/week); tracking "no" habits (no doomscrolling) as well as "do" habits; a sticker on done days.

**Aesthetic vs minimalist camps.** Aesthetic trackers (themed illustrations, colour-by-habit) can make the page a reward; minimalist trackers are faster and don't feel ruined by a gap. A calm pre-printed grid removes set-up and keeps attention on the behaviour.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Pen:** a fine liner (0.3–0.5 mm) for neat half-fills; a slightly thicker one (0.7 mm) for full fills.
- **Paper:** 80+ gsm so filled dots don't ghost.
- **Ruler:** only if drawing your own grid.
- **Stickers:** optional reward dots.
- **Clip or ribbon:** to open straight to the grid.
- **Stamp:** a small circle stamp for hand-drawn grids.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps:** Streaks (iOS), Loop Habit Tracker (Android, open source), Habitica (gamified), Apple Health / Google Fit (auto). Most rely on reminders and streak counters.
- **E-ink:** PDF grids on reMarkable, Kindle Scribe, Boox; tap-to-fill templates exist.
- **Scanning:** Journalwright Studio's scan pipeline could read filled circles from habit grid pages; the scan-ready option on Checkboxes and Scale blocks is aimed at optical mark reading, but reading a full habit grid is **(not built)**.
- **Gained digitally:** automatic totals, charts, auto-tracking (steps, sleep), reminders.
- **Lost digitally:** apps lean on reminders, which Stawarz et al. found support repetition but can hinder habit development; streak counters and badges add pressure; phones distract. Positive reinforcement features didn't help in their 4-week study.
- **Hybrid:** paper grid for chosen habits; a wearable or device for passive data (steps, sleep), without streaks.

## 16. Evidence and research

| Study | Sample | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lally et al. 2010, *Eur J Soc Psychol* | About 96 volunteers **(unconfirmed)**, 12 weeks, one self-chosen eating, drinking or activity behaviour in a daily context | Automaticity rose in a curve that flattened. Median 66 days to 95% of plateau; range 18–254. Missing one opportunity did not materially affect the process | Classic, real-world. Self-report; the curve fit only part of the sample (**about 39 of 82, unconfirmed**); many were extrapolated beyond 84 days |
| Gardner, Lally and Wardle 2012, *Br J Gen Pract* | Review for GPs | Habit advice: repeat in the same context; small, self-chosen changes; expect about 10 weeks | Expert review |
| Singh et al. 2024, *Healthcare* | 20 studies, 2,601 people | Medians 59–66 days, means 106–154; individual range 4–335. Morning, self-chosen, simpler behaviours form faster | Only 4 studies measured duration; most high risk of bias |
| Kaushal and Rhodes 2015, *J Behav Med* | 111 new gym members, 12 weeks | At least 4 sessions a week for 6 weeks needed for an exercise habit; consistency predicted habit | Observational; one setting |
| Wood and Neal 2007, *Psychol Rev* | Theory | Habits are learned context-response links; context triggers action without a goal | Foundational |
| Wood and Rünger 2016, *Annu Rev Psychol* | Review | Habits are the efficient default; change via context | Strong review |
| Gollwitzer 1999, *Am Psychol* | Review of experiments | If-then plans automate action initiation | Foundational |
| Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006, *Adv Exp Soc Psychol* | 94 tests, 8,000+ people | Implementation intentions: d = 0.65 on goal attainment | Strong meta-analysis; possible publication bias |
| Wang, Wang and Gai 2021, *Front Psychol* | 24 effect sizes, 15,907 people | MCII (WOOP) g = 0.34; about 0.24 after bias correction | Moderate; smaller than plain effect estimates |
| Harkin et al. 2016, *Psychol Bull* | 138 studies, 19,951 people | Monitoring progress improves goal attainment; larger when physically recorded or reported | Strong meta-analysis |
| Stawarz, Cox and Blandford 2015, CHI | 4-week study; review of 115 apps | Reminders support repetition but hinder habit; event cues increase automaticity; positive reinforcement ineffective | Small study; HCI venue |
| Silverman and Barasch 2023, *J Consumer Res* | 7 studies | Highlighting intact streaks increases engagement vs broken streaks; stronger when the break is self-blamed; weaker when a break can be "repaired" | Strong design; consumer context |
| Dai, Milkman and Riis 2014, *Management Science* | Archival field data | Goal behaviours rise after temporal landmarks (new week, month, year) | Field data; correlational |

**Claimed vs supported:**
- *Supported:* context-linked repetition forms habits; it takes weeks to months with wide variation; one miss isn't a reset; if-then plans work; writing progress down helps.
- *Partly supported:* streak displays motivate while intact; broken streaks can demotivate; repair options soften this.
- *Claimed:* "never miss twice" as a rule; "21 days" (contradicted); "1% better every day" compounding (a metaphor, not data); identity-based habits; celebration wiring in habits (Fogg) **(no trial found)**.

## 17. Benefits

- Better follow-through on goals when progress is written down — **evidenced**.
- Faster start from if-then plans — **evidenced**.
- Behaviours that become automatic cost less effort — **evidenced** (theory and field data).
- Seeing patterns (days that drop off, links to sleep or shifts) — **claimed**, plausible.
- Motivation from visible progress — **evidenced** for intact streaks; **claimed** in general.
- A record to share with a doctor (meds adherence, movement) — **claimed**.
- Graduation lists as proof of change — **claimed**.

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Tracking 10+ habits | Enthusiasm | 3–5 max; add one only when another graduates |
| Quitting after a broken streak | Streak as goal; self-blame | Show totals, not streaks; half-fill for tiny versions; "next time smaller" |
| Expecting 21 days | Myth | Expect about 2–3 months, sometimes much longer |
| Cue is vague ("in the morning") | No stable context | Anchor to a specific event ("after I pour coffee") |
| Reminder-dependence | Phone alarms do the remembering | Move to an event cue; fade reminders |
| Tracking replaces doing | Marking feels like progress | Mark only after doing |
| Rigid daily goal for a non-daily habit | Wrong frequency | Weekly target (3×/week) |
| Shift work breaks the cue | Different days | Two cues: work days and off days |
| Punitive marks (red X on misses) | Habit from school grading | Leave misses empty |
| Tracking forever | No end point | Graduate at "automatic" |
| Numbers harm (food, weight) | Can feed disordered patterns | Track actions, not body numbers; skip those trackers if in recovery |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** 2–3 habits; half-fill counts; mark at the moment; totals not streaks; novelty via a fresh grid monthly. See [adhd-journaling.md](adhd-journaling.md).
- **Autism and sensory needs:** consistent layout and fixed cues suit many; add "sensory-friendly" versions of habits (e.g. outside in low-noise hours).
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** allow "rest" as a planned mark; habits have a tiny version for flare days; weekly targets over daily.
- **Depression and anxiety:** empty circles, not X's; "any" counts; review with kindness; a broken grid is not a verdict.
- **Trauma:** avoid control-heavy framing; self-chosen habits only.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** icons as habit labels; fill circles, no writing.
- **Low vision:** letter size; 5–6 mm circles; heavy grid lines every 7 days.
- **Motor or hand pain:** a single slash can count as full; half = a dot in the centre; stamps.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** HRT doses as a habit row with half for "late"; no gendered body goals.
- **Shift workers:** cues tied to the shift, not the clock; separate off-day anchors.
- **Low income:** any paper grid; free printables; no app needed.

## 21. Combining

- **Bullet Journal** ([bullet-journal.md](bullet-journal.md)): the monthly grid sits beside the monthly log.

## 23. Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Habit | A behaviour triggered automatically by a context cue, learned through repetition |
| Automaticity | Doing something with little awareness, effort or intention; the core of habit |
| Cue / anchor / trigger | The context or event that prompts the habit |
| Context stability | Same time, place or preceding event each time |
| Implementation intention | An if-then plan: "When X, I will Y" (Gollwitzer) |
| Habit stacking | Clear's name for chaining a new habit after an existing one |
| Tiny Habits recipe | "After I [anchor], I will [tiny behaviour]" (Fogg) |
| Shine / celebration | Fogg's term for a positive feeling right after the behaviour |
| B = MAP | Fogg: Behaviour = Motivation, Ability, Prompt together |
| Four laws | Clear: obvious, attractive, easy, satisfying |
| Streak / chain | Consecutive days done |
| Streak freeze | An app feature that preserves a streak through a missed day |
| Never miss twice | Clear's rule: one miss is fine, avoid two in a row |
| Half-fill | Partial credit mark |
| SRHI | Self-Report Habit Index, 12 items (Verplanken and Orbell 2003) |
| SRBAI | Self-Report Behavioural Automaticity Index, 4 items (Gardner et al. 2012) |
| Plateau / asymptote | Where automaticity levels off |
| Fresh start effect | More goal action after time landmarks (Dai et al. 2014) |
| WOOP / MCII | Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan; mental contrasting with implementation intentions |
| Graduation | Stopping tracking because a habit is automatic |
| Year in pixels | One square per day for a year |

## 24. FAQ

**How long does a habit take to form?** It varies a lot. The best-known study found a median of 66 days, with a range of 18 to 254. A 2024 review found medians of 59–66 days and individual times from 4 to 335. "21 days" isn't supported.

**If I miss a day, do I start over?** No. In Lally's study, missing one opportunity didn't materially change habit formation. Do the tiny version next time.

**Are streaks good or bad?** Both. An intact streak motivates; a highlighted broken one can reduce engagement, especially if you blame yourself. Totals and repair options (like half-fills) soften the harm.

**Should I use reminders?** Early on, maybe. But reminders can keep the habit dependent on them. Aim for an event cue ("after coffee") and fade the reminder.

**How many habits should I track?** Three to five. Clear suggests 3–4 for manual tracking.

**Is *Atomic Habits* evidence-based?** Partly. Its core ideas (cues, making it easy, tracking, habit stacking) align with research. Many examples are anecdotes, and some claims ("1% better every day", identity votes) are framing, not findings.

**Is *Tiny Habits* evidence-based?** The anchor idea matches implementation-intention research. The "celebration wires in habits" claim hasn't been tested in trials that we found.

**What should a missed day look like on paper?** Empty. No X, no red. Optionally a dash for planned rest.

**When can I stop tracking?** When it feels automatic, for example rating 4–5 on "I do it without thinking" for two months.

**Daily or weekly habits?** Match the behaviour. Exercise 3–4×/week is a real target; forcing daily can cause more misses.

### e. Don't adopt

- **Streak counters and "don't break the chain" calendars.** A broken, highlighted streak lowers engagement, and self-blame makes it worse. It's also badge-like pressure the e-ink companion's scope rules out.
- **Red X or failure marks for misses.** Empty is enough.
- **Reminders and nudges on the X4 companion.** Out of scope, and research suggests event cues form habits better than reminders.
- **Points, levels, gamification (Habitica-style).** Busy, pressuring and off-brand for calm design.
- **Body-number trackers (weight, calories) by default.** Can harm; keep them opt-in via Fill-in blanks.
- **"21 days" or "66 days" promises printed on the page.** The range is huge; a number invites a sense of failure.
- **More than 5 habit slots in the default layout.** Overtracking is the most common failure.

## 26. Open questions

- Does half-fill (partial credit) protect motivation the way "repairable" streaks did in Silverman and Barasch's studies? Untested for paper.
- Is "never miss twice" better than "next time smaller"? No trial compares them.
- Do paper trackers form habits as well as, better than, or worse than apps? Harkin found physical recording helps monitoring, but not habit formation directly.
- How much does a celebration step (Fogg) add beyond the cue? Not tested in the studies found; Stawarz found positive reinforcement ineffective in apps.
- For shift workers, does a cue tied to an event (shift end) form habits as well as a fixed daily time? Likely yes by theory; untested.
- Does monthly automaticity rating change what people track, or just add a task?
- Is the 66-day median stable across people with ADHD or depression? No subgroup data found.
