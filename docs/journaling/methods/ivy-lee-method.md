# The Ivy Lee Method

A reference for the Ivy Lee method: six tasks, ranked, done one at a time. It covers the famous Charles Schwab and Bethlehem Steel story (and how weak its sourcing is), the modern versions (1-3-5, MITs, Big 3), the research on to-do lists and plan-making, and what Journalwright Studio should take from it.

Checked against sources on 2026-09-28. Where a detail couldn't be confirmed from a primary source, it's marked **(unconfirmed)**. Where a story is probably legend, it's marked **(apocryphal)**.

---

## 1. At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Creator** | Attributed to **Ivy Ledbetter Lee** (1877–1934), a founder of modern public relations |
| **Year** | Usually dated **1918**. The earliest printed telling James Clear found is from the **1960s**; a 1972 book version is also cited |
| **Type** | A daily to-do list rule (planning practice) |
| **Time per day** | 5–15 minutes at the end of the workday |
| **Time per week** | None required |
| **Cost** | Free. Any paper, index card or app |
| **Formats and sizes** | Anything: an index card, a sticky note, a notebook line, a phone note |
| **Best for** | People who stall on long lists, decision fatigue, deep single-focus work, anyone who wants a very small system |
| **Not ideal for** | Reactive jobs (dispatch, care work, customer queues), days full of fixed appointments, people whose energy swings a lot through the day |
| **Learning curve** | Very low to start. Choosing and ranking honestly takes a few weeks |
| **Supplies** | A pen and a small card |
| **Official site** | None. No owner, no product |
| **Evidence level** | **Anecdotal** for the method as a whole. **Some studies** support its parts: writing specific plans for tomorrow, to-do lists before bed, and avoiding task switching |

## 2. Summary

At the end of each workday, write down the **six most important things** to do tomorrow, and no more than six. **Rank them** by real importance. Tomorrow, work on **only the first** until it's done, then the second, and so on. At the end of the day, move anything unfinished onto the **new list of six**. Repeat every working day. That's the whole method. It's told through a story: in 1918 Charles M. Schwab of Bethlehem Steel let Ivy Lee spend 15 minutes with each of his executives, tried the rule for three months, and then sent Lee a cheque for **$25,000**. The method is real and useful; the story is **poorly sourced**. No 1918 record has been found, the first known printed versions come decades later, and some details (Schwab as "president" in 1918, versions naming Andrew Carnegie) don't fit the historical record. The modern descendants are Leo Babauta's **MITs**, The Muse's **1-3-5 rule** and Michael Hyatt's **Daily Big 3**. Research backs the idea that writing a concrete plan for tomorrow calms the mind (Masicampo & Baumeister 2011; Scullin et al. 2018), but no study has tested "six, ranked, in order" itself.

## 3. History and origin

### Who is said to have created it

- **Ivy Ledbetter Lee** was born on 15 July 1877 near Cedartown, Georgia, the son of a Methodist minister. He studied at Emory and graduated from Princeton.
- He was a newspaper reporter, then a publicity man. In 1906 he issued what's often called the first press release (after a train wreck near Atlantic City) and a **Declaration of Principles** for honest publicity.
- In 1914, after the **Ludlow Massacre**, he was hired by John D. Rockefeller Jr. to repair the family's image.
- He did advise **Bethlehem Steel**. Wikipedia's article says he advised its executives to "list and number their top priorities every day, and work on tasks in the order of their importance" (citing secondary sources).
- **The dark part.** From the late 1920s he worked for the German chemical company **I.G. Farben**. He met Hitler in January 1934 and advised on Nazi Germany's image. Congress investigated him in 1934. He died on 8 November 1934. (Fortune, 2022, drawing on Scott Cutlip's history of PR.)

So the man was a PR pioneer, not a productivity consultant, and part of his career was spent polishing the image of a regime. That doesn't change whether the list works; it's context Journalwright Studio should know before printing his name.

### The Schwab story, and how well it's sourced

**The usual telling (James Clear's version):** In 1918 Charles M. Schwab, "president of Bethlehem Steel", wanted his team to be more efficient. Ivy Lee asked for 15 minutes with each executive and explained the six-task rule. Asked his fee, Lee said to pay what it was worth after three months. After three months Schwab sent a cheque for $25,000 (about $400,000 in 2015 money).

**Problems with it:**

| Issue | What the record says |
|---|---|
| No contemporary source | No 1918 letter, cheque, memo or newspaper report has been published. James Clear, who popularised the modern version, says the earliest reference he could find "is from the 1960s" and asks readers for anything earlier |
| First book trace | An Ask MetaFilter thread (2005) traces the story to R. Alec Mackenzie's *The Time Trap* (1972) and notes Mackenzie "misidentified Lee's relationship to the company" **(unconfirmed; secondary)** |
| The 1918 date | Clear dates the meeting to 1918 from Scott Cutlip's *The Unseen Power: Public Relations, A History* (pp. 118–119). Cutlip is a serious PR historian, but the page is cited second-hand here; the book wasn't checked directly **(unconfirmed)** |
| Schwab's job | **Eugene Grace** was president of Bethlehem Steel from **1916**; Schwab was chairman. In **April 1918** Schwab became Director General of the Emergency Fleet Corporation for the war shipbuilding effort. "President of Bethlehem Steel in 1918" is wrong, and 1918 was an unusually busy year for him |
| Carnegie version | Some tellings (including the one attributed to radio speaker Earl Nightingale) name **Andrew Carnegie**. Carnegie never ran Bethlehem Steel; he sold his steel business in 1901. That version is wrong **(Nightingale attribution unconfirmed)** |
| The money | $25,000 appears in both the productivity story and in the fee Lee was later paid by I.G. Farben (Fortune reports a $25,000 fee plus a $4,000 retainer). This may be coincidence; it's a reason for caution, not proof |
| The six | Wikipedia's summary of Lee's advice says "list and number their top priorities", with no fixed six. The "six" may be a later detail **(unconfirmed)** |

**Verdict.** Lee advising Bethlehem Steel executives to rank their priorities is plausible and widely repeated. The **$25,000 cheque, the "15 minutes per executive", the three-month trial and the number six** are **unverified**, and the story's details shift between versions. Treat it as a **parable**, like the equally unsourced "Warren Buffett 25/5 two lists" story. The method stands on its own.

### Cultural context

- **Scientific management era.** 1910s America was full of efficiency experts (Frederick Taylor, the Gilbreths). A consultant selling a one-page efficiency trick to a steel magnate fits the mood, which may be why the story sticks.
- **1960s–70s time management.** Books like Mackenzie's and seminars by speakers such as Earl Nightingale told short, memorable business parables. Precision about sources wasn't the point.
- **2010s productivity blogging.** The story came back in listicles and blog posts, often with the 1918 date and the $25,000 sum, usually without the sourcing caveat.

### Key quotes

There are **no reliable quotes from Lee** about the method. The steps as usually quoted (from James Clear):

1. "At the end of each work day, write down the six most important things you need to accomplish tomorrow. Do not write down more than six tasks."
2. "Prioritize those six items in order of their true importance."
3. "When you arrive tomorrow, concentrate only on the first task. Work until the first task is finished before moving on to the second task."
4. "Approach the rest of your list in the same fashion. At the end of the day, move any unfinished items to a new list of six tasks for the following day."
5. "Repeat this process every working day."

Schwab's supposed letter calling it the most valuable lesson "from a money standpoint" he had ever learned is part of the unverified story.

## 4. Philosophy and principles

**The problem it solves:** starting the day not knowing what to do first, jumping between tasks, and long lists that never shrink.

**Core principles (as modern writers explain it):**

1. **A hard limit.** Six forces you to drop things. "Too many" is the enemy, not "too few".
2. **Decide the night before.** You choose calmly, not in the morning rush, and the list is waiting when you arrive.
3. **Rank, then obey the rank.** The choice of what to do next is made once, not 20 times a day.
4. **Single-tasking.** Do the first until it's done. No switching.
5. **Carry-over without drama.** Unfinished items simply go on tomorrow's list, re-ranked.
6. **Every working day.** It's a habit, not a project.

**Clear's reasons it works:** it's simple enough to use, it forces tough decisions, it removes the friction of starting, and it makes you single-task.

**What it doesn't do:** it has no goals layer, no weekly review, no calendar and no place for notes or feelings. It assumes you control your day.

## 5. Core components

| Element | What it is | When |
|---|---|---|
| The list of six | Up to six tasks for tomorrow | End of workday |
| Ranking | Number 1–6 by true importance | End of workday |
| First-task focus | Work only on #1 until done | Start of day |
| Sequential work | Then #2, #3 … | Through the day |
| Carry-over | Unfinished items move to the new list | End of workday |
| Repetition | Every working day | Daily |

### 5.1 The list of six

- **Six is a ceiling, not a target.** Fewer is fine.
- Write **tasks**, not projects: "draft the intro" rather than "write report".
- Only things that matter. Routine chores and fixed appointments sit outside the list (or become one line: "admin hour").

### 5.2 Ranking

- "True importance" means consequence, not urgency or ease. Research on the **mere urgency effect** (Zhu, Yang & Hsee 2018) shows people drift toward urgent-looking, lower-value tasks, so ranking by importance on purpose matters.
- A quick test: "If I only get one done, which one?" That's #1. Repeat for the rest.

### 5.3 First-task focus

- Start on #1 before email, if you can.
- Don't touch #2 until #1 is done. If #1 is blocked (waiting on someone), note why and move to #2.

### 5.4 Sequential work

- Go down the list in order. Don't cherry-pick the easy ones.
- Interruptions still happen; return to the current number after them.

### 5.5 Carry-over

- At day's end, unfinished items go on tomorrow's list, **re-ranked** with new items.
- If something carries over three or more days, it's either too big (split it) or not really important (drop it).

### 5.6 Repetition

- The method's power is in doing it daily for weeks. One list changes little.

## 6. Setting it up

### First setup (5 minutes)

1. Get a pack of index cards or a small notebook. (A 3 × 5 in card fits six lines comfortably.)
2. Decide your **end-of-work cue**: last bus stop, clocking out, closing the laptop.
3. Put the card where you'll see it first thing: in your wallet, on your bag, by the kettle.
4. Optional: write a "someday" list on another card so things you drop have somewhere to go.

### First day

1. At the end of work, brain-dump everything on your mind onto scrap paper.
2. Pick up to **six** that matter for tomorrow.
3. Rank them 1–6.
4. Copy them onto the card in order.
5. Stop thinking about work.

### First week

- **Day 1–2:** expect to rank badly. That's fine.
- **Day 3:** notice what carried over. Was it too big? Split it.
- **Day 4:** try writing the list before leaving work, not at home.
- **Day 5:** look at the week's five cards. How often did #1 get done? That's the only number that matters early on.
- **Weekend:** no list, unless you like one.

## 7. Daily practice

| When | Step | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Start of day | Look at the card. Begin #1 | 1 min |
| Morning | Work on #1 until done | — |
| Rest of day | #2, #3 … in order; cross each off | — |
| End of workday | Strike the done. Carry the rest. Add new. Pick ≤ 6. Rank. Write the new card | 5–15 min |
| Evening | Nothing. The plan is made; let it go | — |

**Prompts for the end-of-day list:**

- What's the one thing that would make tomorrow count?
- What did I carry over, and is it still important?
- What's waiting on me that blocks someone else?
- What can I drop, delegate or put on "someday"?
- Is each item small enough to finish in one sitting?

**Why evening, not morning?** The method says end of day. Research on plan-making fits: making a specific plan reduced intrusive thoughts about unfinished tasks (Masicampo & Baumeister 2011), and writing a to-do list at bedtime helped people fall asleep faster (Scullin et al. 2018). Morning planning also works; the evening version just lets you close the day.

## 10. Worked examples

A generic example, for someone who works outdoor shifts and has a few life-admin goals.

### A day

List written Monday at 4:45 p.m., at the bus stop after shift:

```
TUE 13 OCT
1 [x] Call pharmacy: refill before Friday
2 [x] Email landlord about the heater (photos attached)
3 [/] Fill out the benefits form, pages 1–3   → blocked: need pay stub
4 [x] 20-minute walk before shift
5 [ ] Sort the donate bag
6 [ ] Text Jo about Saturday
```

End of Tuesday:

- 1, 2, 4 done. 3 blocked (pay stub is at work). 5 and 6 not reached.
- New list for Wednesday: pay stub first so 3 can move.

```
WED 14 OCT
1 [ ] Get pay stub from office at start of shift
2 [ ] Benefits form pages 1–3        (carried ×1)
3 [ ] Text Jo about Saturday         (carried ×1)
4 [ ] Walk 20 min
5 [ ] Sort the donate bag            (carried ×1)
```

(Five, not six: a long shift day.)

### A week

```
       #1 done?   carried items   notes
MON    yes        2               long shift
TUE    yes        3               form blocked
WED    yes        1
THU    no         2               sick; list was "rest, meds, soup"
FRI    yes        0               cleared the donate bag at last
Done #1: 4 of 5 days. "Donate bag" carried 3 days → should have been split or dropped.
```

### A month

The Ivy Lee method has no monthly layer. A useful add-on is a one-line monthly note:

```
OCTOBER
- #1 done on 17 of 21 workdays
- Things that kept carrying: paperwork (needs a set "admin hour" line)
- Best cue for writing the list: at the bus stop after shift
- Next month: max 5 on long-shift days; 3 on sick days
```

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | By | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| **Ivy Lee (classic)** | Attributed to Ivy Lee; modern wording by James Clear | Six, ranked, sequential, end of day |
| **MITs (Most Important Tasks)** | Leo Babauta, Zen Habits | **Three** MITs; do them "first thing in the morning"; at least one "must be a goal next-action." Babauta says the idea isn't original |
| **1-3-5 rule** | Alexandra Cavoulacos, The Muse; in *The New Rules of Work* with Kathryn Minshew | Nine items **by size**: 1 big, 3 medium, 5 small. Plan the evening before. Shrink to 1-2-3 on heavy days |
| **Daily Big 3** | Michael Hyatt, Full Focus Planner | Three outcomes linked to weekly and quarterly goals; a separate "other tasks" list |
| **"Eat the frog"** | Brian Tracy (book, early 2000s), from a line attributed to Mark Twain | Do the hardest, most important task first. No list limit **(details not checked here)** |
| **The ONE Thing** | Gary Keller & Jay Papasan | One task per day, the one that makes everything else easier |
| **Buffett's "25/5" two lists** | Popular story, **apocryphal** | List 25 goals, circle 5, avoid the other 20 at all costs. No primary source; reported as not true |
| **Workday shutdown ritual** | Cal Newport (2009) | End-of-day review of all tasks and calendar, then a set phrase: "Schedule shutdown, complete" |
| **Time-blocked Ivy Lee** | Common community hybrid | Each of the six gets a time slot on the calendar |
| **Ivy Lee + "someday" card** | Common community hybrid | Dropped items go to a parking list, not the bin |

## 12. Community practice

- **Where it's discussed:** productivity blogs, Medium posts, Reddit threads on to-do methods, and YouTube explainers. Almost all repeat James Clear's steps.
- **What people actually do:**
  - Use **fewer than six**, often three.
  - Keep the ranking but **batch** small tasks into one line ("admin: 3 emails, 1 call").
  - Write the list **in the morning** instead of the evening.
  - Keep a separate master list; the six are a daily slice of it.
  - Put the list on a sticky note on the laptop or phone case.
- **Common customisation:** a "carried ×n" count, a blocked marker, time estimates, and a gentler rule on sick days.
- **Minimalist vs aesthetic:** almost entirely minimalist. There's no Ivy Lee spread culture. Aesthetic users fold it into a bullet journal or planner as a "top 6" box.
- **Criticism in the community:** too rigid for reactive jobs; the story is suspect; six is arbitrary.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps:** any to-do app with manual ordering (Todoist, Things, Microsoft To Do, Apple Reminders). Make a "Tomorrow" list, cap at six, drag to rank. Some apps sell "Ivy Lee" or "1-3-5" modes.
- **Calendar:** put #1 as a morning block.
- **E-ink:** a six-line template on reMarkable, Kindle Scribe or Boox works well; the list is short enough for a small screen. A daily template with six numbered lines is the simplest possible e-ink page.
- **Scanning:** not needed. A photo of the card is a record if you want one.
- **Lost going digital:** the physical limit (apps let you add a seventh item), the calm of a card with no notifications, and the "close the day" feeling of writing it by hand.
- **Gained:** automatic carry-over, reminders, sharing with a team.

## 16. Evidence and research

No study has tested "six ranked tasks done in order" against another method. The evidence below supports **parts** of it.

| Question | Study | Sample and finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does making a plan for unfinished tasks quiet the mind? | **Masicampo & Baumeister 2011**, *JPSP* 101, 667–683 | Five studies of undergraduates (Study 1: 73, analysed 69; Study 2: 68; another: 126). Thinking about two unfinished tasks caused intrusive thoughts and worse reading and anagram performance. Writing a specific plan (how, when, where) removed the effect. Those who later carried out their plans showed the least interference | **Moderate.** Controlled lab studies, but students, short time frames, single labs. Supports "decide tomorrow's tasks and stop thinking about them" |
| Does a to-do list before bed help sleep? | **Scullin, Krueger, Ballard, Pruett & Bliwise 2018**, *J. Exp. Psych.: General* 147(1), 139–146 | 57 healthy adults aged 18–30 in a sleep lab. Five minutes writing a to-do list led to falling asleep faster than writing about completed tasks. More specific lists were linked to faster sleep | **Low–moderate.** Small, one night, healthy young adults. The authors call for larger studies and people with insomnia, anxiety or depression |
| Do concrete if-then plans help people act? | **Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006**, *Advances in Exp. Soc. Psych.* 38, 69–119 | Meta-analysis of 94 tests. Implementation intentions ("when X, I'll do Y") improved goal attainment, d = .65 | **Fairly strong.** A ranked list is a weaker plan than an if-then; adding a time or cue ("#1 at 8 a.m.") makes it closer |
| Do unfinished tasks stick in memory (Zeigarnik effect)? | **Zeigarnik 1927**; **Ghibellini & Meier 2025** meta-analysis, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* | The meta-analysis found **no general memory advantage** for unfinished tasks, but a reliable tendency to **resume** them (Ovsiankina effect) | **Moderate.** Undercuts the popular "open loops nag your memory" claim; supports "unfinished things pull at you to go back" |
| Do people over-promise how much they'll get done? | **Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994**, *JPSP* 67, 366–381 | Thesis students predicted 33.9 days on average; took 55.5. About 30% finished by their prediction | **Strong, often replicated (planning fallacy).** Supports a hard cap on daily tasks |
| Do we pick urgent over important? | **Zhu, Yang & Hsee 2018**, *J. Consumer Research* 45(3), 673–690 | Five experiments: people chose urgent-looking tasks with lower pay-offs | **Moderate.** Supports ranking by importance on purpose |
| Does switching tasks cost us? | **Leroy 2009**, *Org. Behavior & Human Decision Processes* 109(2), 168–181 | Two experiments: attention stays stuck on the previous task ("attention residue") and hurts performance on the next; finishing under time pressure helped people let go | **Moderate.** Supports doing one task at a time and finishing before switching |

**Claimed versus supported:**

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Planning tomorrow the night before helps you let go | **Supported in part** (Masicampo 2011; Scullin 2018) |
| A short list is better than a long one | **Plausible**; supported indirectly by the planning fallacy. No direct test |
| Six is the right number | **Unsupported.** Arbitrary; three and nine are just as common |
| Doing tasks in strict order boosts output | **Supported in part** (task-switching costs), untested as a rule |
| It made Bethlehem Steel more productive | **Unverified** (see History) |
| Unfinished tasks nag your memory, so writing them down helps | **Mixed.** The memory part doesn't replicate; the plan-making part does |

## 17. Benefits

- Know exactly what to do first. **Evidenced in part** (plan-making; urgency bias).
- Less evening work-rumination. **Evidenced in part** (Masicampo 2011; Scullin 2018).
- Less task switching. **Evidenced in part** (Leroy 2009).
- Realistic daily load. **Claimed**, consistent with the planning fallacy.
- Almost no setup and no cost. **Obvious.**
- Builds the skill of choosing. **Claimed.**
- A daily win when #1 is done. **Claimed.**

## 18. Pitfalls

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Six huge projects | Write the next step, sized to one sitting |
| Reactive job blows up the order | Rank anyway; after interruptions, return to the current number. On reactive days, use three |
| Always doing #1 but never #2–6 | That's fine. If it keeps happening, lower the cap |
| Same item carried for days | After 3 carries: split it, schedule it or drop it |
| Forgetting to write the list | Tie it to a fixed cue (clocking out, the bus stop) |
| Writing it in bed makes you anxious | Write it before leaving work, then close the card |
| Fixed appointments eat the day | Put them on the calendar, not the list; list only what fits around them |
| Low-energy or sick days | "Sick-day list": 1–3 care items. That still counts |
| Rigid order feels punishing | Allow one swap per day if #1 is blocked or energy is wrong |
| Perfectionism about ranking | Rank in 30 seconds. "Good enough" beats "right" |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** a strong fit for "what do I do now?" paralysis. Make #1 tiny and concrete. Add a time estimate and a start cue. Allow "interest-based" swaps. Use a sticky note in sight. Fewer than six helps.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** the fixed order and night-before decision reduce morning uncertainty. Keep the card format the same every day. Write a "what to do if #1 is blocked" rule in advance.
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** number by energy as well as importance. Put a spoon cost by each item. Cap at 3 on flare days. Let rest be #1.
- **Depression:** keep the list very small; count one done as a win. Avoid tallying undone items.
- **Anxiety:** write the list at the end of work, then physically put it away. The Scullin and Masicampo results suggest it helps with letting go, but test whether evening planning helps or hurts you.
- **Trauma:** avoid framing unfinished items as failure; "moved" not "missed".
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** one or two words per task; icons; dictate into a phone and copy the top three.
- **Low vision:** big card, thick marker, three lines not six.
- **Motor or hand pain:** pre-printed numbers and boxes; tick rather than write; a digital list with drag-to-rank.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** the list can hold transition admin (name-change forms, HRT refills, appointments) quietly, without labels. Keep the card private if needed.
- **Shift workers:** "end of workday" becomes "end of shift". On days off, skip it or use a home list. For nights, write the list before sleeping after the shift.
- **Low income:** free. Scrap paper works.

## 21. Combining

- **With the Full Focus Planner** ([full-focus-planner.md](full-focus-planner.md)): the Daily Big 3 is Ivy Lee with three slots. Use the "other tasks" list as Ivy Lee's #4–6, in order.
- **With the Bullet Journal** ([bullet-journal.md](bullet-journal.md)): at the end of the daily log, number up to six open tasks for tomorrow. Migration and carry-over are the same move.
- **With the Hobonichi Techo** ([hobonichi-techo.md](hobonichi-techo.md)) or **Jibun Techo** ([jibun-techo.md](jibun-techo.md)): write the six in the top margin of tomorrow's page.
- **With a five-year diary** ([five-year-diary.md](five-year-diary.md)): write "#1 done?" in the one-line entry; over years it shows patterns.
- **With time blocking:** give #1 the first protected block.
- **With a care check-in:** decide the list size from energy (spoons) first, then choose tasks.

## 23. Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **1-3-5 rule** | One big, three medium, five small tasks a day (The Muse) |
| **Attention residue** | Leftover attention on a previous task that hurts performance on the next (Leroy 2009) |
| **Big 3** | Michael Hyatt's three daily outcomes |
| **Carry-over** | Moving an unfinished task to tomorrow's list |
| **Eat the frog** | Do the hardest important task first (Brian Tracy) |
| **Implementation intention** | A plan in the form "when situation X, I'll do Y" (Gollwitzer) |
| **Ivy Lee method** | Six tasks, ranked, done in order, planned the evening before |
| **Mere urgency effect** | Tendency to choose urgent-looking tasks over more valuable ones (Zhu et al. 2018) |
| **MIT** | Most Important Task (Leo Babauta) |
| **Ovsiankina effect** | The urge to resume an interrupted task |
| **Planning fallacy** | Underestimating how long tasks take (Kahneman & Tversky; Buehler et al.) |
| **Plan-making** | Writing how, when and where a task will be done |
| **Shutdown ritual** | An end-of-work routine that closes the day (Newport; Hyatt) |
| **Single-tasking** | Working on one task until it's done or blocked |
| **Someday list** | A parking list for dropped or later items |
| **Zeigarnik effect** | The claim that unfinished tasks are remembered better than finished ones; not supported by a 2025 meta-analysis |

## 24. FAQ

1. **Why six?** No one knows. It's the number in the story. Three (MITs, Big 3) and nine (1-3-5) are just as common. Choose the number you can actually finish most days.
2. **Is the Schwab story true?** Unverified. The first known printed versions are decades later, the details vary, and Schwab wasn't Bethlehem Steel's president in 1918. Lee did work for Bethlehem, so a real kernel is possible.
3. **Evening or morning?** The method says evening. Evidence on plan-making and bedtime lists leans evening. If evening planning keeps you up, plan at the end of work, not in bed.
4. **What about meetings?** They live on your calendar. The list is what you do in the gaps.
5. **What if #1 is blocked?** Note why, do what unblocks it (an email, a call), then move to #2.
6. **Can I reorder during the day?** The strict version says no. A gentle version allows one swap for a real reason (blocked, energy).
7. **What if I finish all six?** Stop, or pull the next item from your master list. Many people say finishing early is itself a good sign the list was right-sized.
8. **Does it work for home life?** Yes, for chores and admin. For caregiving or reactive days, use a shorter list.
9. **Isn't this just a to-do list?** It's a to-do list with three extra rules: a cap, a rank and a strict order.
10. **Should I worry about Ivy Lee's history?** It's worth knowing: he later did PR for I.G. Farben and Nazi Germany. You can use the method without honouring the man; Journalwright Studio can call it "Six, in order".

### d. Proposed method layout: "Six in order" (Ivy Lee)

This layout is not among the built-in method layouts yet (today: original, Bullet Journal daily, Hobonichi, Five Minute, Theme System). Ordered blocks for one click:

1. `sky` (Moon, sun and season): small date anchor
2. `events` (Events)
3. `checks` "First thing": ["#1 done"]
4. `timeline` or `shift` (Work shift), depending on the day type
5. `body` (Writing space, lines): takes the rest
6. `top` "Tomorrow, in order": n 6, carried column on, printed at the bottom so it's the last thing written

Care check-in and Spoons stay optional, and pair well: decide list size from spoons.

### e. Don't adopt

- **The $25,000 story as fact.** It's unverified; if told at all, say so.
- **Ivy Lee's name as the block label.** He later did PR for Nazi Germany. Use "Six, in order" or "Tomorrow, in order".
- **Strict "no reordering ever".** Too rigid for shift work and variable energy. Allow a swap.
- **A fixed six.** Let the number vary (1–6), with 3 as the default.
- **Counts of undone tasks or carry streaks on the X4.** Pressure; drifts toward badges.
- **Countdown or productivity scores.** Against calm design.

## 26. Open questions

- **The earliest printed version of the story.** Clear found the 1960s; MetaFilter points to 1972. The exact first source (book, article, speech) wasn't found.
- **What Cutlip actually says** on pp. 118–119 of *The Unseen Power* about Lee and Schwab, and what his source was.
- **Whether "six" is original** or a later detail.
- **Earl Nightingale's role.** Several blogs say he popularised it, some with Carnegie. No recording or transcript was checked.
- **Direct tests.** No study compares a capped, ranked list with an open list over weeks, or checks the best number of items.
- **Evening versus morning planning** for anxious people: Scullin's sample was healthy young adults.
- **The Zeigarnik question.** If the memory effect doesn't hold, which part of "open loops" actually drives the benefit of plan-making? Masicampo's intrusion measures still stand, but they're from one lab.
