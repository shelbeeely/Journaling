# ADHD Journaling and Planning

A reference for planning and journaling with adult ADHD: the science under it (Barkley's executive function model, time perception, CBT trials), the practical tools people actually use (brain dumps, done lists, visual timers, time estimates, body doubling), why systems get dropped, and what a printed journal with an e-ink companion can take from it.

Checked against sources on 2026-09-28. Where a detail couldn't be confirmed from a primary source, it's marked **(unconfirmed)**. Where a claim is popular but not tested, it's marked **(claimed)**.

This isn't one branded method. It's a cluster of clinical techniques and community practice with a shared goal: **put the plan, the time and the motivation outside your head, where you can see them.**

## 2. Summary

ADHD journaling is planning built for a brain that struggles to hold the future in mind. Barkley's model treats ADHD as a problem of self-regulation and executive function, which makes it a disorder of **doing what you know**, not of knowing what to do. The fix he recommends is to **externalise** at the "point of performance": put information, time and motivation in physical form where the task happens. Clinical programmes (Safren's CBT, Solanto's meta-cognitive therapy) teach exactly this with a calendar, a task list and a daily planner routine, and they beat active control treatments in randomised trials. Around that core, the ADHD community has built tools: brain dumps, done lists, visual timers, guessing time and then checking it, body doubling, and ways past the "Wall of Awful". These are practical and widely loved, but mostly untested. The biggest failure mode isn't the wrong layout. It's shame after a gap, which is why the best ADHD systems are **forgiving by design**: undated, small, easy to restart, and never scoring you.

## 3. History and origin

### Who and their background

- **Russell A. Barkley**, clinical neuropsychologist. His 1997 *Psychological Bulletin* paper, "Behavioral inhibition, sustained attention, and executive functions: constructing a unifying theory of ADHD", proposed that a core deficit in behavioural inhibition disrupts four executive functions: nonverbal working memory, verbal working memory (internalised speech), self-regulation of affect and motivation, and reconstitution (planning and problem-solving).
- **Steven A. Safren**, clinical psychologist at Massachusetts General Hospital and Harvard Medical School when the trials ran. Developed a modular CBT for adults already on medication who still had symptoms.
- **Mary V. Solanto**, clinical psychologist (Mount Sinai at the time of the trial, **unconfirmed**). Developed group "meta-cognitive therapy" (MCT) targeting time management, organisation and planning.
- **Brendan Mahan**, M.Ed., M.S., ADHD coach and host of the *ADHD Essentials* podcast. Created the "Wall of Awful" metaphor.
- **William Dodson**, M.D., psychiatrist. Coined the "interest-based nervous system" in writing for *ADDitude*.
- **Jan Rogers** invented the Time Timer visual timer for her young daughter.

### Cultural context

Adult ADHD moved from "a childhood disorder you grow out of" to a recognised adult condition over the 2000s. Clinical programmes arrived first, then a large peer community online. Paper planners had a revival in the same years (Bullet Journal from 2013), and many ADHD adults found that the flexible, undated, "build as you go" planners suited them better than dated corporate diaries. The community also pushed back against a deficit-only framing, favouring language like "executive dysfunction", "spoons" and "neurodivergent".

### Key quotes

- Barkley: executive function deficits "create disorders mainly of performance rather than of knowledge or skills."
- Barkley: they "are to time what nearsightedness is to spatial vision."
- Barkley: people with these deficits "will be best assisted by 'externalizing' those forms of information."
- Mahan (as summarised on *Hacking Your ADHD*): "It doesn't matter if these bricks are based in reality, it matters how you perceive them."
- Dodson: focus is switched on by "a fleeting sense of: Interest, Competition, Novelty, Urgency."

## 4. Philosophy and principles

**The problem it solves.** You know what to do. You can't make yourself do it at the right time, or you lose track of time while doing something else. The plan lives in your head and evaporates.

**Core beliefs (clinical):**
1. **Externalise information.** If working memory is unreliable, write it down and put it where you'll see it when you need it (Barkley).
2. **Externalise time.** Make time visible: timers, clocks, time blocks, estimates written next to tasks.
3. **Externalise motivation.** Add small, immediate rewards and progress signals because distant rewards don't pull (Barkley; Sonuga-Barke's delay aversion).
4. **Act at the point of performance.** Help belongs where and when the task happens, not in a weekly talk. A planner you don't open at the right moment does nothing.
5. **One system, checked daily.** CBT programmes insist on a single calendar and a single task list, looked at every day (Safren; CHADD).
6. **Break it down.** Large tasks become steps small enough to start.

**Core beliefs (community):**
7. **Start is the hard part.** Task initiation, not task completion, is where most days fail. Tools target the first two minutes.
8. **Shame makes it worse.** Every failure adds a brick to the Wall of Awful. Systems must not scold.
9. **Novelty is fuel.** Systems wear out. Rotating tools is normal, not failure **(claimed)**.
10. **Track what you did, not only what you didn't.** A done list counters the "I did nothing today" story.

### Single capture place
Safren's organisation module spends four of twelve sessions on a calendar and task list system. CHADD's planner advice: carry it always, keep it in one home spot, plan daily, and check it at least three times a day. The point is that there's only one place to look.

### Brain dump
Write every open loop down, fast, without sorting. Masicampo and Baumeister (2011) found that making a specific plan for an unfinished goal removed its intrusive effect on unrelated tasks. Scullin et al. (2018) found that writing a specific to-do list before bed helped people fall asleep about 9 minutes faster than writing about completed tasks. Note the dump alone isn't the plan: the benefit in Masicampo's work came from **plan-making**, so a dump works best when followed by one "next step" line.

### Top priorities
Pick 1–3 from the dump. Safren's programme uses A/B/C priority ratings on the task list **(details from the client workbook; unconfirmed here)**. Keeping the number tiny protects against the overplanned day.

### Time estimate vs actual
Write a guess beside a task, then write what it really took. Over weeks you learn your own multiplier ("I'm about 1.5× slow on email, 3× on laundry"). Time perception deficits are documented in children and adolescents with ADHD (Zheng et al. 2022: 27 studies, 2,869 participants, less accurate and less precise timing, with a tendency to overestimate). Evidence for adults and for this particular exercise is thinner **(no trial of the estimate-vs-actual page found)**.

### Visual timer and time bubbles
A timer that shows time as a shrinking disc (Time Timer) or a row of 15-minute circles to fill makes time something you can see. This is Barkley's "make time itself more externally represented", put into an object.

### Time blocks
Hours down the side, tasks placed into them. Useful for shift days. Risk: over-scheduling, then feeling you've failed when the plan slips.

### First tiny step
Write the smallest physical action ("open laptop", "put shoes by door"). This is an if-then plan in miniature: "When I finish coffee, I'll open the laptop." Implementation intentions have a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment across 94 tests (Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006). See [habit-tracking.md](habit-tracking.md).

### Distraction parking lot
Safren's distractibility module teaches a "distractibility delay": when a stray thought hits, jot it on a pad and go back to the task, then deal with the list later **(technique name from the treatment manual, as commonly described; unconfirmed here)**. On paper it's a small box labelled "Later".

### Done list
At the end of the day, list what you actually did, including unplanned things. It counters "I did nothing". Amabile and Kramer's diary research on knowledge workers found that making progress in meaningful work was the strongest day-to-day booster of inner work life ("the progress principle"). That was workers, not ADHD, and not a done-list trial. Note one counterweight: in Scullin's sleep study the completed-task list was the control and people fell asleep slower than with a to-do list. So a done list may suit the end of the workday more than the moment before bed **(inference)**.

### Body doubling
Working with another person present, in the room or on video, who isn't doing your task. Eagle et al. (2024) surveyed 220 people (about 88% neurodivergent) and found people use it for motivation, task initiation and completion, often for chores. They describe two axes: same time/place to different time/place, and ambient companionship to active accountability. It's a self-selected survey, so it tells us how and why people use it, not whether it works.

### Wall of Awful check
Mahan's model: repeated failure, disappointment, rejection and shame stack up as emotional bricks in front of a task. He names five approaches: the Stare, Going Around, the Hulk Smash (powering through at a cost), Climbing (sitting with the emotion and understanding it) and Putting a Door In (changing your emotional state, e.g. a walk or music, which can slide into procrastination). On paper this becomes one prompt: "What's the brick?"

### Energy, spoons and meds
A capacity check before planning. Journalwright Studio already has a Care check-in block and a Spoons block. Tie planning to capacity: low spoons, one priority.

### Weekly reset
A short look-back and a clean restart. CBT programmes review the week's planner use each session. The community version is "no catch-up": you don't fill in missed days.

### Novelty rotation
Many ADHD adults report a planner working for 2–6 weeks, then going stale. The community response is to rotate on purpose: new pen, new layout, new block order, a fresh notebook at the month. This is backed by lived experience, not trials **(claimed)**. The fresh start effect (Dai, Milkman and Riis 2014) suggests month and week boundaries are natural restart points.

## 6. Setting it up

### First setup (about 20 minutes, can be split)
1. **Pick one home for it.** One book, one pen tied to it, one spot where it lives (by the keys or the kettle).
2. **Decide your check times.** Three anchors: after the first drink of the morning, after lunch or mid-shift, and before you leave work or start the evening.
3. **Set the page up small.** Top 3, a brain dump area, a time row, a done list. Nothing else yet.
4. **Get a timer.** A visual timer, or a phone timer set to 15 minutes.
5. **Write a first-page note to yourself:** "Gaps are normal. Start on today's page. Don't back-fill."
6. **Choose a restart ritual.** For example: "If I've missed days, I do a 2-minute brain dump and pick one thing."

### First day
- Morning (3 min): brain dump everything, circle up to 3, write a first tiny step for #1.
- Midday (1 min): glance, tick, move one thing if needed.
- Evening (2 min): done list, one line for tomorrow.

### First week
- Day 1–2: just the morning dump and evening done list.
- Day 3–4: add guesses beside tasks. Don't fill actuals yet.
- Day 5–6: add actuals for one or two tasks.
- Day 7: weekly look (10 min): what got done, what kept moving forward undone, one thing to drop.
- Expect to miss at least one day. On the next day, start fresh.

## 7. Daily practice

| When | Time | Steps | Prompts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wake / first drink | 1 min | Check energy, meds, sleep | "Spoons today?" "Meds taken?" |
| Morning plan | 2–5 min | Brain dump → circle up to 3 → first tiny step → estimate time | "What's rattling around?" "Which 3 matter today?" "What's the first 2-minute move?" |
| Start of a task | 30 s | Start the timer, write the start time | "Guess: __ min" |
| During | as needed | Park stray thoughts in "Later" | "Later, not now" |
| End of a task | 15 s | Write the actual time, tick | "Took: __ min" |
| Midday | 1 min | Glance, re-choose if the day changed | "Still the right 3?" |
| End of work | 2 min | Done list, carry over | "What did I actually do?" |
| Evening | 1 min | One line for tomorrow's first step | "Tomorrow starts with..." |

**Order matters.** Capacity first, then the plan. Planning 8 tasks on a 3-spoon day sets up failure.

**Bad-day version (30 seconds):** one word for energy, one thing to do, one thing done.

## 8. Rhythms

- **Weekly (10–20 min):** read the done lists; list what kept moving forward undone ("carried 3+ times" = drop, shrink or ask for help); check estimate multipliers; pick one experiment for next week.
- **Monthly (20–30 min):** a fresh start. Look at which blocks you actually used and remove the rest. Rotate one thing for novelty. Note meds or schedule changes.
- **Quarterly:** look at patterns: shift weeks vs off weeks, seasonal energy, which tools survived. Check in with a prescriber or therapist if you have one.
- **Yearly:** keep the done lists. They're the evidence against "I never get anything done". Archive the rest.
- **Re-entry after a gap (any time):** no back-filling. Today's page, a 2-minute brain dump, one thing. Optionally write "Back" and the date. That's it.

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Who | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| CBT planner system | Safren et al. (MGH), 2005 | Calendar plus task list with priority ratings, checked daily, taught over 4 sessions, plus distractibility and adaptive thinking modules |
| Meta-cognitive therapy (MCT) | Solanto et al., 2010 | Group programme (12 weekly sessions) on time management, organisation and planning with a planner **(session details from memory, unconfirmed)** |
| Bullet Journal for ADHD | Ryder Carroll, who has ADHD; widely adapted | Rapid logging and migration. See [bullet-journal.md](bullet-journal.md) |
| Emergent Task Planner | David Seah | Time bubbles and a time column. Named by ADDitude readers as "external scaffolding". See [emergent-task-planner.md](emergent-task-planner.md) |
| Time-block planning | Cal Newport and others | Every hour assigned. See [time-block-planner.md](time-block-planner.md) |
| Visual timeline apps | Tiimo (Copenhagen) | Colour timeline, focus timer, routines. Now includes AI task breakdown |
| Body doubling platforms | Focusmate and others | Booked 25/50/75-minute video sessions with a stranger |
| Done list / "ta-da list" | Community | Only records what you did. No to-do list |
| "One thing" planning | Community | A single priority per day, for low-spoon days |
| Dopamine menu | Community (widely credited to ADHD creators on social media, **origin unconfirmed**) | A list of quick, medium and long rewards to pick from, like a restaurant menu |
| Pomodoro for ADHD | Francesco Cirillo's technique, adapted | 25 on, 5 off. ADHD adaptations shorten to 10–15 minutes or lengthen to ride hyperfocus |

## 12. Community practice

**What people actually do** (r/ADHD, r/adhdwomen, r/bulletjournal, ADDitude reader surveys, YouTube and TikTok creators):
- Buy many planners. A common joke is "a drawer of abandoned planners". Readers in ADDitude's roundup name 15 favourites, from the colourful Happy Planner to plain Moleskine dailies, and one reader says cheap notepads work best because an expensive journal feels too precious to "ruin".
- Prefer **undated** layouts, so a missed week leaves no empty dated pages.
- Use sticky notes as movable tasks (they migrate without rewriting).
- Keep a "doom pile" or "Later" list rather than trying to finish everything.
- Use timers constantly: visual timers on desks, phone timers, "one song" timers.
- Body double in person, by video, or by watching "study with me" streams.
- Rotate: a new app or planner every few months, often returning to an old one.

**Common customisations:** a "brain dump" page at the front of each week; habit trackers limited to 3–4 items; colour for categories (in colour books); stickers as rewards; putting the planner on the kitchen counter, open.

**Aesthetic vs minimalist camps.** The aesthetic camp says decoration is the reward that brings them back. The minimalist camp says set-up becomes a procrastination task and blank beautiful spreads trigger shame. Many ADHD adults swing between the two. For Journalwright Studio, the calm printed page handles the look so no set-up time is needed.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Pen:** one that lives with the book (clip or elastic loop). Gel or ballpoint on 80–100 gsm paper. Avoid wet markers that bleed; ghosting through the page is visual noise.
- **Paper:** 80+ gsm to stop show-through; dot grid or light lines.
- **Timer:** a visual timer, a sand timer (5/10/15 min), or a phone timer with the screen face down.
- **Sticky notes:** small ones for movable tasks.
- **Highlighter:** one colour only, to mark "the one thing".
- **Place:** a stand or tray that keeps the book open where you pass it.
- **Headphones:** for body-doubling streams or brown noise.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps:** Tiimo (visual timeline), Structured, Sunsama, Todoist, Apple Reminders, Google Calendar; Focusmate for body doubling; Forest-style focus timers. Some now include generative AI to split tasks (Tiimo Pro; Goblin Tools' "Magic ToDo").
- **E-ink:** reMarkable, Kindle Scribe and Boox tablets run PDF planners with hyperlinks. Fewer notifications than a phone, which helps focus; but a hyperlinked PDF adds navigation steps.
- **Scanning:** The Studio's scan system lets paper pages flow into a digital archive without re-typing.
- **Gained digitally:** reminders at the point of performance (Barkley's key idea), recurring tasks, search, syncing.
- **Lost digitally:** the phone is also the biggest distraction. Notifications fragment attention. Stawarz et al. (2015) found reminders supported repetition but hindered habit development. Paper is always "on", never buzzes, and handwriting may aid memory **(claimed; not reviewed here)**.
- **Hybrid that works for many:** the phone holds alarms and fixed appointments; paper holds the day's plan, the dump and the done list.

## 16. Evidence and research

| Study | Sample | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barkley 1997, *Psychological Bulletin* | Theory review | Inhibition deficit disrupts four executive functions | Influential theory. Not every person with ADHD shows EF deficits on tests; the model is debated |
| Barkley, fact sheet "The Important Role of Executive Functioning and Self-Regulation in ADHD" | Clinical guidance | Externalise information, time and motivation at the point of performance | Expert opinion. Also cites "ego depletion" research, which failed large replications **(not opened here; flag)** |
| Sonuga-Barke 2002, *Behavioural Brain Research* | Theory | Dual pathway: inhibitory control and delay aversion | Supports "make rewards immediate" |
| Safren et al. 2005, *Behaviour Research and Therapy* | 31 adults on meds | CBT + meds beat meds alone on ADHD symptoms, anxiety and depression | Small; no active control |
| Safren et al. 2010, *JAMA* | 86 adults on meds | CBT (12 sessions, 4 on calendar/task-list organisation) vs relaxation + education: 67% vs 33% responders; symptoms lower; responders' gains held at 6 and 12 months | Strong RCT with active control |
| Solanto et al. 2010, *Am J Psychiatry* | 88 adults | Group MCT beat supportive therapy on ADHD severity; odds ratio for response 5.41 | Strong RCT; group format |
| Knouse, Teller and Brooks 2017, *J Consult Clin Psychol* | 32 studies | Medium-to-large pre-post effects; small-to-medium vs control; responders 56% vs 13% | Meta-analysis; mixed study quality |
| Nimmo-Smith et al. 2020, *Psychological Medicine* | 32 RCTs | CBT most studied; most found symptom gains. Mindfulness and cognitive remediation also help | Systematic review |
| López-Pinar et al. 2026, *Behav Res Ther* | 70 studies | CBT improves global functioning (SMD 0.47 post, 0.67 follow-up), most in work life | Recent, large meta-analysis |
| Zheng et al. 2022, *J Atten Disord* | 27 studies, 2,869 children/adolescents | Less accurate and precise time perception; tendency to overestimate | Children only |
| Masicampo and Baumeister 2011, *JPSP* | Lab experiments, general population | Making a specific plan removes intrusive thoughts about unfinished goals | Not ADHD-specific |
| Scullin et al. 2018, *J Exp Psychol: General* | 57 young adults, sleep lab | Writing a to-do list before bed: asleep about 9 min faster than writing a completed-task list | Small; healthy sleepers; not ADHD |
| Harkin et al. 2016, *Psychological Bulletin* | 138 studies, 19,951 people | Monitoring goal progress helps attainment, more when physically recorded | Not ADHD-specific |
| Eagle et al. 2024, *ACM TACCESS* | Survey, 220 people (88% ND) | Body doubling used for initiation, motivation, completion; two-axis model | Self-selected survey; no outcome measure |
| Dodson, *ADDitude* | Clinical observation | Interest, competition, novelty, urgency drive ADHD attention | **Not peer-reviewed.** A useful description, not an established neural model |

**Claimed vs supported:**
- *Supported:* structured CBT that teaches a calendar + task list + daily routine reduces symptoms and improves functioning. Writing plans down reduces mental load. Recording progress helps goals.
- *Plausible, partly supported:* visual timers and time estimates (time perception deficits are real; the tool is untested).
- *Claimed only:* body doubling effectiveness, done lists for ADHD, the Wall of Awful as a mechanism, the interest-based nervous system as biology, novelty rotation.

## 17. Benefits

- Fewer forgotten appointments and tasks — **evidenced** (CBT trials include organisation outcomes).
- Lower ADHD symptom ratings when planner use is part of CBT — **evidenced**.
- Better work functioning — **evidenced** (López-Pinar 2026).
- Less racing mind at bedtime with a specific to-do list — **evidenced** in general adults.
- Better time estimates over weeks — **claimed**.
- Less shame, more sense of progress from a done list — **claimed** (related progress research).
- Easier task starts with a body double — **claimed** (survey).
- Easier restarts after gaps with undated, forgiving pages — **claimed**.
- A record for prescribers and therapists — **claimed**, but commonly requested by clinicians.

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Buying a planner instead of using one | Novelty rush | One book at a time. Rotate layouts inside it, not books |
| Overplanning | Hope; forgetting the time multiplier | Max 3 priorities. Multiply guesses by your x |
| Not opening the book | Out of sight, out of mind | Put it at the point of performance: open on the counter. Anchor checks to meals |
| Blank dated pages after a gap → quitting | Shame | Undated or "skip freely" pages. Rule: never back-fill |
| Set-up becomes the task | Decoration as procrastination | Pre-printed pages; a 5-minute cap on set-up |
| Too many trackers | Enthusiasm | 3–4 items max (James Clear gives the same limit) |
| Guilt from carried-over tasks | The list grows | "Carried 3+ times" → drop, shrink, or ask |
| Timer anxiety | Countdown feels like pressure | Use bubbles to fill (count up), not a countdown |
| Hyperfocus eats the day | Interest-based attention | A "stop" line: set an end time before starting |
| Phone app distraction | Notifications | Paper for the plan, phone only for alarms |
| System works 3 weeks then dies | Novelty wears off | Planned monthly swap of one element |
| Comparing to others' spreads | Social media | Calm, plain pages; no "aesthetic" goal |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** this whole doc. Key moves: externalise, keep it tiny, forgive gaps, pair with meds and support if you have them.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** predictable, identical layouts every day reduce decision load. Avoid surprise changes; make novelty opt-in. Sensory load block beside the plan. Body doubling may be too social; "ambient" versions (a silent stream) may suit better.
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** plan from spoons, not hours. A one-thing page. "Rest" counts as a done item.
- **Depression and anxiety:** done lists help counter "I did nothing". Keep the Wall of Awful prompt gentle; name feelings without scoring them. If the dump turns into rumination, cap it at 2 minutes.
- **Trauma:** avoid prompts that demand explanation ("why didn't you...?"). Offer choices to circle rather than blank "why" lines.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** icons and circle-to-answer, not writing. Big line spacing. Voice memos for the dump, with one written word as a pointer.
- **Low vision:** 8.5 × 11 edition, large type, heavy lines, high contrast. Bubbles at least 6 mm.
- **Motor or hand pain:** circles to fill or slash rather than write; ticks instead of words; a wider pen grip.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** executive dysfunction often co-occurs with dysphoria and medical admin. A "Later" list for appointments and forms; HRT timing in the meds row; no gendered prompts.
- **Shift workers:** time rows that start at the shift, not at 8 a.m. Plan in "before shift / after shift" chunks. The Work shift block already exists.
- **Low income:** any notebook and a sand timer or phone timer is enough. Free Focusmate tier (3 a week). Printable pages.

## 23. Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ADHD | Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, a neurodevelopmental condition with inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity |
| Executive function (EF) | Mental skills for goal-directed action: inhibition, working memory, planning, emotional regulation, self-motivation |
| Self-regulation | Actions you direct at yourself to change what you'd otherwise do, for a future goal (Barkley) |
| Point of performance | The place and time where a task needs doing and knowledge fails to turn into action (Barkley) |
| Externalising | Putting information, time or motivation in physical, visible form |
| Time blindness | Popular term for trouble sensing time passing and future time. Barkley's phrase is "temporal myopia" |
| Delay aversion | Strong preference to escape waiting, driving choices for smaller, sooner rewards (Sonuga-Barke) |
| Task initiation | Starting a task. Often the hardest step |
| Brain dump | Writing every open loop down, unsorted |
| Done list / ta-da list | A list of what you did, planned or not |
| Body doubling | Working with another person present who isn't doing your task |
| Wall of Awful | Mahan's metaphor for the emotional barrier built from past failures |
| Interest-based nervous system | Dodson's term: attention driven by interest, competition, novelty, urgency. Not peer-reviewed |
| Hyperfocus | Intense, hard-to-break focus on an interesting task |
| Distractibility delay | Writing a distraction down to handle later, then returning to the task (Safren's CBT) |
| CBT | Cognitive behavioural therapy |
| MCT | Meta-cognitive therapy (Solanto), a group CBT targeting time and organisation |
| Time multiplier | Your personal ratio of actual to guessed time |
| Spoons | A unit of daily energy (spoon theory) |
| Novelty rotation | Changing one element of the system on purpose to keep it fresh |
| Dopamine menu | A list of rewards sorted by size |
| Fresh start effect | Motivation spikes after time landmarks: new week, month, birthday (Dai et al. 2014) |
| Implementation intention | An if-then plan: "When X, I will Y" (Gollwitzer) |

## 24. FAQ

**Do I need a diagnosis to use this?** No. The tools help anyone with executive dysfunction. A diagnosis opens access to medication and CBT, which have the strongest evidence.

**Can a planner replace medication or therapy?** No. The planner is the tool CBT teaches you to use; the trials that showed benefits combined it with coaching over 12 sessions, and most participants were already on medication.

**Why do I keep abandoning planners?** Novelty wears off, gaps create shame, and set-up becomes a task. Use undated or skip-friendly pages, never back-fill, and rotate one element monthly.

**Is time blindness real?** Time perception differences are documented in children and adolescents with ADHD (meta-analysis of 27 studies). "Time blindness" is a popular label, not a diagnosis. Adult data are thinner.

**Does body doubling work?** People report it helps them start and finish, especially chores. There's no outcome trial yet, only surveys. It's low-risk to try.

**Is the interest-based nervous system a real brain type?** It's a clinician's description, not a peer-reviewed model. It describes the experience well. Don't read it as "I can only do interesting things".

**Should I use a countdown timer?** If countdowns cause panic, use a count-up (fill a bubble each 15 minutes) or a sand timer.

**Paper or app?** Many do both: phone for alarms and fixed appointments (point of performance), paper for the daily plan and done list.

**How many priorities?** One to three. On low-spoon days, one.

**What if I miss a week?** Start on today's page. Write "Back" if you like. No catch-up.

**Is a done list better than a to-do list?** They do different jobs. The to-do list plans; the done list counters "I did nothing". Before bed, a specific to-do list for tomorrow helped sleep more than a done list in one lab study.

### e. Don't adopt

- **Streaks, scores, points or "productivity %".** They turn gaps into failure, the opposite of low-shame design.
- **Countdown alarms or reminders on the e-ink companion.** Notifications are out of scope, and alarms spike anxiety.
- **AI task breakdown (like Tiimo Pro or Goblin Tools).** Generative AI is out of scope for the firmware, and pre-printed prompts can do the core job ("first tiny step").
- **Colour-coded timelines.** The printed book is B&W. Use shape and position.
- **Dated guilt.** No "missed days" counters, no "you skipped" messages anywhere.
- **Heavy daily layouts.** More than 6-7 blocks overwhelms. Keep it to one page.
- **Diagnostic language on the page.** No "symptom" checklists that feel clinical; this is a calm notebook.

## 26. Open questions

- Does the estimate-vs-actual practice improve adults' time estimates, or just make the gap visible? No trial found.
- Which single element of CBT planner training does most of the work? Trials test whole packages.
- Does body doubling improve output, or mainly start-up? Only survey data exist.
- Is a done list at the end of the day helpful for ADHD adults' mood and sleep, given Scullin's finding for to-do lists at bedtime?
- How much do novelty and rotation help long-term use, and how much do they just restart the cycle?
- Do adults show the same time-perception pattern (overestimation) found in children's studies?
- Is a silent visual timer on e-ink calming or distracting? Needs a trial with real users.
