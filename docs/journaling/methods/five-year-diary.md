# Five-Year Diary (One Line a Day, Q&A a Day, Hobonichi 5-Year Techo)

A diary where each page is one calendar date, split into five slots, one per year. You write a few lines today
under this year; next year you write under the next slot and see what you wrote a year ago. After five years each
page holds five of the same day. This doc covers the format's history, the main products, the page anatomy, the
research on why looking back works, and what Keeping Watch can borrow.

> Facts checked on 2026-09-28. "Uncertain" marks anything sources disagree on or I couldn't confirm.

---

## 2. Summary

A five-year diary turns one habit, writing a line about today, into a slow comparison across years. Each page is a
date, not a day: "May 5" holds 2027, 2028, 2029, 2030 and 2031. Because space is tiny, the bar to write is low, and
because last year's line is right above today's, every entry comes with a small surprise: what you were doing, who
was around, how you felt. American stationers sold "Line-A-Day" five-year diaries for much of the 20th century.
Chronicle Books revived the idea in 2009 with *One Line a Day*, which with its spin-offs has sold over three
million copies. Potter's *Q&A a Day* adds one question per date, and Hobonichi's *5-Year Techo* adds a full grid
page per date for notes. Research on "rediscovery" suggests people enjoy rereading ordinary records far more than
they predict, which is the whole engine of the format. For Keeping Watch, the key idea is "On this day" across
years: a small, calm block that shows past entries for the same date.

## 3. History and origin

### Early "Line-A-Day" diaries
- Archivists sometimes catalogue these books as **"Line-A-Day" journals**. A five-year diary is one where "each page
  displays a single day spanning five years" (CSUN University Library).
- An example: the **Millicent Farrington Lowry Diary** (CSUN Special Collections), a five-year diary kept in New York
  from **1943 to 1947**, recording home-front life during and after World War II. Its printed instructions promise:
  "You will find amusing recollections that will bring chuckles – possibly business affairs whose record will prove
  valuable."
- Many mid-century American five-year diaries had a small lock and key and were popular gifts (widely seen in collections and resale listings; not verified against a primary source).
  **Uncertain:** who printed the first one. I found no reliable source on the earliest publisher.
- Japan has a parallel tradition of **3-year, 5-year and 10-year diaries** (連用日記, *ren'yō nikki*, "consecutive-use
  diaries"). Hakubunkan, a publisher founded in 1887, is today mainly a diary maker and sells 10-year diaries.
  **Uncertain:** when Japanese multi-year diaries started.

### Cultural context
- Five-year diaries belong to the long tradition of the **almanac and daybook**: short, dated records of weather, work,
  visits and money. The modern versions add reflection and gift appeal.
- The 2009 revival rode a wave of small, giftable "memory books" and later Instagram "5-year journal" posts.
- In Japan, the multi-year diary is a gift for retirement, a new decade or a new family.

### Key quotes
- Hobonichi: the 5-Year Techo lets you "compare what you were doing or thinking a year ago, and to plan ahead."
- Hobonichi: "5 years may feel like a long time, but it's actually designed for casual use."
- Chronicle Books: "This classic memory book is the perfect way to track the ups and downs of life, day by day."
- Ting Zhang (on the rediscovery study): "What is ordinary now actually becomes more extraordinary in the future."

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- **Small is sustainable.** One line is a habit you can keep when tired, sick or busy.
- **The date is the index.** You never search: you open to today and the past is waiting.
- **Comparison without scoring.** You see change (or sameness) year over year without charts.
- **The ordinary becomes precious.** Lines that felt boring when written are the ones people love later.
- **Five years is long enough to see patterns** (seasons, anniversaries, moods, jobs) and short enough to finish.
- **Problem it solves:** big blank journals that feel like homework, and memories of whole years that blur.

## 5. Core components

| Element | What it is |
|---|---|
| Date page | One page (or spread) per calendar date, 366 in all |
| Year slots | Five boxes per page, each with a blank "20__" or a printed year |
| Question (Q&A a Day) | One prompt at the top, the same every year |
| Notes page (Hobonichi) | A facing grid page for the same date |
| Leap day | Feb 29 page, used once or twice in five years |
| Yearly memo / review pages | Hobonichi: 5 pages for plans or reviews |
| Remember This / lists | Hobonichi back pages: notes for all five years, My Lists, gifts, addresses |
| Ribbon | Marks today |
| Rituals | The daily line; reading the lines above; the yearly start of a new slot |

### Date page
The whole book is ordered by date, not by year. January 1 is page 1 forever. That makes finding "today" instant.

### Year slots
Each slot is a few lines. In *One Line a Day* each page has five entries headed "20__"; you fill in the year. In
the Hobonichi 5-Year the years are printed with the weekday, so the book is tied to one five-year span. A6 gives
about **178 grid squares** per year per date; A5 about **352**.

### Question (Q&A a Day)
A fixed question such as "What is the most honest thing you've said today?" is printed at the top of each date.
You answer it five times across five years, so you see how your answer changes.

### Notes page (Hobonichi)
The right page is blank 3.7 mm grid for the same date: overflow, sketches, stickers, a pasted photo.

### Leap day
Feb 29 exists as a page. In a 2027–2031 book only 2028 has a Feb 29. Users write "no day" or use it for a yearly note.

### Yearly and back pages (Hobonichi)
"Yearly memo pages (5 pages)" let you "write down plans for the year or write down a review of what happened".
"Remember This" is for "notes that aren't time-sensitive or things you'd like to note across all five years".
My Lists holds "8 lists of themes of your choice", 10 entries each. Gift and address pages scale with size
(A6: 17 gifts and 6 addresses; A5: 40 and 16).

### Rituals
- Read last year's line first, then write today's.
- On January 1, start the next slot.
- At the end of five years, read the whole book in one sitting.

## 6. Setting it up

1. **Pick a type:**
   - Undated (*One Line a Day*) if you might skip years or start mid-year.
   - Question (*Q&A a Day*) if blank space freezes you.
   - Hobonichi 5-Year if you want room for notes and like the Techo paper.
   - 3-year versions if five feels too long; 10-year if you're settled.
2. **Write the years** on each page of an undated book, or only as you reach them. Many people write the year as they
   go so a missed year doesn't leave printed gaps.
3. **Choose your line rule.** Examples: "what happened", "one good thing", "weather + mood + one fact", or "answer the
   question".
4. **Choose a time.** Bedtime, morning coffee, or after the last shift task.
5. **Put the book where you'll see it** with a pen clipped to it.

### First day
Open to today's date, write the year, write one line. Done. If you start mid-year, the book fills up mid-year five
years later. That's fine.

### First week
- Days 1–7: one line each. Try two different rules and keep the one that felt easier.
- Day 7: read all seven lines. Notice what you want more of (names, weather, food, feelings).

### First year
The first year feels flat because there's nothing above your line yet. Year two is when the format starts working.
Keep going.

## 7. Daily practice

1. **Open to today** (ribbon).
2. **Read what's above** (from year 2 on). 10–30 seconds.
3. **Write one line**, about 10–25 words. Useful formula: *fact + feeling* ("Walked to the river. Calm.").
4. **Optional marks:** a weather symbol, a mood number 1–5, a spoons number.
5. **Close the book.** Total: 1–3 minutes.

**Prompts when you're stuck:**
- What will I want to know about today in a year?
- Who did I see?
- What did I eat that I liked?
- What was the weather doing?
- One thing that went fine.

**Missed days:** write "–" or leave it blank. Don't back-fill more than a day or two; memory fills gaps with guesses.

## 8. Rhythms

- **Weekly:** optional flip through the week's seven pages. Tick anything you want to carry forward.
- **Monthly:** read the month across all years so far; jot one pattern on a yearly memo page (Hobonichi) or the
  inside cover.
- **Quarterly:** check the seasons: when did you get sick last year, when did energy drop, when did the garden start?
- **Yearly:** on 1 January, note the new year's first slot; write a one-paragraph review on a yearly memo page. Birthdays
  and anniversaries become natural check-in dates because they recur on the same page.
- **End of book:** read the whole book, pick favourite lines, shelve it and start a new one. Some people start the next
  book with a copy of their "best of" lines on the first page.
- **Index upkeep:** none needed; the date is the index. Hobonichi's "Remember This" and My Lists act as a cross-year index.

### A date page after three years

```
MAY 5
2027  Rain. Lilacs by the gate. Ana called, laughed a lot. Mood 4.

2029  Lilacs again! Planted one at home. Ana visited. Mood 5.
```

What the page shows: lilacs bloom around May 5; Ana is a steady good thing; energy dips in a route change.

## 12. Community practice

- **Where:** Instagram (#5yearjournal, #onelineaday, #hobonichi5year), YouTube flip-throughs at the end of a book,
  Reddit journaling communities.
- **What people actually do:**
  - Write in bed as the last act of the day.
  - Use it as a "light" diary alongside a bigger journal.
  - Track one number (mood, pain, spoons, weight, weather) plus a phrase.
  - Parents record kids' sayings.
  - Couples share one book and alternate lines.
  - Hobonichi 5-Year users paste tiny photos or tickets on the right page.
- **Common customisations:** colour-coding each year's ink; a symbol key on the inside cover (sun, cloud, heart,
  spoon); small stickers for events; tabs for birthdays.
- **Aesthetic camp:** matching pens per year, washi-taped right pages, themed covers.
- **Minimalist camp:** pencil, one line, no decoration. The entire point is speed.
- **Known frustrations:** tiny slots for large handwriting; guilt at gaps; sadness at seeing grief or hard years;
  undated books drifting (users forget to write the year).

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Pen:** fine (0.3–0.4 mm) gel or ballpoint so lines fit small slots. Pencil fades less than people think but
  smudges over five years of handling.
- **Paper:** trade books use standard book paper (weight not published); some bleed with fountain pens. Hobonichi's
  Tomoe River S is thin but bleed-resistant; test fountain pens.
- **Colour per year:** five pen colours help scanning across years. For black-and-white print, use symbols instead.
- **Stickers and stamps:** only tiny ones; slots are small.
- **Ribbon or clip:** marks today.
- **Rulers:** not needed.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps:** many journaling apps (Day One and others) show "On this day" past entries. Spreadsheets with a date
  column and year columns work as a DIY five-year diary.
- **Templates:** printable five-year pages (366 pages) sold on Etsy and similar.
- **E-ink:** a five-year PDF works on reMarkable, Kindle Scribe or Boox; the date index needs hyperlinks to be usable.
- **Scanning:** scanning a whole five-year book is slow (366+ pages). Photographing only pages with notable lines is
  more realistic.
- **Lost vs paper:** the side-by-side view of five years on one page (apps usually show one past entry at a time);
  handwriting changes over years; the object.
- **Gained:** reminders (not wanted in Keeping Watch), search, photos, backups, no running out of space.

## 16. Evidence and research

No trial has tested five-year diaries. Related research:

| Study | Sample and method | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zhang, Kim, Brooks, Gino and Norton (2014), "A 'Present' for the Future", *Psychological Science* 25(10): 1851–1860 | Study 1: 135 students made time capsules and predicted future interest; follow-ups months later; more online and lab studies | People underestimate how curious and pleased they'll be rediscovering records; ordinary moments are underestimated most. 58% chose to reread a conversation they'd had, though only 27% had chosen to record it | Solid design. Short horizon (months), mostly students. Directly supports the "reread the ordinary" mechanism |
| Wagenaar (1986), "My memory", *Cognitive Psychology* 18: 225–252 | One person recorded 2,400 events over 6 years (what, who, where, when) and tested recall | Many details were lost; "when" was the weakest cue; pleasant, salient and emotional events were remembered better | Single subject; old. Shows dates fade first, which is what a date-indexed diary preserves |
| Frattaroli (2006), meta-analysis, *Psychological Bulletin* 132(6) | 146 randomized disclosure-writing studies | Small positive effect, r = 0.075 | About emotional disclosure writing, not one-line logs |
| Baikie and Wilhelm (2005), review, *Advances in Psychiatric Treatment* 11(5) | Review of expressive writing | Benefits for health and mood; short-term distress after writing; not helpful or harmful for some trauma groups | Different practice (15–20 min, emotional topics) |
| Greater Good in Action, "Time Capsule" practice (UC Berkeley) | Practice sheet based on Zhang et al. | 30 minutes to create, 15 to revisit, repeat every few months | Practice guide, not a study |

**Claimed vs supported:**
- Supported (modest): writing down ordinary days creates records people enjoy more than expected; diaries preserve
  dates and details memory drops.
- Claimed: five-year diaries improve self-awareness, reveal patterns, build gratitude, reduce stress. Plausible, not tested.
- Unknown: whether reading past hard years on the same date helps or harms people with depression or grief.

## 17. Benefits

- Takes 1–3 minutes, so it survives bad days. *(claimed)*
- Rereading ordinary records brings more pleasure and meaning than expected. *(evidenced: Zhang et al. 2014)*
- Keeps dates and details memory loses. *(evidenced: Wagenaar 1986, single subject)*
- Shows seasonal and anniversary patterns (energy, illness, mood). *(claimed)*
- Makes progress visible ("last year I couldn't do this"). *(claimed)*
- Low cost and no rules. *(claimed)*
- Builds a keepsake that's quick to read. *(claimed)*
- Question formats remove blank-page freeze. *(claimed)*

## 18. Pitfalls

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Missing a week and feeling the "gap" forever | Mark gaps with "–"; the book still works; restart today |
| Slots too small | Use the Hobonichi right page, a 3-year book, or write only keywords |
| Painful anniversaries show up every year | Use a symbol instead of words for hard days; allow skipping that page; talk to someone |
| First year feels pointless | Know that year 2 is when it pays off; read the week each Sunday for early value |
| Undated books: forgetting to write the year | Write all five years on January 1 pages as you reach them, or use colour per year |
| Boredom with "what happened" | Rotate a question per month or switch to Q&A a Day |
| Rereading makes you compare and judge | Read only one line above, not all; treat it as data, not grades |
| Losing the book | Keep it at home; don't carry it |
| Five years feels too long | Three-year versions; or treat each year as complete on its own |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** tiny task, fixed place, pen attached. Use symbols for speed. Missed days don't break anything.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** predictable format; same layout every day; choose a plain cover; a fixed sentence
  frame ("Today: __. Felt: __.").
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** the one-line limit fits; log symptoms or spoons as numbers; patterns across years
  (flares, seasons) are a real payoff to show a clinician.
- **Depression and anxiety:** use a "small good thing" rule; don't reread alone on hard dates; seeing that past hard
  days passed can help, but it can also hurt. Let the person choose.
- **Trauma:** anniversaries recur on the same page. Offer a symbol-only option and permission to skip pages.
  Don't use it as a trauma record.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** single words, symbols or stamps; voice memo titled with the date as a backup.
- **Low vision:** A5 (Hobonichi Large) or a DIY book with big slots; bold pen; high-contrast lines. Trade books have
  small type and faint lines.
- **Motor or hand pain:** one line suits; thick-grip pen; a book that lies flat (Hobonichi does).
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** a quiet year-over-year view of transition (voice, name use, HRT anniversaries,
  euphoria moments). Keep it private at home. Use initials if needed.
- **Shift workers:** write at the end of the shift; for overnight shifts pick one rule (the date you started) and stick to it.
- **Low income:** DIY in any notebook: 366 pages, five boxes each. Or a single sheet per month with dates down and years across.

## 21. Combining

- **With the Hobonichi Techo:** Techo for detail, five-year for the line. See [hobonichi-techo.md](hobonichi-techo.md).
- **With trackers:** add a 1–5 number (mood, pain, spoons) to each line; the same date across years becomes a tiny chart.
- **With gratitude:** make the line "one good thing".
- **With a yearly review:** read the five-year book's month pages before writing a year-end review.
- **With Keeping Watch:** the monthly books already have an "On this day" block; a five-year view extends it across years.

20. What would you tell yourself a year ago?

Collections and trackers:
21. Seasonal firsts (first snow, first lilac, first hot day).
22. Anniversaries (name, HRT, moves, jobs).
23. Spoons per day as a number.
24. Books finished.
25. People list: who shows up across years.
26. "Remember this" list: things worth knowing every year (e.g. "hat by the door in May").

## 23. Glossary

- **Five-year diary / memory book:** one date per page, five year slots.
- **Line-A-Day:** older name and catalogue term for these diaries.
- **Slot:** the space for one year on a date page.
- **Undated years:** "20__" slots you fill in (One Line a Day).
- **Printed years:** years and weekdays printed (Hobonichi 5-Year).
- **Q&A a Day:** question-per-date variant.
- **Ren'yō nikki (連用日記):** Japanese multi-year diary.
- **Remember This:** Hobonichi back page for notes across all years.
- **Rediscovery:** rereading records of the past; the effect studied by Zhang et al.
- **On this day:** showing past entries for today's date.
- **Leap-day page:** Feb 29, used once every four years.

## 24. FAQ

1. **What if I start in the middle of the year?** Start on today's date. The book ends mid-year five years later.
2. **What if I miss days?** Leave them blank or mark "–". Don't back-fill more than a day or two.
3. **What should I write?** Pick one rule and keep it: "fact + feeling" is the easiest.
4. **Is one line enough to matter?** Yes. The value comes from rereading, and short records still trigger memories.
5. **One Line a Day or Q&A a Day?** One Line if you like freedom; Q&A if blank space freezes you.
6. **Why pick the Hobonichi 5-Year?** More room (a notes page per date), lay-flat binding, printed weekdays. It's Japanese-language and pricier.
7. **What about Feb 29?** Use it when it comes; otherwise use it for a note or leave it.
8. **Will seeing bad years hurt?** It can. Use symbols for hard days and skip pages when you need to.
9. **Can two people share one?** Yes. Alternate lines or use different pens.
10. **What do I do when it's full?** Read it all once, mark favourite lines, shelve it, start another.

### d. Proposed method layout: "Five-year line"

1. Moon/sun/season
2. On this day (with last year's line when available)
3. One line (fact + feeling, 2 lines)
4. Scale: mood 1–5
5. Space

### e. Don't adopt

- **Pre-printed five-year slots in monthly books:** each monthly book is used once, so the slots would stay empty.
  Put the five-year view in the Keeper instead.
- **Automatic "memories" alerts on the X4:** that's a notification; out of scope.
- **Daily questions that probe trauma or body image:** risky for some users and against the calm design.
- **Colour-per-year coding:** the books are black and white; use symbols.
- **Hobonichi's quotes or layout art:** copyrighted.
