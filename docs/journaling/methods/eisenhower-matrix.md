# The Eisenhower Matrix (urgent / important)

A reference for the urgent/important matrix: where the idea came from, how the four boxes work on paper, what the research says about urgency, where the method breaks, and what Journalwright Studio should take from it.

Checked against sources on 2026-09-28. Where a detail couldn't be confirmed from a primary source, it's marked **(unconfirmed)**.

---

## 1. At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Creator** | No single creator. The saying comes from Dwight D. Eisenhower's 1954 speech, where he credited an unnamed "former college president". Stephen R. Covey turned it into the four-quadrant "Time Management Matrix" in *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People* (1989) |
| **Year** | Saying: 19 August 1954. Matrix: 1989 (Covey), expanded in *First Things First* (1994) |

## 2. Summary

The Eisenhower Matrix sorts tasks on two questions: is it urgent (does it need doing soon), and is it important (does it matter to your goals and values)? That gives four boxes: do it now (urgent and important), schedule it (important, not urgent), hand it off or batch it (urgent, not important), and drop it (neither). The idea traces to a line Eisenhower quoted in 1954, and Stephen Covey made it famous in 1989 as the heart of his Habit 3, "Put first things first". Covey's key claim is that the important-but-not-urgent box (Quadrant II: health, relationships, planning, prevention) is where lives improve, and that it gets crowded out by urgent things. Lab research backs the worry: in five experiments, Zhu, Yang and Hsee (2018) found people pick a task with a fake deadline over a task that pays more, and that reminding people of the payoff cuts the effect. The matrix itself has never been tested head to head. Its weak spots are real: "important" is subjective, "delegate" assumes you have someone, and the boxes ignore energy. For Journalwright Studio it works best as one small four-box block, with kinder labels, and one daily "Quadrant II" line.

## 3. History and origin

### Who created it

**Nobody owns it.** The idea has three layers:

1. **The saying.** On 19 August 1954, President Dwight D. Eisenhower spoke to the Second Assembly of the World Council of Churches in Evanston, Illinois. He said: "I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important. The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent." He was quoting "a former college president", whom he didn't name.
2. **The name.** Later writers attached Eisenhower's name to the grid, so it's called the Eisenhower Matrix, Eisenhower Box, or Eisenhower Method. There's no sign Eisenhower drew a grid himself **(unconfirmed either way; no primary source for a grid was found)**.
3. **The grid.** Stephen R. Covey (1932–2012), a leadership writer and teacher, published the four-quadrant "Time Management Matrix" in *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People* (1989), under Habit 3.

Eisenhower's background matters to the quote. He was Supreme Commander of the Allied forces in Europe in WWII, then Columbia University's 13th president (1948–1953), then first Supreme Commander of NATO (on leave from Columbia, 1950), then US President (1953–1961). So the "former college president" line came from a man who was himself a former college president. Quote Investigator found no evidence identifying who Eisenhower meant, and concluded Eisenhower was an early popularizer, not the originator.

### Cultural context

- **Postwar management.** 1950s America was building the modern office and its crises. Eisenhower used the line to say big problems (peace, faith, cooperation) don't shout, while small ones do.
- **Christian devotional writing.** Hummel's 1967 booklet framed the problem as spiritual: busyness steals time from what God asks. This framing fed evangelical and business culture.
- **1980s–90s self-help boom.** Covey's books sold at huge scale, and the matrix became a standard corporate training slide.
- **2000s productivity blogs.** The grid got simple verbs and colours and became an app feature.

### Key quotes

- Eisenhower, 1954: "I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important. The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent."
- Covey (as quoted by FranklinPlanner's reading of *7 Habits*): "The key is not to prioritize what's on your schedule, but to schedule your priorities."
- FranklinCovey today: "The more time we can spend in Q2, the less we'll be dealing with other people's urgencies."
- *First Things First* (via summary): important tasks may not be urgent, and urgent tasks are not necessarily important.

## 4. Philosophy and principles

**Stated goals**

- Spend less time reacting and more time on what matters.
- Prevent crises by doing important work before it becomes urgent.
- Say no (or "not me") to urgent-but-unimportant demands.

**Core beliefs**

1. **Urgency and importance are separate.** A ringing phone is urgent; a checkup is important. Most people treat urgency as if it meant importance.
2. **Urgency is loud.** Urgent things demand attention by themselves. Important things wait quietly until they become emergencies.
3. **Quadrant II is the lever.** Covey's core claim: time spent on important, non-urgent work (planning, prevention, relationships, health, learning, rest that restores) shrinks Quadrant I over time, because fewer things become crises.
4. **Values define importance.** For Covey, importance comes from your mission, roles and principles, not your inbox. This is why his planning starts with roles.
5. **Schedule priorities, don't prioritise a schedule.** Put the important things on the calendar first ("big rocks"), then fit the rest around them.

**The problem it solves**

- Feeling busy all day with nothing to show.
- Chronic firefighting.
- Important but slow goals (health, a skill, a relationship, a move) never starting.

## 5. Core components

| Element | What it is | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| Two axes | Urgent / not urgent; important / not important | The grid |
| Quadrant I (Q1) | Urgent + important. Crises, deadlines, real emergencies | Top left |
| Quadrant II (Q2) | Important, not urgent. Planning, prevention, health, relationships, rest, learning | Top right |
| Quadrant III (Q3) | Urgent, not important. Many interruptions, some calls and messages, other people's minor asks | Bottom left |
| Quadrant IV (Q4) | Neither. Busywork, mindless scrolling, trivia | Bottom right |
| Verbs | Do / Schedule / Delegate / Delete (popular version) | Box labels |
| Brain dump | The full list you sort from | Any page |
| Weekly compass (Covey) | Roles, weekly goals per role, "sharpen the saw" | Weekly page |
| Big rocks | The few Q2 things placed first in the week | Weekly page |
| Daily adapt | A daily look to fit Q1 in without losing the big rocks | Daily page |
| Time log (optional) | A record of where time actually went, sorted into quadrants | Any page |

### 5.1 The two axes

- **Urgent** means time-sensitive: it has a deadline or a consequence if not done soon. Urgency is usually visible (a due date, a person waiting, a smell of smoke).
- **Important** means it serves your goals, values, health, people or duties. Importance is a judgment, which makes it the hard axis. Mind Tools puts it as: important work leads to your goals; urgent work demands attention and often serves someone else's goals.

### 5.2 Quadrant I: urgent and important

Crises, deadlines, a sick kid, a rent notice, a broken tooth. You have to do these. The aim is not to avoid Q1 but to keep it from being your whole life. Covey's point: many Q1 items are Q2 items that waited too long (the checkup you skipped becomes the emergency room).

### 5.3 Quadrant II: important, not urgent

The heart of the method. Examples: refilling a prescription before it runs out, planning meals, sleep, exercise, calling a friend, learning, fixing the bike before it breaks, filing paperwork before the deadline, rest that actually restores. Nothing forces these to happen, so they don't. The fix is to schedule them.

### 5.4 Quadrant III: urgent, not important

Things that feel pressing but don't serve your goals: many messages, some meetings, other people's minor emergencies, "reply-all". Covey calls this the quadrant of deception, because it feels like Q1. The popular advice is to delegate. Real-world fixes also include batching, setting a time, saying no, or letting it wait.

### 5.5 Quadrant IV: neither

Covey's examples include trivia, some mail, time wasters. The popular label is "Delete". **Care note:** real rest isn't Q4. Rest that restores you is Q2. Only numbing activity you don't want counts as Q4, and even that is often a sign of depletion, not laziness.

### 5.6 The verbs

The four verbs (Do, Schedule, Delegate, Delete) aren't in Covey; they come from later blogs and apps. They're easy to remember but blunt. Section 25 proposes gentler labels: **Now**, **Plan**, **Hand off or batch**, **Let go**.

### 5.7 Covey's weekly planning (the "fourth generation")

Covey described four "generations" of time management: notes and checklists; calendars and appointment books; daily prioritising and goal setting; and his own, which manages yourself around roles and Q2. His weekly process, as summarised by FranklinPlanner:

1. Identify your roles (for example: self, friend, worker, artist).
2. Pick one or two important goals per role for the week.
3. Schedule time for those goals (big rocks first).
4. Adapt daily: review each morning and adjust while keeping the commitments.

*First Things First* adds "sharpen the saw" (renewing body, mind, heart and spirit) as a standing role, and the image of fitting big rocks into a jar before the gravel and sand.

### 5.8 The time log

Some versions ask you to log a few days of real time use and mark each block Q1–Q4. It shows how much of the day is Q3. Useful once; heavy as a habit.

## 6. Setting it up

### First setup (10 minutes)

1. Take one page. Draw a cross to make four boxes. Label the top edge "Urgent / Not urgent" and the left edge "Important / Not important".
2. Brain dump: list everything on your mind on a separate page or the back.
3. Sort each item into a box. If you're unsure, ask: "What happens if this waits a week?" (urgency) and "Would I care about this in a month?" (importance).
4. Circle one Q2 item. Give it a time today or this week.
5. For Q3, write one word next to each: who (hand off), when (batch), or "no".
6. Cross out Q4 items you're letting go of.

### First day

- Do Q1 first, but set a stop.
- Put your one Q2 item into a real slot, even 15 minutes.
- At the end of the day, look at the grid. Note which box grew.

### First week

- Day 1–2: sort daily in the morning.
- Day 3: notice patterns (who fills Q3? what keeps becoming Q1?).
- Day 5–7: do a short weekly plan: roles, one Q2 goal per role, scheduled.
- End of week: ask what Q1 items could have been Q2 last week.

## 7. Daily practice

About 5 minutes in the morning, 2 minutes at night.

**Morning (order matters)**

1. **Capture** (1 min): new things since yesterday.
2. **Sort** (2 min): put each into a box. Keep it to 3–4 lines per box; the matrix is for choosing, not storing.
3. **Pick** (1 min): one Q2 item for today. Give it a time and a size (15, 30, 60 min).
4. **Guard** (1 min): decide one Q3 thing you'll batch, hand off or skip.

**During the day**

- When something new lands, ask one question: "Now, plan, hand off, or let go?"
- Before starting something, notice if it's urgent only because of a made-up deadline (see section 16).

**Night**

1. Tick what's done.
2. Move what's left: Q1 leftovers go to tomorrow's top; Q2 leftovers get a new slot.
3. Prompt: "What urgent thing today could have been planned last week?"

**Prompts**

- If this waited a week, what would happen?
- Whose goal is this?
- Is the deadline real or someone's preference?
- What would make tomorrow easier?
- What's the smallest Q2 step I can do in 15 minutes?

## 8. Rhythms

| Rhythm | What to do | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **Daily** | Sort, pick one Q2, guard against one Q3 | 5 min |
| **Weekly** | Covey planning: roles, 1–2 goals per role, schedule big rocks; review last week's grid for Q1 items that started as Q2 | 15–30 min |
| **Monthly** | Look at the month's Q2 items. Which roles got nothing? Pick a Q2 theme for next month | 15 min |
| **Quarterly** | Review roles. Drop or add a role. Look at repeat crises and plan prevention | 30 min |
| **Yearly** | Revisit mission or values (Covey's personal mission statement). What mattered this year? | 1 hour |

There's no index, migration or archive system; the matrix borrows those from whatever notebook it sits in.

## 9. Page anatomy

### The classic grid (one page, portrait)

Equal quadrants. Axis labels outside the frame. Each box gets 3–5 lines. On 5.5×8.5 in, each box is about 2.4 × 3.5 in.

```
                 URGENT              NOT URGENT
            +------------------+------------------+
            | I   NOW          | II  PLAN         |
 IMPORTANT  | [ ] ____________ | [ ] ____________ |
            | [ ] ____________ | [ ] ____________ |
            | [ ] ____________ | [ ] ____________ |
            |                  |  when: ______    |
            +------------------+------------------+
            | III HAND OFF /   | IV  LET GO       |
 NOT        |     BATCH        |                  |
 IMPORTANT  | [ ] ______ who:_ | ~ ____________   |
            | [ ] ______ who:_ | ~ ____________   |
            | [ ] ______ when: |                  |
            +------------------+------------------+
```

### Day-page strip (compact, inside a daily page)

A short 2×2 block, 1.4 in tall on a small page. Two lines per box.

```
+-----------------------+-----------------------+

| [ ] _________________ | [ ] _________ ____    |
+-----------------------+-----------------------+
| > Hand off / batch    | ~ Let go              |

+-----------------------+-----------------------+
```

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Who | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| **Covey Time Management Matrix** | Stephen Covey, 1989 | Roman-numeral quadrants; focus on Q2 and weekly planning by roles. No "Do/Delete" verbs |
| **FranklinCovey Time Matrix / "5 Choices"** | FranklinCovey (Kogon, Merrill, Rinne, *The 5 Choices*, 2015) | Names Q2 "extraordinary productivity"; ties it to attention and energy **(quadrant names from this book unconfirmed beyond Q2)** |
| **Do / Schedule / Delegate / Delete** | Popular blogs and apps | Action verbs in each box |
| **Tyranny of the Urgent** | Charles E. Hummel, 1967 | Essay, no grid; spiritual framing of the same idea |
| **Priority matrix apps** | Eisenhower.me, Priority Matrix, Todoist filters | Drag tasks between boxes; Todoist maps P1–P4 to quadrants |
| **Impact / effort matrix** | Product and lean practice (no single creator) | Swaps axes for value and effort: quick wins, big bets, fill-ins, money pits |
| **Energy matrix** | Chronic illness and neurodivergent communities (no single creator) | Adds or swaps an axis for energy cost (low / high spoons) |
| **ABC / ABCDE** | Alan Lakein (1973), Brian Tracy | Letter ranking instead of a grid |
| **MoSCoW** | Dai Clegg, 1994 (software) | Must / Should / Could / Won't: importance only, no urgency axis |
| **Three-box "now / next / later"** | Common in agile boards | Drops importance; purely time |

## 12. Community practice

What people actually do (Reddit r/productivity, r/bulletjournal, r/ADHD; blogs; YouTube spread tours):

- **As a Sunday spread** in a bullet journal: one page per week sorting the task list. The most common use.
- **As a sticky-note board**: four sticky notes on a wall or desk; move them.
- **Only Q2**: many people drop the grid and just keep a "Q2 list" or "important not urgent" box on the weekly page.
- **Colour coding**: red / green / yellow / grey quadrants. Popular online; useless in black-and-white print.
- **Energy and spoons swap**: disabled and ADHD writers add an energy axis or mark each task with a spoon cost.
- **Criticisms heard often**: "everything is urgent at my job", "I have no one to delegate to", "I end up sorting instead of doing", "the matrix made me feel bad about resting".
- **Aesthetic camp**: hand-lettered quadrants, washi tape borders, icons per box.
- **Minimalist camp**: two lines on an index card: "Must today" and "Matters, not urgent".

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Pen**: any. A fine liner (0.3–0.5 mm) keeps small boxes readable.
- **Paper**: 80–100 gsm is plenty; no special bleed needs.
- **Ruler**: helps draw a clean cross. Or fold the page in four.
- **Sticky notes**: small ones (1.5×2 in) let you move tasks between boxes.
- **Stamps**: 2×2 grid stamps exist; nice for daily use in a blank notebook.
- **No colour needed**: use icons instead (! for now, o for plan, > for hand off, ~ for let go).

**Apps and templates**

- Todoist: labels or priorities P1–P4 mapped to quadrants, with a filter per quadrant.

**Lost versus gained**

| Paper | Digital |
|---|---|
| Forces a small list; you can't overfill a box | Unlimited tasks; boxes fill up and stop helping |
| Rewriting is a natural review | Moving tasks is instant, so it's easy to never decide |
| No pings | Due dates create reminders, which manufacture urgency |
| Hard to sort across many days | Filters and search |

## 16. Evidence and research

### The mere urgency effect (Zhu, Yang & Hsee, 2018)

- **Paper**: "The Mere Urgency Effect", *Journal of Consumer Research* 45(3): 673–690. Authors: Meng Zhu (Johns Hopkins), Yang Yang (University of Florida), Christopher K. Hsee (University of Chicago).
- **Claim**: people pick urgent tasks over important ones even when urgency has no real consequence. In the authors' words, people act "as if pursuing an urgent task has its own appeal, independent of its objective consequence."
- **Design**: five experiments (labelled 1, 2A, 2B, 3, 4 in the paper). Participants chose between two equal tasks where one paid more (important) and the other had a fake short deadline (urgent). Both could be done in time.
- **Experiment 1**: 124 university students. Writing five product reviews for chocolate (Hershey's Kisses). The lower-paying task "expired" in 10 minutes, the better one in 24 hours. 13.3% chose the worse task in control vs 31.3% when it looked urgent.
- **Experiment 2A**: 203 Amazon MTurk workers, real money. 13.9% vs 35.3% chose the lower-paying task when it looked urgent. In their own words, 41.2% in the urgency condition mentioned expiration time (vs 1.0% in control), and fewer mentioned payoff (69.6% vs 82.2%). So urgency pulls attention away from outcomes.
- **Experiment 3 (outcome salience)**: reminding workers of each task's payoff cut the urgent-but-worse choice from 33.7% to 20.6%, close to control (15.0%, not a significant gap).
- **Experiment 4 (busyness)**: the effect showed up in people who felt busier, not in people who felt less busy. Over three rounds, 58.8% of workers in the urgency condition picked the low-payoff task at least once, vs 38.4% in control. The authors link this to real life, such as putting off a medical checkup.
- **Strength**: randomised lab and online experiments with clear manipulations, replicated across students and paid online workers. Published in a leading consumer research journal.
- **Limits**: small stakes (candy, cents); artificial tasks; short time frames; mostly US students and MTurk workers. It shows a bias, not that the matrix fixes it.
- **Why it matters here**: it's the best evidence for Eisenhower's worry, and it suggests a concrete fix: **write the outcome next to the task**. A matrix box that asks "why does this matter?" is doing what the reminder in experiment 3 did.

### Time management research in general

- **Aeon, Faber & Panaccio (2021)**, PLOS ONE, "Does time management work? A meta-analysis". 158 studies, 490 effect sizes, 53,957 participants. Time management correlates moderately with job performance (r ≈ .25), more strongly with wellbeing and life satisfaction, and negatively with distress (r ≈ −.36). It tracks strongly with conscientiousness (r ≈ .45). Caveats: mostly cross-sectional, training content is poorly described, and the authors note time management can be a function of socioeconomic advantage.
- **Claessens, van Eerde, Rutte & Roe (2007)**, *Personnel Review* 36(2): 255–276. Review of 32 empirical studies (1982–2004). Time management behaviours link to perceived control of time, job satisfaction and health, and to lower stress. The link to performance is unclear. Training improves time management skills but doesn't automatically improve performance.

### Planning research that supports Quadrant II scheduling

- **Gollwitzer (1999)**, *American Psychologist* 54: 493–503. "Implementation intentions" (if-then plans: "When X happens, I'll do Y") help people start and follow through.
- **Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006)**, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38: 69–119. Meta-analysis of 94 tests: if-then plans have a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (d = .65). Covey's "schedule it" for Q2 is a form of this.
- **Masicampo & Baumeister (2011)**, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. Making a specific plan for an unfinished goal stopped it from intruding on other tasks. Relevant to "Plan: when?".

### Claimed versus supported

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| People put urgent before important | **Supported** in lab (Zhu et al. 2018) |
| Reminding yourself of outcomes reduces the bias | **Supported** in lab (Zhu et al. 2018, exp. 3) |
| Scheduling a specific time helps important things happen | **Supported** (implementation intentions) |
| Time management practices go with better wellbeing | **Supported, correlational** (Aeon et al. 2021) |
| More Q2 time reduces Q1 crises over time | **Claimed**; plausible, not tested |
| The 2×2 matrix improves productivity or wellbeing | **Not tested**; no trial found |
| Q4 is "waste" | **Claimed**; ignores rest and recovery |

## 17. Benefits

- Notices the pull of fake urgency. **Evidenced** (the bias is real; noticing it is a reasonable step).
- Writing why a task matters lowers the urgency pull. **Evidenced** in lab.
- Scheduling Q2 tasks makes them likelier to happen. **Evidenced** (if-then planning).
- Less stress and more sense of control. **Evidenced, correlational** (time management broadly).
- Fewer crises over time. **Claimed.**
- Clearer "no" to other people's minor emergencies. **Claimed.**
- Fast: a whole day sorted in minutes. **Claimed** (common report).
- Makes values visible in daily choices. **Claimed.**

## 18. Pitfalls

| Problem | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Everything lands in Q1 | Job or life really is reactive; or fear labels everything urgent | Ask "what happens if this waits a week?" Cap Q1 at 3 lines. Move the rest to Q2 with a date |
| "Important" is fuzzy | No values written down | Write 3–5 roles or values once. Importance = serves one of them |
| No one to delegate to | Most people aren't managers | Rename Q3 "Hand off or batch": batch it, shorten it, or say no |
| Sorting replaces doing | The grid feels productive | Limit to 2 minutes; only sort new items |
| Q2 never happens | Nothing forces it | Give Q2 one line with a time: "when: __" |
| Guilt about Q4 | "Delete" and "waste" framing | Call it "Let go". Rest that helps is Q2 |
| Binary boxes feel wrong | Urgency and importance are scales | Allow a mark on the line; or rank within a box |
| Ignores energy | Two axes don't include spoons | Add a spoon cost next to tasks; pick Q2 items that fit the day's energy |
| Ignores dependencies | Some tasks unblock others | Mark "unblocks" tasks with an arrow and move them up |
| Colour-dependent templates | Online designs lean on colour | Use icons and position |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD**: time blindness makes urgency the loudest signal, so the mere urgency effect hits hard. Keep the grid tiny (2 lines per box). Put Q2 items on a clock time, not "sometime". Use body doubling or a timer for Q2. Write the reason next to each Q2 item ("so I don't run out of meds").
- **Autism and sensory needs**: the rules are clear, but "important" is vague. Define it once in writing (roles or values list). Predictable layout, same place every day. No colour or busy borders.
- **Chronic illness and low spoons**: the real limit is energy. Add spoon costs; on low days, Q1 only plus the smallest Q2 (a refill call). Covey's "sharpen the saw" (rest, care) belongs in Q2, not Q4.
- **Depression**: everything can feel unimportant, or everything urgent. Start with one Q2 item under 15 minutes. Skip Q4 entirely; don't audit yourself.
- **Anxiety**: anxiety makes things feel urgent. The "what happens if it waits a week?" question is a gentle reality check. Keep a "not today" list instead of a delete box.
- **Trauma**: hypervigilance treats everything as Q1. Avoid shaming words. Make "rest" an explicit Q2 item. Let the grid be optional on hard days.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia**: icons for boxes (! o > ~), short labels, big boxes, checkboxes instead of writing where possible.
- **Low vision**: 8.5×11 page, thick outer frame, 12pt+ labels, high contrast, no grey text.
- **Motor or hand pain**: fewer lines; tick boxes; sticky notes you move instead of rewriting; voice capture then sort.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people**: HRT refills, name and document changes, and insurance appeals are classic Q2 items that turn into Q1 crises when left. Put them in Q2 with dates. Dysphoria-heavy days need a low-energy version (Q1 + care only).
- **Shift workers**: urgency is set by the rota. Treat the shift as fixed Q1 and plan Q2 around days off; plan by week, not by day.
- **Low income**: scarcity makes real urgency constant, and "delegate" means paying someone. Drop the verbs; keep the Q2 line for prevention that saves money (bills before late fees, refills before running out).

## 21. Combining

- **With GTD** ([getting-things-done-paper.md](getting-things-done-paper.md)): GTD captures and organises; the matrix picks from the next-actions list on a crowded day. David Allen doesn't recommend a matrix; his "priority" criterion comes last after context, time and energy. Use the matrix only at the weekly review to choose Q2 projects.
- **With Bullet Journal** ([bullet-journal.md](bullet-journal.md)): sort the daily log's open tasks into the grid during migration. Or mark tasks with a small Q-number instead of drawing a grid.
- **With Hobonichi or a day-per-page planner** ([hobonichi-techo.md](hobonichi-techo.md)): a tiny 2×2 at the top of the page.
- **With a five-year diary** ([five-year-diary.md](five-year-diary.md)): at month end, write one line: "This month's Q2 win".
- **With spoons and care check-ins**: pick the Q2 item that fits today's spoons.

## 23. Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Urgent** | Time-sensitive; demands attention soon |
| **Important** | Serves your goals, values, health, people or duties |
| **Quadrant I (Q1)** | Urgent and important: crises, deadlines |
| **Quadrant II (Q2)** | Important, not urgent: prevention, planning, relationships, rest |
| **Quadrant III (Q3)** | Urgent, not important: interruptions, some messages |
| **Quadrant IV (Q4)** | Neither: trivia, busywork |
| **Eisenhower Matrix / Box / Method** | The 2×2 grid, named after Eisenhower's 1954 quote |
| **Time Management Matrix** | Covey's name for the grid (1989) |
| **Big rocks** | The few important things scheduled first (*First Things First*) |
| **Sharpen the saw** | Covey's Habit 7: renewing body, mind, heart, spirit |
| **Roles** | The parts of life you plan by (worker, friend, self) |
| **Weekly compass** | Covey's weekly roles-and-goals plan |
| **Fourth generation** | Covey's term for role- and Q2-based self-management |
| **Mere urgency effect** | The bias to pick urgent tasks even when they pay less (Zhu et al. 2018) |
| **Spurious urgency** | A deadline that feels real but has no real consequence |
| **Outcome salience** | Keeping a task's result in view; reduces the urgency bias |
| **Implementation intention** | An if-then plan: "When X, I'll do Y" |
| **Delegate** | Hand a task to someone else |
| **Batch** | Group small tasks into one time slot |
| **Tyranny of the urgent** | Hummel's 1967 phrase for urgency crowding out importance |

## 24. FAQ

**Did Eisenhower invent the matrix?**
No. He quoted the idea in 1954 and credited an unnamed former college president. Covey drew the grid in 1989. The "Eisenhower" name was attached later.

**Did Covey credit Eisenhower?**
**(Unconfirmed.)** The 7 Habits chapter presents the matrix as Covey's own. Whether it cites Eisenhower couldn't be checked from a primary source for this doc.

**Does it work?**
The bias it targets is real in lab studies. Scheduling important tasks helps them happen. But no study tested the grid itself.

**What if everything is urgent?**
Then your problem is load, not sorting. Ask "what happens if this waits a week?", cap Q1 at three, and write down what's being dropped.

**What if I have no one to delegate to?**
Most people don't. Batch it, shrink it, set a time for it, or say no.

**Is resting Q4?**
No. Rest that restores you is Q2. Q4 is only stuff you don't want and don't need.

**Daily or weekly?**
Covey meant weekly planning with a daily check. A small daily grid is fine; the weekly Q2 plan matters more.

**How many items per box?**
Three or four. If a box is fuller, it's a list, not a decision.

**Can I use it with spoons?**
Yes. Write a spoon cost next to each task. On low days: Q1 plus one small Q2.

**Is colour needed?**
No. Use position and icons. It prints better and is calmer.

### d. Proposed method layout: "Eisenhower" (planned)

1. `sky` (Moon, sun & season)
2. `events` (Events)
3. `matrix` (Four boxes: Now / Plan / Hand off / Let go, n=2, when=on)
4. `top` (Matters, not urgent, n=1, with 15-min bubbles)

### e. Don't adopt

- **"Delete" and "Waste" labels**: shame-y; rest gets misfiled. Use "Let go".
- **"Delegate" as the default**: most users can't. Use "Hand off or batch".
- **Colour-coded quadrants**: the books are black and white; colour also adds stimulation.
- **Daily time logs**: heavy, and they turn journaling into auditing.
- **Due-date reminders on the e-ink companion**: that's notifications and manufactured urgency, the thing the method warns against.
- **Mission-statement pages every month**: too much; once a year in the Keeper at most.
