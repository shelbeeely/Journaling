# Expressive writing (the Pennebaker paradigm)

Research doc for Journalwright Studio. Sources checked 2026-09-28. Anything marked **[uncertain]** came from memory or a source I could not open; verify before relying on it.

**Health note.** This doc describes a research procedure and what studies found. It is not treatment. If you are in crisis, use your safety plan ([safety-planning.md](safety-planning.md)) or call or text 988 (US).

## 1. At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Creator** | James W. Pennebaker (psychologist, University of Texas at Austin), first study with Sandra Beall |
| **Year** | 1986 (first study); the paradigm has been repeated in 200+ studies since |
| **Type** | Writing practice (a time-limited research protocol, not an open-ended journaling style) |
| **Time per day** | 15-20 minutes of writing, plus about 10 minutes to settle afterward |
| **Time per week** | Standard dose is 3-5 sessions in total, usually on consecutive days. That is one block of roughly 1-2.5 hours in total including settling time, once |
| **Cost** | Free. A notebook and a pen |
| **Formats and sizes** | Any. Handwriting, typing and speaking all work about equally in the meta-analysis |
| **Best for** | Making sense of one stressful or unresolved experience; people who tend to bottle things up; a short structured exercise instead of an ongoing habit |
| **Not ideal for** | Acute crisis; the first weeks after a fresh trauma; people who ruminate without stepping back; anyone who wants a daily habit (this is a course, not a routine) |
| **Learning curve** | Very low. One instruction, one rule ("keep writing") |
| **Supplies** | Pen and paper (or a keyboard), a timer, a private spot, a plan for the 10 minutes afterward |
| **Official site** | No single official site. Pennebaker's lab pages and the LIWC site are the nearest thing; I did not open them this session |
| **Evidence level** | **Some studies** (big literature, small average effects). Strong only for a narrow question: it is safe enough for most people and helps some. Not established as a treatment for depression or anxiety |

## 2. Summary

Expressive writing is a short experiment that turned into a method. You write, continuously and privately, about your deepest thoughts and feelings on one difficult experience, for 15-20 minutes on each of about four days. You do not share it and nobody grades it. Pennebaker and Beall (1986) found that students who did this went to the health centre less in the following months. Since then researchers have run hundreds of trials. The honest summary is: on average the effect is real but small (Frattaroli 2006: r = .075, roughly d = .15), it is bigger in some conditions and for some outcomes, it feels worse for an hour before it might help, and it does not reliably reduce depression or anxiety in meta-analyses of healthy people (Reinhold 2018; the NIHR review of long-term conditions, Nyssen 2016). A structured form of it, Written Exposure Therapy, does work as a clinician-led treatment for PTSD. For a journal like Journalwright Studio it fits best as an occasional, opt-in, four-day "write it out" spread, never as a daily default, and always with a stop rule and an aftercare step.

## 3. History and origin

### The person
James W. Pennebaker is a social and health psychologist. He spent much of his career at Southern Methodist University and then the University of Texas at Austin **[uncertain on the exact dates; I did not open a biography page]**. His early interest was inhibition: the idea that holding back thoughts and feelings about a big event is physical work that wears the body down.

### Cultural context
The idea that "getting it off your chest" is healthy is old (catharsis, confession, Freud and Breuer). Pennebaker's contribution was to test it with randomisation and a control that wrote about something dull. That made the claim testable, and it also produced an awkward finding: the writing does not work by venting. Baikie and Wilhelm (2005) note there is little support for catharsis, since writing only about emotion was less useful than writing about the event plus the emotion, and the benefit did not track how upset people got.

### What the creator says
The standard instructions (below) are the closest thing to a creator quote. Pennebaker's 2018 paper describes the "scientific and personal backstories" of the 1997 review (abstract only). I did not find a quotable line from him I could verify, so this doc quotes none.

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- **The problem it solves.** An unresolved experience keeps coming back as intrusive thoughts. Holding it in takes effort. Putting it into words might reduce that effort and organise the memory.
- **Facts plus feelings.** Both are needed. The 1986 group that wrote only facts or only emotions did not show the doctor-visit drop. (Frattaroli 2006)
- **It is private.** The writing is for you. Studies with more privacy had larger effects. (Frattaroli 2006)
- **It is time-boxed.** A few days, a fixed number of minutes. Baikie and Wilhelm advise capping at 20 minutes so it does not become overwhelming.
- **Keep going.** The single rule: once you begin, keep writing until time is up. Do not fix spelling or grammar.
- **Short-term cost, possible later gain.** Feeling worse right after is expected. That distress was unrelated to later benefit in Smyth (1998).
- **It is not a substitute for care.** Baikie and Wilhelm: it should be an adjunct, not a replacement for medical or psychological treatment.

## 5. Core components

| Element | What it is |
|---|---|
| The instruction | A fixed paragraph that sets the topic and the one rule |
| The session | 15-20 minutes of continuous writing |
| The dose | 3-5 sessions, usually on consecutive days |
| The topic | A trauma or an important emotional issue, chosen by the writer |
| Privacy | The writing is not read or graded |
| The control | In research, neutral writing (a room, plans for the day). Not part of the personal practice |
| Aftercare | About 10 minutes to compose yourself (Baikie & Wilhelm) |
| Follow-up | An optional look-back weeks later, in your own review |
| Word-use readout | LIWC counts, used by researchers. Not needed at home |

### 5.1 The instruction
Baikie and Wilhelm (2005, Box 1) give the typical wording:

> For the next 4 days, I would like you to write your very deepest thoughts and feelings about the most traumatic experience of your entire life or an extremely important emotional issue that has affected you and your life. In your writing, I'd like you to really let go and explore your deepest emotions and thoughts. You might tie your topic to your relationships with others, including parents, lovers, friends or relatives; to your past, your present or your future; or to who you have been, who you would like to be or who you are now. You may write about the same general issues or experiences on all days of writing or about different topics each day. All of your writing will be completely confidential. Don't worry about spelling, grammar or sentence structure. The only rule is that once you begin writing, you continue until the time is up.

Notes:
- "Most traumatic experience of your entire life" is the research wording. It is not required. For self-directed use, "an experience that still weighs on you" is a safer entry (see section 18).
- Baikie and Wilhelm say to let the patient choose the topic and not to specify a trauma. Studies that assigned the specific event people were selected for more often found nothing (same source).

### 5.2 The session
Continuous writing, no editing, no rereading mid-way. Same time and place each day is typical but not a proven requirement.

### 5.3 The dose
Frattaroli (2006): studies with at least three sessions, and sessions of at least 15 minutes, had larger effects. Spacing (daily versus weekly) was not significant there. Guo (2023) found shorter gaps (1-3 days) did better in studies with long follow-ups.

### 5.4 Topic and framing
You may repeat the same topic or change it. Switching-instruction studies had larger effects in Frattaroli. Narrative that becomes more organised over the days is the pattern linked to benefit (section 16).

### 5.5 Aftercare
Baikie and Wilhelm suggest 30 minutes in total: 20 to write, 10 to compose yourself. Add a contact number in case of distress, and tell people they can stop at any time.

## 6. Setting it up

**First setup (10 minutes, once):**
1. Choose a notebook you will keep private, or a page in a book that stays home. In Journalwright Studio that means the Keeper (the yearly book that stays home), not the scannable day pages (see section 25).
2. Pick a private place where you will not be interrupted. Privacy is one of the moderators.
3. Pick four days in a row when the next day is not high-stakes. Not before a hard shift or a court date.
4. Pick a timer (15-20 min). Choose 15 if you have low spoons.
5. Write your stop rule where you will see it (section 7).
6. Write your aftercare list: three things you will do afterward (water, walk outside, text a person).
7. Write who you will contact if it goes badly, and your crisis line.
8. Decide in advance what happens to the pages: keep, tear up, or store. The research says keep it private. Either is fine.

**First day:** read the instruction once. Set the timer. Write. Stop when it rings, even mid-sentence. Do the aftercare.

**First week:** four writing days, then a rest day, then a five-minute look-back (section 8).

## 7. Daily practice

Only on writing days (not a daily habit).

| Step | Time | What |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Check in | 1 min | Rate distress 0-10. Note sleep, food, meds. If above 7 or you are unwell, skip today |
| 2. Prepare | 1 min | Pen, timer, water. Read the instruction |
| 3. Write | 15-20 min | Continuous. Facts and feelings. Do not edit |
| 4. Stop | 0 min | When the timer ends, stop, even mid-sentence. Do not extend on a hard day. Baikie and Wilhelm cap sessions at 20 minutes; they allow continuing only if you choose and it is feasible, so treat that as the exception |
| 5. Settle | 10 min | Breathe, drink water, stand up, move, look outside |
| 6. Check out | 1 min | Rate distress 0-10 again. Rate "how meaningful was that" 0-10 |
| 7. Return | Rest of the day | Do a normal, gentle activity. Avoid big decisions or hard conversations for a few hours |

**Stop rule.** Stop writing if you feel dissociated, panicky in a way you cannot settle, or unsafe. Stand up, ground yourself (name five things you can see), and use your safety plan. Baikie and Wilhelm advise that people can stop at any time.

**Prompt ideas for each day** (all optional; the standard protocol lets you write the same topic four times):
- Day 1: what happened, and what you felt.
- Day 2: what it did to your relationships and your sense of yourself.
- Day 3: how it connects to your past and future.
- Day 4: what you understand now, and what you will carry forward.

This day-by-day arc is a common adaptation and is not the standard protocol **[uncertain: it is a teaching convention, not something I traced to a study]**.

## 8. Rhythms

- **Daily:** none on non-writing days. The value is the dose, not a streak.
- **Weekly:** in the week of writing, one look-back page on day 5.
- **Monthly:** decide whether another four-day block is warranted. There is no proven repeat schedule. A booster session in Gortner et al. (2006) did not add benefit.
- **Quarterly:** at most one block per quarter for most people **[uncertain: my suggestion, not evidence-based]**.
- **Yearly:** the "Looking back" pages are a natural place to note "I wrote about X in March, and here is how it looks now."
- **Delayed effect.** Benefits, when they appear, often show at follow-up and not right away. Guo (2023) says the effect emerged after a delay. Lumley et al. (2012) note improvements often show a month or more later. So check in at 4-6 weeks, not at 4 days.

## 10. Worked examples

All invented sample content.

### 10.1 A day (Day 2)
```
Before: 5    Sleep ok. Ate lunch. No shift tomorrow.
Timer: 15 minutes.

[writing, excerpt] "I keep saying it was fine, but it was not fine.
The thing with my old manager, the way they made me redo the
schedule in front of everyone. I think what I felt was shame, and
under that, anger. I realise I never told anyone because I thought
I should have handled it. Because I froze, I have been afraid to
speak up ever since. Maybe that is why I do not ask for help..."

After: 6    Meaningful: 7
Aftercare: water, walked to the mailbox, texted a friend "long day".
```

Note the words "realise" and "because": insight and causal words are the ones linked to benefit (Baikie & Wilhelm).

### 10.2 A week
| Day | Plan | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Mon | Day 1, 15 min | Before 4, after 7. Tired. Walk. |
| Tue | Day 2 | Before 5, after 6. Meaningful 7 |
| Wed | Day 3 | Before 4, after 5. Wrote about the future |
| Thu | Day 4 | Before 3, after 4. Felt lighter |
| Fri | Rest | Nothing |
| Sat | Rest | Look-back page: three sentences |
| Sun | Rest | No writing |

### 10.3 A month
- Week 1: four writing days.
- Week 2: rest, notice sleep and mood.
- Week 3: normal life. Check whether the memory bothers you differently.
- Week 4: 4-6 week follow-up rating. Decide: another block on a different topic, or stop.

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Who | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Standard paradigm | Pennebaker & Beall 1986 | Trauma or important emotional issue, 15-20 min, 3-5 sessions |
| Facts-only or emotions-only | Pennebaker & Beall 1986 | Control conditions. Combined was better |
| Best possible self (BPS) | King 2001 | Write about your ideal future for 20 min over 4 days (section 11.1) |
| Benefit finding | King & Miner 2000; Stanton et al. 2002 | Write about positive aspects of a hard event **[uncertain: I opened King 2001, which cites King and Miner]** |
| Written Exposure Therapy | Sloan and colleagues | Clinician-led PTSD treatment, 5 sessions of 30 min (section 11.2) |
| Self-distancing writing | Park, Ayduk & Kross 2016 | Writing led to more self-distancing, which mediated later benefits |
| Cognitive-reappraisal or perspective-switching versions | Seih, Chung & Pennebaker 2011 (title seen only) | Change perspective across sessions **[uncertain: not opened]** |
| Positive-events writing | Burton & King 2008/2009 (titles seen) | Writing about intensely positive experiences **[uncertain: not opened]** |
| Adolescent versions | Travagin et al. 2015 | Same idea, small effects, protocol may need adjusting |
| Online or typed | many | Frattaroli found no significant difference by mode |

### 11.1 Best possible self (King 2001)
Instruction (verbatim from the paper): "Think about your life in the future. Imagine that everything has gone as well as it possibly could. You have worked hard and succeeded at accomplishing all of your life goals. Think of this as the realization of all of your life dreams. Now, write about what you imagined."

Design: 81 undergraduates wrote 20 minutes a day for 4 days about trauma, best possible self, both (trauma the first two days, BPS the last two), or a neutral control. Writing about life goals was significantly less upsetting than writing about trauma and raised subjective well-being at 3 weeks. At 5 months, trauma, BPS and combined groups all had less illness (health-centre visits) than controls. A 2019 meta-analysis (Carrillo et al.: 29 studies, N = 2,909, mostly students) found effects versus controls of d = .51 on positive affect, .33 on optimism, .33 on well-being, .19 on negative affect and .12 on depression, with no study meeting all quality criteria and no clinical samples.

### 11.2 Written Exposure Therapy (WET)
A brief PTSD treatment, not a self-help exercise. In the Sloan et al. (2022) trial, 169 service members had 5 weekly sessions (about an hour the first time, about 45 minutes after), writing for 30 minutes each time, first about the details of the event and the thoughts and feelings during it, later about how it affected their lives. It was non-inferior to Cognitive Processing Therapy, with lower dropout (23.5% versus 45.2%). In a 2023 trial with 178 veterans it was non-inferior to Prolonged Exposure, with 12.5% versus 35.6% dropout. This is done with a trained clinician. It is included to show that a structured, supported version of writing has stronger evidence than the do-it-yourself version.

## 12. Community practice

I did not survey forums for this doc, so this section is limited.

- **What people say they do** **[uncertain: from general awareness, not from sources I opened]:** many therapists give the exercise as homework. Writing communities often blend it with morning pages ([morning-pages.md](morning-pages.md)) or free writing, which is not the same thing: expressive writing is time-limited and about one topic.
- **Common customisations:** typing in a locked doc, writing then burning or shredding, writing a letter you do not send, splitting the four days into past, present, future and lessons.
- **Aesthetic versus minimalist:** the research protocol is as minimal as it gets. No decoration, no prompts, no templates. Any bullet-journal styling would be additive and could distract.
- **Warning from the field:** the literature itself cautions against routine or indiscriminate recommendation (Lumley et al. 2012). If you see "everyone should do this," treat that as marketing.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Pen.** Whatever flows without effort. Handwriting is common in research, but mode did not significantly matter in Frattaroli (2006). Baikie and Wilhelm suggest offering handwriting or computer.
- **Paper.** A bound notebook you can keep private. If you may want to destroy it, use loose paper.
- **Timer.** A kitchen timer or phone (phone in do-not-disturb; the point is no interruptions).
- **Privacy.** A closed door. Headphones with quiet music if the space is noisy.
- **Aftercare kit.** Water, a snack, a coat for a walk, a friend's number.
- **Lock.** A locked note app if you type. Do not use a shared or synced account you do not control.

---

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps and templates.** Any text editor works. Studies have used email and web forms. Frattaroli found no significant difference by mode (handwriting, typing or talking).
- **E-ink (reMarkable, Kindle Scribe, Boox).** Handwriting on e-ink gives paper-like feel with easy deletion. The deletion option may help people who fear being read.
- **Scanning.** Do not scan these pages into anything cloud-synced. In Journalwright Studio, scan zones and SEND TO strips are for pages that are meant to be sent. These pages are not.
- **Gained by digital:** privacy via lock, no need to store a physical record of hard content, word count.
- **Lost:** the physical release of closing the book; the option to burn it. Also the temptation to reread and edit on screen.
- **Speaking.** Talking into a recorder was used in some studies, for example Lyubomirsky et al. (2006): worst experiences were helped by both writing and talking relative to just thinking.

## 16. Evidence and research

### 16.1 Studies

| Study | Sample | Finding | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pennebaker & Beall 1986 | College students (I did not verify N) | Trauma writers made fewer health-centre visits over following months | Students, single lab. Abstract not opened |
| Petrie et al. 1995 | 40 medical students, hepatitis B vaccine | Higher antibody levels at 4 and 6 months; fewer circulating T helper cells and basophils right after writing | Small, one immune outcome |
| Smyth 1998 (meta-analysis) | 13 studies of healthy people | d = 0.47 overall (as reported by Baikie & Wilhelm); health improved in 4 outcome types but not health behaviours. | Smyth et al. 1999 | 112 patients (asthma n=61, RA n=51) | At 4 months, lung function rose (FEV1 63.9% to 76.3% of predicted) in asthma; RA disease severity fell 28%. 47.1% of writers versus 24.3% of controls had clinically relevant improvement | Volunteers, 4 months, cannot say if it lasts. ### 16.2 What is supported and what is not

**Reasonably supported**
- A small average benefit on a mix of health and functioning outcomes (Frattaroli 2006).
- Immediate increase in distress that does not predict outcome (Smyth 1998; Baikie & Wilhelm).
- Structured, clinician-led writing helps PTSD (van Emmerik 2013; Sloan 2022, 2023).
- Benefits in inflammatory arthritis at short follow-up (Nyssen 2016), with earlier mixed results.

**Not supported or contradicted**
- That it reliably lowers depression in healthy adults (Reinhold 2018; Nyssen 2016).
- That it is cathartic. Writing only emotion did not help as much as facts plus emotion.
- That disinhibition is the sole mechanism. Writing about imagined traumas also helped in one study, and prior disclosure did not matter (Baikie & Wilhelm).
- That everyone benefits. Effects differ by design and person.

### 16.3 Mechanisms
Baikie and Wilhelm's Box 4 rates them:
- **Catharsis:** unlikely.
- **Confronting inhibited emotion:** may reduce physiological stress, but unlikely to be the only explanation.
- **Cognitive processing (building a coherent narrative):** likely. Evidence: narrative formation and coherence appeared necessary for benefit (Smyth et al. 2001, cited there); working memory capacity increased (Klein & Boals 2001, cited there).
- **Repeated exposure:** mixed. Sessions are short compared with the 45-90 minutes thought needed for habituation, and people benefit whether they repeat one event or switch. Sloan and Marx's work supports some exposure role.

**LIWC findings.** LIWC counts words in categories (82 in the 2001 version, per Baikie and Wilhelm). The most consistent finding: people whose health improved used more positive-emotion words, a moderate number of negative-emotion words, and an increasing number of "cognitive mechanism" words: insight (understand, realise) and causal (because, reason). Pennebaker and Seagal (1999) summarise this as the formation of a narrative. Session-to-session change in pronoun use also tracked improvement (Baikie & Wilhelm, citing Pennebaker 2002). Park et al. (2016) linked more causation words, fewer negative-emotion words and fewer "I" words to self-distancing.

Caution: these are correlations. Writing "because" more does not by itself cause benefit. Do not coach yourself to use certain words.

### 16.4 Moderators (Frattaroli 2006)
Larger effects: participants with physical health problems, or with a history of trauma or stressors; non-students; writing at home; writing in private; more men; fewer participants; paid participants; follow-up under 1 month; at least 3 sessions; at least 15 minutes per session; more recent events; previously undisclosed topics; directed questions or specific examples; instructions about switching topics.
Not significant: age, ethnicity, education, spacing, valence of topic, warning participants in advance, mode of writing.
Baikie and Wilhelm also note larger benefit for people high in alexithymia, and that findings for emotional health are less consistent than those for physical health.

### 16.5 Limits
- Most trials are students.
- Meta-analyses vary with which studies are included; unpublished studies have smaller effects (Frattaroli).
- Effect sizes are small. Frattaroli's own binomial display: 54% improved in treatment versus 46% in controls.
- Few studies track harm rigorously.
- Pavlacic et al. (2019) meta-analysis on PTSD symptoms, growth and quality of life exists but I could not open it, so I do not quote its numbers. **[uncertain]**

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---|---|
| Picking the worst event first | Start with something still hard but manageable. The research wording ("most traumatic experience") is for research |
| Feeling worse and quitting | Expect it. Aftercare and time-limit help. If distress does not settle within a few hours, or you feel unsafe, stop and use support |
| Ruminating in circles | Aim for a story: what happened, what I felt, what it meant. Try writing from a step back (Park et al. 2016) |
| Rereading and editing | The rule is to keep writing. Do not reread during the session |
| Doing it too soon after a fresh event | Frattaroli found more recent events had larger effects in studies, but clinical guidance warns about acute trauma. I did not find a source that draws a clear line. Talk to a clinician if it is very recent **[uncertain]** |
| Doing it alone with severe trauma | Use a clinician. Written Exposure Therapy has trained delivery |
| Making it a daily habit | It is a course, not a routine. Stop after four days |
| Writing about a positive thing and overanalysing it | Lyubomirsky (2006): analysing a happy day lowered well-being. Savour, do not dissect |
| Sharing before you are ready | Keep it private unless you choose. In studies, the privacy of the session mattered |
| Risk of being found | Store or destroy it. Do not leave it in a shared space |
| Expecting instant relief | Effects often appear weeks later (Guo 2023; Lumley 2012) |
| Believing it must be trauma | The paradigm covers "an extremely important emotional issue." That could be a conflict, a loss or a decision |
| Harm in specific groups | Baikie and Wilhelm list studies where it was detrimental for adult survivors of childhood abuse (Batten 2002, cited there) and for eight Vietnam veterans with PTSD (Gidron 1996, cited there). Frattaroli notes one study in which people selected for negative body image got worse. Treat these as reasons for care, not proof of harm for everyone |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD.** Use a timer. The structure (one rule, fixed minutes) is helpful. If four days is a lot, do 15 minutes. Link: [adhd-journaling.md](adhd-journaling.md).
- **Autism and sensory needs.** Choose a quiet, low-light, low-smell space. Predictable time and paper. Consider typed if handwriting is stressful. Link: [autistic-sensory-journaling.md](autistic-sensory-journaling.md).
- **Chronic illness and low spoons.** 10-15 minutes, spread as you can. Frattaroli found spacing did not matter, but Guo (2023) found 1-3 day gaps did better in long follow-up studies, so keep gaps short if you can, and rest rather than skip the aftercare. Do it on a better day. Skip if flaring. RA and asthma trials were in ill people, but home-based RA writing had no benefit (Baikie and Wilhelm).
- **Depression and anxiety.** Reinhold and Nyssen found no reliable effect on these. Do not rely on it as treatment. If you do it, use aftercare and tell someone. If low mood is severe, speak with a clinician first.
- **Trauma.** See "Trauma-informed guidance" below.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia.** Typing or speaking is fine (no significant difference by mode). Turn off spellcheck if it distracts. Dictation into a private recorder is an option, but treat the audio as sensitive.
- **Low vision.** Bold pen, high-contrast paper, large type, or voice. Use a big-print notebook.
- **Motor or hand pain.** Type or dictate. Shorten to the time you can sustain.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people.** Writing about dysphoria, misgendering or family rejection is legitimate content, but the exposure risk is real. Choose one thread, not the whole history. Pair with a plan for the next day. See [gender-euphoria-and-hrt-log.md](gender-euphoria-and-hrt-log.md) for a balancing practice (euphoria logging). I did not find a study of expressive writing specifically in trans populations **[uncertain / gap]**.
- **Shift workers.** Do not write right before sleep or a shift if it stirs you up. Choose days off. Keep the sleep window intact.
- **Low income.** Free. Paper from a used notebook. No app needed.

### Trauma-informed guidance
This is a summary of what the sources support plus common sense. It is not clinical advice.
1. **You choose.** Topic, place, amount. Do not let anyone require it (studies that paid participants and gave specific examples had larger effects, but that is research, not a reason to push a person).
2. **You can stop.** Baikie and Wilhelm advise telling people they can stop at any time and providing contact numbers.
3. **Limit the dose.** Maximum 20 minutes per session (same source).
4. **Aftercare, always.** 10 minutes to settle.
5. **Pick a manageable target.** Do not start with the worst memory.
6. **Not a replacement for care.** Use as an adjunct (Baikie and Wilhelm).
7. **Higher-risk groups.** If you have PTSD, a history of childhood abuse, active suicidal thoughts or dissociation, talk to a clinician first. WET exists precisely because supported, structured trauma writing works better than going alone.
8. **Privacy.** Do not put it on pages that get scanned or sent.
9. **Signals to stop.** Flashbacks, feeling unreal, panic that does not settle, thoughts of self-harm. Then use the safety plan: [safety-planning.md](safety-planning.md).

## 21. Combining

- **With a daily check-in or care log.** A distress rating before and after is the only tracking needed.
- **With gratitude or "went well" writing.** Use these on the evenings after a writing block. It is a gentle bridge back. Link: [five-minute-journal.md](five-minute-journal.md).
- **With Stoic reflection.** After the four days, use a distance question: what would I tell a friend? Ties to self-distancing findings. Link: [stoic-journaling.md](stoic-journaling.md).
- **With morning pages.** Keep them separate. Morning pages are open-ended and daily; this is time-boxed and topical. Link: [morning-pages.md](morning-pages.md).
- **With a safety plan.** Put the plan on the same page as the start card. Link: [safety-planning.md](safety-planning.md).
- **With habit dots.** Use one dot per writing day for the four days only, not as a streak. Link: [habit-tracking.md](habit-tracking.md).
- **With therapy.** Bring the pages if you choose. Baikie and Wilhelm describe using it as a short-term start to dialogue, best with a follow-up appointment.
- **With dream journaling.** Nightmares can be a topic; see [dream-journal.md](dream-journal.md) for Imagery Rehearsal Therapy, which is a different, evidence-backed method for nightmares.

3. "How does this connect to who I was, who I am, who I want to be?"

7. Day 1: the event and the feelings.
8. Day 2: what it did to my relationships.
9. Day 3: how it links to my past and my future.
10. Day 4: what I know now; what I carry forward.

11. "Imagine everything has gone as well as it possibly could..."
12. BPS: work, 20 min.
13. BPS: relationships.
14. BPS: body and health.

15. Before and after distress 0-10.
16. "How meaningful was this?" 0-10.

20. [ ] Water [ ] Move [ ] Outside [ ] Text someone [ ] Nap or rest [ ] Food.

## 23. Glossary

- **Alexithymia:** difficulty identifying and describing feelings. Associated with larger benefit in some studies (Baikie and Wilhelm).
- **Aftercare:** the settling routine after writing.
- **BPS (best possible self):** King's future-focused writing variant.
- **Catharsis:** the idea that venting releases emotion. Not supported as the mechanism.
- **CPT:** Cognitive Processing Therapy, a PTSD treatment.
- **Cognitive mechanism words:** LIWC category including insight and causal words.
- **Disinhibition:** letting out previously held-back thoughts and feelings.
- **d, g, r:** effect-size measures. d and g are standardised mean differences; r is a correlation. r = .075 is about d = .15.
- **Expressive writing (EW):** the Pennebaker paradigm.
- **Exposure:** repeated, safe contact with a distressing memory until it loses intensity.
- **Inhibition theory:** holding thoughts back is physiological work.
- **LIWC:** Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count, a dictionary-based word-counting program.
- **Moderator:** a variable that changes how big an effect is.
- **PE:** Prolonged Exposure, a PTSD treatment.
- **PTSD:** post-traumatic stress disorder.
- **RCT:** randomised controlled trial.
- **Self-distancing:** viewing an experience from an observer's perspective.
- **WET:** Written Exposure Therapy.

## 24. FAQ

**1. Do I have to write about the worst thing that ever happened?**
No. The research wording says that, but the same wording allows "an extremely important emotional issue." For self-directed use, choose something that weighs on you and that you can handle.

**2. Will it make me feel worse?**
Probably for a while. Studies show a short-term rise in distress and negative mood, which did not predict who benefited (Smyth 1998; Baikie and Wilhelm). If it does not settle or you feel unsafe, stop.

**3. Does it work?**
On average, a little. Frattaroli found r = .075 across 146 studies. It has been most reliable for physical-health and functioning outcomes, and less so for depression and anxiety in healthy adults (Reinhold 2018).

**4. Is it a substitute for therapy?**
No. Baikie and Wilhelm say to use it as an adjunct. If you have PTSD, a clinician-led form (Written Exposure Therapy) has trial evidence.

**5. Can I type it?**
Yes. Frattaroli found no significant difference between handwriting, typing and talking.

**6. Why four days?**
That was the original design. Frattaroli found at least three sessions and at least 15 minutes did better.

**7. Should I reread it?**
The protocol does not include rereading. Reading at a later date is your choice. Do it when calm.

**8. Should I share it?**
The research kept it private. Sharing is a separate decision. A therapist can help.

**9. Can I do it every day?**
It was designed as a short course. Daily open-ended writing is a different practice (see [morning-pages.md](morning-pages.md)).

**10. What if I cannot stop crying?**
Stop, ground yourself, drink water, contact your person, and use your plan. Speak to a clinician if this keeps happening.

**11. Does it help with sleep?**
One study cited by Baikie and Wilhelm found shorter sleep-onset latency in poor sleepers. Not a proven sleep treatment.

**12. Is this what a "brain dump" or free write is?**
No. The difference is the topic (emotional, personal), the framing (thoughts and feelings), and the limited dose.

### e. Don't adopt
- **On-device or in-editor text analysis of what someone writes** (LIWC-style scoring, or any AI feedback). Out of firmware scope, and the language markers are correlations.

- **Scan codes or SEND TO on writing pages.**
- **A prompt "engine"** pushing people to write. No notifications.
- **Showing example trauma text** in the editor sample data. Sample data is generic (`test.ics`).
- **Default-on.** It must be opt-in and framed as a research procedure, not a habit.

## 26. Open questions

- Where exactly the risks lie. Adverse events are under-reported in the trials I read. The 2016 NIHR abstract does not discuss harms.
- Whether it helps transgender or neurodivergent people specifically. I found no sources.
- Whether four days is a magic number or arbitrary. Meta-analyses suggest at least three sessions, at least 15 minutes.
- Whether the meta-analytic differences (Reinhold null, Guo small and significant) come from different inclusion rules. I did not reconcile them fully.
- Whether handwriting differs from typing in trauma-related outcomes. Frattaroli says no significant difference, Lumley et al. suggest handwriting has more support. Unresolved.
- Safe timing after a fresh trauma. No source I opened gives a clear rule.
