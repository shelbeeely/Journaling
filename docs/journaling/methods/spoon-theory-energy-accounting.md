# Spoon theory and energy accounting

> **Not medical advice.** This is a research summary for a journaling system. Pacing and energy plans for ME/CFS, long COVID,
> lupus and other conditions should be set up with your care team. Nothing here replaces that. If tracking makes you feel
> worse, or you notice a decline you can't explain, tell a clinician.

Research checked 2026-09-28. Uncertain points are marked **(uncertain)**. Sources are in section 28.

## 2. Summary

Spoon theory is a story. In 2003 Christine Miserandino, sitting in a diner with her best friend, gave her twelve spoons and asked
her to describe a day. Every task cost a spoon, and when the spoons ran out the day was over. It gave people with chronic illness
a quick way to explain limited, unpredictable energy, and it spread through disability and chronic illness communities until
"spoonie" became an identity and "I'm out of spoons" became shorthand. As a journaling tool it turns into a daily count: how many
spoons did I wake with, what did things cost, what did I have left. Its cousins tune the idea: fork theory (Jen Rose, 2018) is
about stacking stressors rather than energy, knife theory is about borrowing from tomorrow, and **energy accounting** (Maja Toudal,
developed with Tony Attwood) adds both deposits and withdrawals, a numbered balance and a daily aim of finishing at or above zero.
In medicine the same idea appears as **pacing** and **energy management**: NICE (2021) says people with ME/CFS should be helped
to plan activity and rest within their own fluctuating limits, with activity "never automatically increased", and should not be
offered graded exercise therapy. The honest evidence picture: spoons and energy accounting are respected, useful and mostly
anecdotal; pacing has research behind it but definitions vary; and every version works best when it stays gentle, flexible and
free of guilt.

## 3. History and origin

### Christine Miserandino and the diner (2003)

- **Who:** Christine Miserandino, an American writer, blogger, speaker and lupus advocate. She has lupus; in the essay she
  mentions a cane, medicines, and vomiting from illness. She runs **But You Don't Look Sick** (butyoudontlooksick.com).
- **When:** The essay is dated **2003** (© 2003 Christine Miserandino). Wikipedia and MEpedia agree on 2003.
- **The scene:** Late at night in a diner while both were in college, eating French fries with gravy. Her best friend and
  roommate watched her take medicine and asked "what it felt like to have Lupus and be sick": "not physically, but what it felt
  like to be me, to be sick." Christine grabbed every spoon on the table, "hell I grabbed spoons off of the other tables," and
  handed her twelve. Her friend asked for more; Christine said no.
- **The rules in the story:**
  - Healthy people start each day with an unlimited supply. She had to count.
  - Every task costs a spoon, including ones others never think about: getting up, showering, getting dressed, eating, standing
    on a train, typing too long.
  - "When your spoons are gone, they are gone." You can sometimes **borrow against tomorrow**, but tomorrow will be harder.
  - Some days you start with fewer spoons. You also can't predict when illness will take some away.
  - You keep **one spoon in reserve**, "in my pocket," for surprises.
  - The point isn't just limits: "The difference in being sick and being healthy is having to consciously think about things when
    the rest of the world doesn't have to."
- **Ending:** The friend cries and asks, "How do you do it? Do you really do this every day?" Christine hands her the spare spoon
  and says she has "been forced to think about everything I do," so she doesn't waste spoons, and "I chose to spend this time with
  you." She also writes that "my family and friends refer to spoons all the time. It has been a code word for what I can and
  cannot do."

### Spread

- The essay circulated on chronic-illness and autoimmune message boards through the 2000s. Wikipedia says the metaphor "expanded
  significantly within chronic illness communities," producing the identity term **spoonie**. MEpedia notes ME/CFS communities
  adopted it "to explain fluctuating energy levels" and to counter being seen as lazy.
- Charities and clinics now reprint the essay (for example Lymphoma Action reproduced it with permission for its 2020 conference).
- **Criticism.** Naomi Chainey (Sydney Morning Herald, 2016, "Stop appropriating the language that describes my illness") argued
  that non-disabled people were using "spoons" for everyday tiredness and that this marginalised people with invisible
  disabilities. MEpedia also notes there is no official guide for using spoons in ME/CFS, and that the metaphor "may not
  represent the impact of all symptoms."
- **Research mentions.** Recent studies mention spoon theory in passing: a 2026 pacing study calls it a "subjective
  self-management approach" where people "budget their perceived energy" (Barakou et al.), and long-COVID participants in a
  2025 trial said they had previously used it (McMullan et al.).

### Variants (community)

- **Fork theory** (Jen Rose, December 2018; credited to her husband's concept): forks are external stressors. "You know the
  phrase, 'Stick a fork in me, I'm done'... Fork Theory is that one has a Fork Limit." Small forks (hunger, a hangnail, an
  itchy tag), medium forks (a new bill, a diagnosis), large forks (emotional triggers). Unlike spoons, forks don't come back
  slowly with rest; they need time and recovery.
- **Knife theory:** two versions. Jen Rose (in a comment) uses knives for serious traumas needing active intervention; Terry
  Masson uses them for borrowing future spoons, "grabbing a knife by the blade" (per Wikipedia).
- **Matchstick theory:** energy as "finicky and unpredictable" (Wikipedia).
- **Cutlery drawer** (Ashley L. Peterson, Mental Health @ Home, 24 Nov 2020): whisk (anxiety and sensory overwhelm), meat
  tenderizer, turkey baster, pastry blender, corkscrew; a commenter proposed a ladle for replenishing activities. Playful, not
  research.

### Energy accounting (Maja Toudal, Tony Attwood)

- **Who:** Maja Toudal is an autistic (and ADHD) psychologist from Copenhagen, Denmark. Tony Attwood is a clinical psychologist in
  Brisbane, Australia, known for work on Asperger syndrome. energyaccounting.com says the method was clinically tested and
  refined for more than 10 years before the book.
- **When:** Toudal's method was in circulation by 2020 (a Middletown Centre for Autism post is dated June 2020); the book
  *Energy Accounting: Stress Management and Mental Health Monitoring for Autism and Related Conditions* (Jessica Kingsley
  Publishers, 256 pp., ISBN 9781787757745) came out **19 December 2024**. Attwood and Toudal recorded a training in February
  2026.
- **Idea:** "It uses the idea of a bank account, where the withdrawals and deposits are energy rather than money" (National
  Autistic Society, *Know yourself* guide).
- **Two lists:** things that use energy (withdrawals) and things that give it (deposits), weighted with numbers or colours. Aim
  to end the day on zero or a positive number (NAS), or, in Dr Alice Nicholls's version, to keep about 20–40% in reserve.

### Pacing and energy management (clinical)

- **Occupational therapy** has taught "energy conservation" for decades: the Royal College of Occupational Therapists' *Lift Up
  Your Everyday* uses **Pace, Plan, Prioritise**.
- **ME/CFS and PEM.** The CDC describes post-exertional malaise (PEM) as "the worsening of symptoms following even minor physical or
  mental exertion," typically 12 to 48 hours later, and says it "can be mitigated by activity management (pacing)." It uses the
  term **energy envelope**.
- **NICE NG206 (29 October 2021)** made energy management a core recommendation and withdrew graded exercise therapy (details
  in section 16).
- **Autistic burnout research.** Raymaker et al. (2020) define burnout as chronic life stress plus mismatched expectations
  without support, with exhaustion, loss of function and reduced tolerance to stimulus lasting three months or more.
  Participants described "having all of your internal resources exhausted beyond measure and being left with no clean-up crew."
  That is the problem energy accounting aims to prevent.

### Cultural context

Spoon theory arrived when blogs, message boards and then social media let sick people describe their lives to each other. It gave
a shared language before "disability justice" and "neurodiversity-affirming" became common phrases. Energy accounting sits in the
later neurodiversity movement: it treats energy as real, budgetable, and shaped by environment rather than willpower.

## 4. Philosophy and principles

**Stated goals**

- *Spoons:* help others understand; help you plan. "I wanted something for her to actually hold."
- *Energy accounting:* "reduces stress, boosts energy and enhances quality of life" (publisher's description); "stress management
  and mental health monitoring."
- *Pacing (NICE):* "use the amount of energy they have while reducing their risk of post-exertional malaise or worsening their
  symptoms by exceeding their limits."

**Core beliefs**

1. **Energy is finite and variable.** Not every day starts at the same number.
2. **Costs are personal.** A shower can cost one spoon or three. Sensory load, pain, masking and stress are costs.
3. **You are the expert.** NICE: people "are experts in judging their own limits."
4. **Rest is a deposit, not a failure.** And deposits differ: "you can be resting and replenishing one supply of energy while
   using up another" (Nicholls).
5. **Borrowing has interest.** Overdrawing today means a worse tomorrow.
6. **Plan with slack.** Keep a spoon in reserve; end the day at zero or above.
7. **Numbers serve you.** They are a tool for choices, not a grade.

**Problems it solves**

- Explaining an invisible limit to friends, family, employers.
- Deciding what to drop when you can't do it all.
- Noticing that "lazy" days are actually overdrafts.
- Preventing burnout and post-exertional crashes by planning recovery.

### Spoon count

Pick your daily total at the start of the day (Miserandino used twelve for the story; real numbers vary). Cross one off per
task or block. Journalwright Studio's Spoons day block prints up to 16 spoons, default 12, and the X4 companion stores "spoons left".

### Costs

Miserandino's list shows small tasks add up: getting ready for work, reaching high or low, dressing (buttons if hands hurt, long
sleeves if bruises), skipping lunch, standing on a train, typing too long, cooking versus cleaning pots. Your own cost list is the
core of the method.

### Reserve and borrowing

The "extra spoon in my pocket" is a habit: don't plan to zero. Borrowing is allowed but recorded, so you can see the bill.

### Withdrawals and deposits

From the National Autistic Society guide: rate withdrawals 1 = "only takes a little bit of energy" to 10 = "uses up a lot"; rate
deposits 1 = "gives me a little bit" to 10 = "gives me lots". Example items from the guide: withdrawals such as school (9),
noise (8), change (9), pain (9), crowds (7); deposits such as time alone (8), reading (9), time with pets (7), walking (2). Add
them up over the day and "aim to end your day on a zero or a positive number." Some items "exhaust me" and "energise me quite a
lot" (a young ambassador in the guide), so both lists can hold the same item.

### Colours

Green (gives energy), amber (uses some), red (uses a lot). The guide suggests a day ends with "either a positive number or more
green events than red," and that an occasional red day is okay if you rebuild soon. Colour-coding a calendar (red social, green
personal) lets you plan recovery the day after a red event.

### Daily balance

Sum of deposits minus withdrawals; or spoons left. Nicholls recommends ending with about 20–40% of the starting energy left.
Treat these numbers as personal defaults **(uncertain: they come from one clinician's write-up, not a trial)**.

### Energy types

Nicholls names physical, social and cognitive energy. Adding **sensory** and **emotional** columns suits autistic and trauma-affected
people. A day can drain one type while refilling another.

### Low-energy card

A list written when you're well of things that help when you're low: reading alone, a walk, time with pets, crafting, a favourite
game. Keep it where you can see it.

### Activity diary (pacing)

NICE 1.11.7 says to make self-monitoring "as easy as possible by taking advantage of any tools the person already uses, such as
an activity tracker, phone heart-rate monitor or diary." A diary typically records the time, activity, how hard it felt, and
symptoms during and 24–48 hours later (because PEM is delayed).

### Weekly review

A short scan: which day overdrew? What cost more than expected? What deposits worked? Adjust the lists.

## 6. Setting it up

**First setup (30 minutes, once)**

1. **Choose your unit.** Spoons, batteries, beans or percent. Pick what feels natural (Nicholls lists spoons, battery
   percentage, beans).
2. **Choose your daily maximum.** Start with 10 or 12 spoons. You'll tune it in week 2.
3. **Brain-dump your withdrawals.** Include activities, places, people, parts of the day (NAS prompts), plus invisible costs:
   pain, masking, noise, commuting, decisions, waiting, phone calls.
4. **Brain-dump your deposits.** Include rest, but also things that give energy while doing something (walking, crafts, pets).
5. **Rate them.** Give each item a 1–10 weight or a 1–3 spoon cost, or sort into green/amber/red.
6. **Write the low-energy card.** Five things that help when you're low.
7. **Decide your reserve.** One spoon (or 20–40%) is untouchable.
8. **Write your stop rule.** "If the count starts to feel like a scoreboard, I stop counting and just note high/medium/low."

**First day**

- In the morning, write your starting spoons. Be honest, not aspirational.
- Cross off as you go. Don't total at night; just note where you ended and one surprise cost.

**First week**

- Log the starting number, the spoons left and any borrowing.
- On day 7, compare which activities cost more than you guessed. Update the cost list. Keep your daily maximum unchanged until
  you have 7 days of data.

## 7. Daily practice

**Morning (1 minute)**

1. **Check in.** Sleep, pain, mood, weather. How many spoons do I have today? Circle the number (or cross out the ones you don't
   have).
2. **Look at the day.** Fixed items first (shift, appointments), then the flexible ones.
3. **Pre-spend.** Assign rough costs; is the plan under budget? If not, drop, delay, delegate or reduce an item.
4. **Hold the reserve.** Draw a box around one spoon.

**During the day (a few seconds each)**

5. Cross off spoons as you spend them, or note the count at breaks. Don't stop mid-task to do it.
6. Add deposits when you take rest (a spoon back, or a tick).

**Evening (1–2 minutes)**

7. Write spoons left (or the balance).
8. Circle the biggest surprise cost and the best deposit.
9. Mark "borrowed" if you went below the reserve.
10. Plan tomorrow's first step. If you borrowed, plan a lighter tomorrow.

**Pacing-style additions (ME/CFS, long COVID)**

- Break activities into chunks and alternate types (NICE 1.11.4: "plan periods of rest and activity, and incorporate the need for
  pre-emptive rest; alternate and vary between different types of activity and break activities into small chunks").
- Watch for delayed effects: rate symptoms tomorrow, not only today.
- Activity is "never automatically increased but is maintained or adjusted (upwards after a period of stability or downwards when
  symptoms are worse)."

## 8. Rhythms

**Weekly (10 minutes)**

- Add up borrowing days. Any pattern (Thursday after a double shift)?
- Move the heavy things away from each other; put a deposit after every red.
- Update the cost list: what surprised you?

**Monthly (15 minutes, Closing the month)**

- Count "good-spoon days" (Journalwright Studio's tracker uses 4 or more left).
- Compare with sleep and mood (see [mood-tracking-year-in-pixels](mood-tracking-year-in-pixels.md)).
- Decide: raise, keep or lower the daily maximum. NICE says levels may be adjusted "upwards after a period of stability", and
  never automatically.

**Quarterly (20 minutes)**

- Review the whole cost list and the low-energy card. Have deposits changed? Did a med or a schedule change alter your baseline?
- If your maximum keeps dropping, bring the logs to your clinician.

**Yearly**

- Look at the year of spoon counts alongside seasons and life events. Winter, illness flare-ups and work busy seasons will show.
- Archive the diary; start next year's costs from the last list. NICE notes stabilising or increasing tolerance "can take weeks,
  months or sometimes even years".

## 10. Worked examples

**A sample day (12 spoons, shift worker)**

```
Wake: 8 spoons (slept 5 h, pain 2)         reserve: 1
Plan:  shower 1 · dress 1 · bus 1 · shift 4 (7 h) · bus home 1 = 8   → zero, no reserve
Adjust: swap shower to after work; wear same top ⇒ save 1 spoon      → reserve kept
Actual: shift cost 5 (noisy) · dinner soup 1 · nothing else
End:    1 spoon left (the reserve). Not borrowed.
Biggest cost: noisy shift. Best deposit: 20 min alone with music.
Tomorrow: day off; keep to 5 spoons; no errands.
```

**A sample day with energy accounting (numbers)**

```
Withdrawals                    Deposits
shift (noisy)        −8        walk home            +2
bus both ways        −4        20 min alone/music   +5
group chat           −2        time with cat        +4
appointment call     −3        hot meal             +3
Total                −17       Total                +14
Balance: −3.  Overdraft. Tomorrow: two deposits first, one withdrawal.
```

**A sample week (grid)**

```
           M   T   W   T   F   S   S
 Start     8   9   7   8   6   9   9
 Left      1   2   0   2  −2   4   7
 Borrowed  ·   ·   ·   ·   ✓   ·   ·
 Note   Fri: closing shift + errand; Sat: rest. Sun: full day out.
Pattern: Fridays overdraw. Next week: no errands after a Friday closing.
```

**A sample month (summary)**

```
Good-spoon days (4+ left): 12 of 30      Borrowed days: 5 (4 were Fridays)
Average start: 7.4      Average left: 2.1
Costs that surprised me: bright supermarket (−3), phone calls (−2)
Deposits that worked: music alone (+5), cat (+4), light walk (+2)
Change: shop at quiet hours; move calls to mornings.
Maximum stays at 12 (illness flare mid-month, so not raised).
```

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Creator | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| Spoon theory | Miserandino, 2003 | Daily count of spoons; costs; borrowing; reserve |
| Fork theory | Jen Rose, 2018 | External stressors; a fork limit; no gradual refill |
| Knife theory | Jen Rose (traumas); Terry Masson (borrowing) | Two different meanings |
| Matchstick theory | Community | Unpredictable, "finicky" energy |
| Cutlery drawer | Ashley L. Peterson, 2020 | Whisk, tenderizer, baster, ladle etc. |
| Energy accounting | Maja Toudal with Tony Attwood | Weighted withdrawals and deposits, balance, green/amber/red |
| Battery / percent | Community; Nicholls | Energy as a phone battery; end the day with 20–40% left |
| Pacing / energy envelope | Clinical (OT, ME/CFS) | Stay within limits; log activity and symptoms; never auto-increase |
| Quota-contingent (operant) pacing | Chronic pain and CBT tradition | Plan by quotas and gradually increase; distinct from symptom-contingent pacing (Antcliff et al. 2018) |
| 3 Ps: Pace, Plan, Prioritise | RCOT (occupational therapy) | Break tasks up, organise ahead, choose |
| Activity Pacing Questionnaire (APQ-26) | Antcliff et al. | Five dimensions: planning, adjustment, consistency, acceptance, progression |

## 12. Community practice

**What people actually do**

- **Chronic-illness blogs, Tumblr, Instagram and TikTok:** "spoonie" accounts share daily counts, spoon-shaped trackers and
  "spoons per activity" charts.
- **Reddit and forums (lupus, ME/CFS, fibromyalgia, long COVID, autism, ADHD):** people trade cost lists, say "I'm out of spoons" to
  end a conversation, and argue about borrowing and burnout.
- **Clinics and charities:** many patient guides reprint the essay (kidney, lupus, mitochondrial disease, lymphoma).
- **Autistic communities:** use energy accounting, batteries, colour-coded calendars ("red for social, green for personal";
  a young ambassador in the NAS guide).
- **Long COVID:** participants used pacing books, videos and apps; brain fog made written material hard; full-time work and
  caregiving made "pacing not possible" (McMullan et al. 2025).

**Common customisations**

- Different spoon counts for physical, social and cognitive.
- A reserve spoon drawn in a different colour.
- "Spoon debt" column.
- Emoji or drawings of spoons (Type 3 font risk in print; draw them as SVG).
- Batteries or beans instead of spoons for people who dislike cutlery.

**Aesthetic versus minimalist**

- *Aesthetic:* decorated spoon rows, colour keys, stickers.
- *Minimalist:* a number in a corner. Journalwright Studio: a row of simple spoon icons, nothing else.

**Gatekeeping debate.** Some people with chronic illness ask non-disabled people not to use the word for ordinary tiredness
(Chainey 2016). Others say the metaphor is for anyone who counts energy. Respect it either way: if you use spoons in shared
space, say why, and let people choose their own words.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Pencil:** cross out, erase, re-count.
- **Stamp or sticker spoons:** optional; a spoon stamp is fast for people with hand pain.
- **Highlighters or hatching:** in B&W, three hatch densities replace red/amber/green.
- **Ruler and 4 mm grid:** for the weekly grid; the 4 mm grid paper option in the editor makes hand-drawn tables tidy.
- **Wearables (optional):** NICE mentions activity trackers and heart-rate monitors as self-monitoring tools **(they are personal
  choices; some people find numbers stressful)**.
- **Timer:** for pacing chunks. A kitchen timer avoids a phone.

## 16. Evidence and research

### Spoon theory

- **No trials.** Spoon theory is a metaphor; searches found only passing mentions in qualitative and pacing studies
  (a Europe PMC search returned mostly studies that mention it in interviews).
- **Passing mentions:** Barakou et al. 2026 (*BMJ Open*; 29 adults with chronic conditions and fatigue) mention spoon theory as a
  subjective way of budgeting energy; McMullan et al. 2025 (*Sci Rep*; 35 people with long COVID) list it among tools participants
  had used before.
- **Critique:** Chainey 2016 (appropriation); MEpedia notes it "may not represent the impact of all symptoms."

### Energy accounting

- **No trials found.** The publisher says the method is "evidence-based" and the site says it was "clinically tested and
  refined for over 10 years", but no peer-reviewed trial was located **(uncertain)**. Treat it as clinical practice and
  practical knowledge.
- **Related evidence:** autistic burnout research (Raymaker et al. 2020; also Higgins et al. 2021; Mantzalas et al. 2022;
  Arnold et al. 2023 found in a Europe PMC search, not opened) supports the burnout dynamic energy accounting is meant to prevent.

### Pacing and energy management

- **NICE NG206 (29 Oct 2021):** ME/CFS management. Key statements from the guideline:
  - energy management "is not curative" and is a self-management strategy "led by the person themselves";
  - it "includes all types of activity (cognitive, physical, emotional and social)";
  - each person "has a different and fluctuating energy limit and they are experts in judging their own limits";
  - activity is "never automatically increased but is maintained or adjusted";
  - "It can take weeks, months or sometimes even years to reach stabilisation";
  - **1.11.14: do not offer** any therapy based on physical activity or exercise as a cure; generalised exercise programmes; or
    "fixed incremental increases in physical activity or exercise, for example, graded exercise therapy."
  - The plan should cover cognitive activity, mobility, daily tasks, emotional and social demands, rest, sleep and sensory
    stimulation (1.11.3).
- **CDC (May 2024):** PEM "can be mitigated by activity management (pacing)"; "keeping individual activity and symptom diaries may
  help patients identify their personal limitations"; standard exercise recommendations "can substantially harm patients with
  ME/CFS."
- **Pacing in chronic pain and fatigue:** Antcliff et al. (2018) describe pacing as multifaceted (planning, adjustment,
  consistency, acceptance, progression) and distinguish symptom-contingent from quota-contingent approaches. Nielson et al. 2013
  (*Clin J Pain*) reviewed concepts and evidence for pacing in chronic pain (found in a search, not opened).
- **Barakou et al. 2026 (BMJ Open):** 29 adults with chronic conditions; five activity patterns (for example high morning
  fluctuations in 12 people); those who had fatigue advice regulated activity more; conclusion: tailored, individual
  approaches, not standardised.
- **McMullan et al. 2025 (Sci Rep):** 35 long-COVID participants; pacing book, video and app; 64% agreed it helped them
  understand how to pace; barriers were work and family, brain fog and app fatigue.

### Claimed versus supported

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Spoons help others understand limits | **Anecdotal** (widely reported; no trial) |
| Counting energy reduces overspending | **Plausible; supported in pacing literature** (with caveats about definitions) |
| Energy accounting reduces burnout | **Claimed**; no trial found |
| Pacing is right for ME/CFS | **Supported by guidance** (NICE, CDC), while evidence quality is debated **(uncertain)** |
| Graded exercise helps ME/CFS | **Not recommended** by NICE 2021 |
| Rest fully restores energy | **Not established**; deposits vary by person and type |

## 17. Benefits

- Makes hidden limits visible to you and others. **Claimed** (strong community reports; qualitative).
- Helps plan and prioritise on low-energy days. **Claimed**; consistent with pacing guidance.
- Reduces boom-and-bust (overdo, crash). **Claimed**; pacing rationale in NICE and CDC.
- Lets you explain "no" without arguing. **Claimed**.
- Turns recovery into a planned deposit. **Claimed**.
- Notices delayed effects with an activity diary. **Evidenced as good practice** (CDC on diaries; NICE 1.11.7).
- Supports self-compassion ("fluctuating capacity"). **Claimed** (blogger accounts).
- Helps spot autistic burnout early. **Claimed**; burnout itself is well described (Raymaker 2020).
- Gives clinicians a picture of daily function. **Claimed**.

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Turns into guilt about "wasting spoons" | Counting feels like judgement | Rate costs, not worth; add "deposits I did well" line; pause counting for a week |
| Starting count is a guess | Hard to sense energy in advance | Use a 3-level start (low, medium, high) for a month, then numbers |
| Costs feel random | Sensory, pain and stress costs aren't obvious | Log surprises; add energy types (physical, social, thinking, sensory) |
| Overplanning to zero | No slack | Reserve spoon is untouchable |
| Using it as an excuse to avoid all activity | Fear of crashes | Talk to a clinician about safe, flexible activity; NICE endorses tailored, not fixed, plans |
| Using it as a target to push through | "I have 8, so I'll use 8" | The aim is balance, not maximum; end above zero |
| Borrowing without recording | Debt hides | A "borrowed" tick and a lighter next day |
| Deposits don't refill (rest doesn't help) | Some fatigue isn't fixed by rest; PEM delays | Track delayed effects the next day; don't assume rest = full refill |
| Gatekeeping conflict | The word is contested | Choose your own unit (batteries, beans, percent) |
| Hard to keep up on bad days | Brain fog, low energy | One mark only: the number left; the X4 or a single circle |
| Diary burden | Logging costs energy too | Log only 3 days a week, or one block a day; use tools already at hand (NICE) |
| Numbers vary by mood | Low mood inflates costs | Cross-check with the mood chart ([mood-tracking-year-in-pixels](mood-tracking-year-in-pixels.md)) |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** Time blindness and executive load are costs. Add "starting" and "switching" as withdrawals. Keep the count to one row.
  See [adhd-journaling](adhd-journaling.md).
- **Autism and sensory needs:** Energy accounting was designed for autistic people. Add masking, noise, light, crowds,
  change, and unplanned demands as withdrawals; include special interests, time alone and sensory tools as deposits (NAS list).
  See [autistic-sensory-journaling](autistic-sensory-journaling.md).
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** The method's home. Keep the daily task tiny (one number). Track delayed effects if you have
  PEM. Coordinate with your care team; see [symptom-tracking](symptom-tracking.md).
- **Depression and anxiety:** Low mood lowers the start number and inflates costs. Track mood beside spoons to tell "depleted" from
  "low." Avoid negative self-talk in the count. Start with high/medium/low.
- **Trauma:** Hypervigilance is an energy cost; safety and predictability are deposits. Avoid forcing detailed logs; allow a "skip".
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** Icons and circles over words; pre-printed lists.
- **Low vision:** Large spoons, high contrast, 8.5×11.
- **Motor or hand pain:** Crossing off many small spoons is work. Use a single tick or a number; a stamp; X4 buttons.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** Dysphoria, passing work, safety scanning and medical logistics are real costs. Include
  "gender labour" as a withdrawal if it fits, and gender-affirming activities as deposits. Track dose days and recovery.
- **Shift workers:** Plan the "day" from wake to sleep, not midnight to midnight. Night shifts drain more; add a "recovery day"
  after a run of nights.
- **Low income:** Pencil and paper. Free NAS worksheets. A written list costs nothing.

## 23. Glossary

- **Activity diary / log:** a record of activities, effort and symptoms, used for pacing.
- **Activity pacing:** balancing activity and rest to stay within energy limits.
- **Autistic burnout:** chronic exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance after prolonged stress and mismatch.
- **Baseline:** the level of activity you can sustain without a crash.
- **Boom and bust:** overdoing on good days followed by crashes.
- **Borrowing:** spending tomorrow's spoons today.
- **Deposit:** something that gives energy.
- **Energy accounting:** a bank-account model of energy with withdrawals, deposits and a balance (Toudal).
- **Energy envelope:** the range of activity you can manage without worsening symptoms (used by CDC).
- **Energy management (NICE):** a self-managed plan for using limited energy, not a cure.
- **Fork theory:** forks are external stressors with a limit (Jen Rose).
- **Masking:** hiding autistic traits to fit in; costs energy.
- **ME/CFS:** myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome.
- **PEM (post-exertional malaise):** worsening of symptoms after exertion, often delayed by hours or days.
- **Quota-contingent pacing:** activity set by planned amounts and gradually increased.
- **Reserve spoon:** one spoon you don't plan to spend.
- **Spoonie:** a person with chronic illness or disability who uses spoon theory.
- **Spoons:** units of energy in spoon theory.
- **Symptom-contingent pacing:** stop or rest when symptoms rise.
- **Withdrawal:** something that uses energy.

## 24. FAQ

1. **How many spoons should I start with?** Whatever feels true this week. Miserandino used 12 in the story; the Studio's
   Spoons block defaults to 12. Adjust after a week of data.
2. **Are spoons the same as energy accounting?** No. Spoons count what you have and spend. Energy accounting adds deposits,
   weights and a balance.
3. **Do I have to have a diagnosis?** No, but if your energy is very limited or you crash after activity, see a clinician.
4. **Is it okay to use spoons if I'm not disabled?** It's contested. Use your own unit (batteries, percent) if in doubt, and don't
   appropriate the identity "spoonie" (Chainey 2016).
5. **What if rest doesn't refill me?** Some conditions don't refill on a schedule. Track delayed effects and talk to your clinician.
6. **Should I push my number up?** Not automatically. NICE says activity is never automatically increased; raise only after a
   stable period.
7. **What's the difference between spoons and pacing?** Pacing is the clinical practice; spoons is a way of picturing it.
8. **Is it OK to borrow?** Occasionally, but record it, plan a lighter next day, and hold a reserve.
9. **What about graded exercise?** NICE (2021) says not to offer graded exercise therapy or fixed incremental increases for
   ME/CFS. Talk to your team about what's safe for your condition.
10. **I hate the guilt.** Switch to high/medium/low for a while, or write only deposits.
11. **Can I use it with a partner or carer?** Yes. NAS notes energy accounting can be done "yourself or with others helping."
12. **Where does fork theory fit?** Use forks for stress stacking, spoons for energy. Some people use both.

### e. Don't adopt

- **Automatic "you're overspent" warnings, streaks, badges.** Out of scope; also produce guilt.
- **Rising targets ("5% more each week").** NICE says activity is never automatically increased; graded-exercise-style ladders are
  the opposite of spoon logic.
- **Medical claims** ("this will treat ME/CFS"). Keep the book as a record; leave treatment to clinicians.
- **Apps that push spoon predictions from wearables or AI.** Out of scope (the companion has no AI), and predictions can be wrong.
- **Decorative spoon art.** Busy; emoji risk Type 3 fonts. Keep the simple SVG spoon.
- **Forcing weights on everyone.** Numbers can be stressful. Make weights and balance opt-in.
- **Shame-tinged copy** ("wasted spoons"). Use neutral language: "spent", "held", "borrowed".

## 26. Open questions

- Is there any trial of spoon-style counting on outcomes (fatigue, crashes, quality of life)? None found.
- Is there any peer-reviewed evaluation of energy accounting? None found; the book cites the author's clinical practice.
- What are the best default start numbers and reserves for different conditions? Anecdotal only (Nicholls's 20–40%).
- How well does the method work with PEM's delayed effects? Diaries are recommended, but paper formats vary.
- What is the effect of counting on anxiety or rumination? Not studied directly; mood-monitoring literature suggests some risk.
- How should trans-specific costs (dysphoria, safety, medical logistics) be counted? No research found; community practice only.
