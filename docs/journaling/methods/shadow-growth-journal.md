# Shadow work and shadow growth journaling

> **Not medical advice.** This is a research summary to help design journal pages. It is not therapy and does not
> replace a clinician. Shadow work is a popular-psychology framing of an idea from Carl Jung. It is not a treatment
> with clinical trials behind it. Anything heavy (trauma, abuse, suicidal thoughts, a depressive episode, dissociation)
> belongs with a therapist, not alone with a notebook.
>
> **If you are in crisis right now:** in the US, call or text **988** (24/7), or call **911** if you are in immediate
> danger. **Trans Lifeline** (peer support from trans people) is **(877) 565-8860** in the US. These are the same
> numbers as the book's Support page. Shadow work is not the right tool for a crisis; a safety plan is (see
> [safety-planning](safety-planning.md)).

Checked 2026-09-29. Where a point is uncertain it is marked **(uncertain)**. Three layers are kept apart throughout:

- **Jungian theory:** what Jung wrote, and what Jungian analysts still say.
- **Therapist-led work:** what happens in analysis or psychodynamic therapy, and the evidence for it.
- **Self-help claims:** what books, courses and social media say you can do alone with prompts.

| Year | Shadow concept developed from about 1912 (*Psychology of the Unconscious*), stated most clearly in *Aion* (1951). Popular "shadow work" from the late 1980s and 1990s; a social-media boom in the 2020s. |

## 2. Summary

Jung used "shadow" for the part of your personality your conscious self does not recognise or does not want: traits,
impulses and feelings you push out of sight because they do not fit who you think you are. He said the shadow is
largely met through projection (you notice the trait strongly in other people) and that meeting it takes moral effort
rather than a quick technique. Popular shadow work turns this into a journaling routine: notice a strong reaction,
ask what it says about you, and reclaim the disowned trait, including good ones ("the gold in the shadow"). It can
be a useful lens for self-reflection and for noticing patterns like envy or people-pleasing. It is also easy to do
badly: the evidence for shadow work as self-help is essentially absent, "projection" as a mechanism is only partly
supported by experiments, the practice can turn into rumination or self-blame, and it can stir up trauma. It can also
sit awkwardly with trans and gender-diverse readers when it borrows Jung's gendered ideas. The honest position is:
take the prompts that build curiosity and self-compassion, drop the mysticism you do not want, keep it short, and use
a therapist for anything heavy.

## 3. History and origin

### Who Jung was
Carl Gustav Jung was born in 1875 in Kesswil, Switzerland, studied medicine at Basel and Zürich, and took his MD in
1902. He worked at the Burghölzli hospital in Zürich under Eugen Bleuler, where his word-association experiments led him
to the term **complex**: an emotionally charged cluster of associations that the person may not be aware of
(Britannica). He worked closely with Sigmund Freud from about 1907 to 1912, was the first president of the International
Psychoanalytical Association from 1911, and broke away after *Psychology of the Unconscious* (1912; later *Symbols of
Transformation*) disagreed with Freud on sexuality and the unconscious. He resigned from the association in 1914.

### What Jung said (theory)
The IAAP's short article on the shadow paraphrases the tradition well: "The shadow is everything that the ego-consciousness
does not know about itself, the entire unconscious part of the personality." The same article says integrating it
"expands consciousness, creates new meanings and releases the energy that was previously spent on the fight against the
shadow," and that it leads to "fairer relations with oneself and others, acceptance of human limits and rejection of
omnipotent perfectionism in favor of wholeness." It also notes the shadow can hold "gold": denying good qualities
can be as damaging as denying bad ones.

The IAAP's abstract of *Aion* (Collected Works vol. 9 part 2) gives Jung's own summary: the ego is "the center of
consciousness but not the center of the personality"; the shadow is made of dark, emotional, primitive elements; and
projections from it are hard to recognise because they attach to outside objects, which can leave a person in "a
self-perpetuating world of illusion." Jung's oft-quoted line from *Aion* is that the shadow is a moral problem that
challenges the whole ego-personality, since no one becomes conscious of it without considerable moral effort **(quote
seen in secondary listings only; not opened in a primary text)**. Note the tone: this is a lifelong, serious task, not a
weekend exercise.

### Cultural context
Jung's psychology grew from early 20th-century European psychiatry, Christian and alchemical symbolism, and myth.
Popular shadow work arrived with the American men's movement and self-help boom (Bly, Zweig and Abrams), and returned
online as therapy-speak in the 2020s. In the process a clinical-mythic concept became a checklist. That gap is the
main thing to keep in mind.

## 4. Philosophy and principles

**Stated goals (Jungian):** wholeness, not perfection; more consciousness of what drives you; less energy spent
defending an image of yourself; fairer treatment of others. Jung's word for the lifelong process is **individuation**.

**Core beliefs (as taught):**
1. Everyone builds a **persona** (the face you show) and pushes the rest into the shadow.
2. What you disown does not vanish; it leaks out as moods, slips, projections and sudden behaviour you regret.
3. Strong reactions to other people (disgust, envy, contempt, or idealising) point at your own disowned material.
4. Bringing it into awareness gives you choice. The aim is to know it, not to act it out.
5. The shadow holds positive potential too.

**The problem it claims to solve:** a gap between the self you present and the self you are, showing up as
irritability, repeated relationship patterns, self-sabotage, and harsh judgment of others.

**What the evidence says about the claims (short version):** beliefs 1–2 are partly compatible with mainstream findings
on suppression; belief 3 (projection) is only partly supported; beliefs 4–5 are unproven as self-help. Section 16
lays it out.

## 5. Core components

| Element | What it is | Layer |
|---|---|---|
| Persona | The social mask; the "good" self on display | Jungian theory |
| Shadow | Disowned or unknown traits, negative and positive | Jungian theory |
| Projection | Seeing your disowned trait in someone else | Jungian theory (partly tested) |
| Triggers | Strong reactions to people or events | Self-help practice |
| Prompt journaling | Timed writing from set questions | Self-help practice |
| Dream work | Noticing figures of the same gender or "unacceptable" figures in dreams | Jungian theory; therapist-led |
| Active imagination | Dialogue with an inner figure in writing or image | Jungian; advanced; therapist-led is safer |
| The golden shadow | Disowned strengths (admiration, envy of talents) | Popular Jungian |
| Integration | Owning the trait without acting it out | Jungian theory |
| Ritual and closing | Ending the session and grounding | Self-help (and trauma-informed) practice |

### Persona
The persona is Jung's term for the role you play in public: polite, competent, easy-going. It is not a fake self;
everyone has one. Shadow work starts by asking what you had to leave out to keep it going.

### Shadow (personal versus collective)
Jungians separate the **personal shadow** (your own history) from the **collective shadow** (what a group or society
calls "dark"). The IAAP article notes that any society "extols one side and calls it 'the light', and rejects the other
naming it 'dark.'" This matters for the trans section below: what a culture calls shadow is partly a social judgment.
A trait your family labelled shameful is not automatically a hidden truth about you.

### Projection
Freud named projection first; Jung tied it to the shadow. In self-help form: "the thing that annoys me most in others
may be something I have not accepted in me." The experimental record is mixed (section 16). Treat it as a hypothesis
to test, not as a fact about you. Sometimes the person really is being unkind.

### Triggers
The everyday entry point. A trigger is a reaction out of proportion to the event. In shadow work you write down the
event, the reaction, the judgment ("she is so selfish"), and then ask an open question. Beware the word "trigger":
in trauma care it means a reminder of a traumatic event, which is a different thing (see section 19).

### Dream work
Jungians read dreams as messages from the unconscious. A same-gender figure who is threatening, shabby or shameful
is classically read as shadow (IAAP article). See [dream-journal](dream-journal.md). There is no good evidence that
dream interpretation reveals hidden traits; use it as a source of images and questions.

### Active imagination
Jung's own method from the *Red Book* years: entering a waking dialogue with an inner figure. It can be vivid and
destabilising. Jung's personal version was long and intense. It is the part of the tradition most clearly meant for
an experienced guide.

### The golden shadow
Popularised by later writers: the admirable traits you deny (courage, creativity, ambition), often visible when you
feel awe or envy toward others. It is a good, low-risk entry because it points at strengths.

### Integration
Integration means acknowledging the trait and choosing what to do with it. It does not mean "act on every impulse."
In popular versions the word often blurs into vague self-acceptance.

## 6. Setting it up

**Rule 0. Screen yourself first.** Do not start if you are in a depressive episode, in crisis, freshly bereaved,
dissociating, or in the middle of trauma processing. If you have a therapist, tell them and ask if it fits. If you
notice you feel worse after two sessions, stop and see section 18.

**Step by step**
1. **Pick a container.** A separate notebook, or a paper block in your day page. It does not need to be pretty.
2. **Set a limit.** 15 minutes, a timer you can see, one topic per session.
3. **Choose a time.** Not right before sleep, and not right after a fight. Daylight, seated, water nearby.
4. **Write three lines on the first page.** (a) "I am safe right now" and the date. (b) A calm thing to do afterwards.
   (c) One person or number to contact if it gets too much (your Support page).
5. **Choose your version of "shadow."** Some readers dislike the mystic language. "Parts I do not like to admit,"
   "disowned traits" or "what I judge" work equally well.
6. **Start small.** First topic: something mild (mild irritation, mild envy), not your worst memory.

**First day.** Write for 10 minutes: "Something that irritated me this week." Then answer the three questions in
section 7. Close with the grounding step.

**First week.** Two sessions, not seven. After each, write one line in the day page: "Felt: lighter / same / worse."
If "worse" twice, pause the practice.

## 7. Daily practice

Shadow work is not a daily practice. Two to three short sessions a week is more realistic, with the rest of the days
handled by ordinary check-ins.

**A 15-minute session**
1. **Ground (1 min).** Feet on the floor, three slow breaths, name five things you can see.
2. **Trigger (2 min).** Write what happened, factually, in two or three lines. No interpretation yet.
3. **Reaction (2 min).** What did you feel and where in your body? Rate it 0–5.
4. **Judgment (2 min).** Complete "I can't stand it when ___" or "She is so ___".
5. **Question (4 min).** Choose one, gently:
   - "Where might a small version of this be true of me, or once was?"
   - "What is the need under my reaction?" (recognition, rest, freedom, safety)
   - "What would it cost me to allow this trait in myself, in a small, safe way?"
   - "Is this partly just about them?" (an honest check; sometimes it is)
6. **Kindness (2 min).** Write one sentence to yourself as you would to a friend (from self-compassion practice).
7. **Close (2 min).** Rate the feeling again. Do the calming thing from your first page. Close the notebook.

**Order matters:** ground, then write, then soften, then close. Never end on the harshest line.

## 8. Rhythms

| Rhythm | What to do | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly | Re-read the week's notes. Circle repeated words (for example "lazy", "fake", "selfish"). Write one pattern. | 10 min |
| Monthly | List three recurring triggers and one "gold" trait. Note what helped, what made things worse. Decide whether to continue, pause or bring it to a therapist. | 20 min |
| Quarterly | Ask honestly: do I feel more understanding, or more critical of myself? If the second, stop or change method. | 20 min |
| Yearly | A short letter to yourself. Archive or shred the notebook. Private material should not sit in shared places. | 30 min |

**Archiving.** These pages are among the most private you will write. Keep them at home, out of the scan and send
paths, and decide in advance what happens to them (keep, burn, shred).

## 10. Worked examples

### A sample session (fully filled in)

```
Date: Tue 6 Oct        Title: Coworker who takes credit        Tags: work, envy
Safe right now: [x]    Feeling: 4/5
What happened: In the meeting Dana presented the same idea I'd mentioned last week
and everyone praised her.
I can't stand it when: people push themselves forward and take credit.
A small version in me: I want to be noticed and I usually hide that. I don't speak
up, then resent people who do.
Need underneath: recognition.
To me, as a friend: Wanting credit is normal. You can say it out loud, kindly.
Feeling after: 2/5.    Closed: [x]   Next small step: email my boss my notes.
Also: is Dana partly just being careless? Maybe. Both can be true.
```

### A sample week
- **Mon:** no session (long shift).
- **Tue:** 15-minute session above.
- **Thu:** a dream noted in two lines, no analysis.
- **Sat:** golden-shadow page: envied a friend's calm at parties; one small step: say hi first at the meetup.
- **Sun:** 10-minute weekly review: "credit" and "unseen" appeared twice.

### A sample month
Week 1: two sessions. Week 2: one session plus a therapist conversation. Week 3: paused (bad week, low spoons, no
guilt). Week 4: one session. Monthly page: triggers = credit, being ignored, being told what to do; gold = leadership;
felt lighter 3 of 4 times; continue at one session a week.

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Who | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| Analytical (Jungian) analysis | Jungian analysts, IAAP-affiliated institutes | Long therapy, weekly or more; dreams, transference, active imagination; a therapist guides |
| Shadow work prompt books | Various popular authors, 2010s–2020s | Lists of prompts; self-directed; wide quality range |
| The 3-2-1 Shadow Process | Ken Wilber (Integral); popularised in the 2000s **(uncertain on details)** | Describe a trigger in third, second, then first person; a quick exercise |
| Golden shadow work | Popular Jungian writers | Focus on disowned strengths |
| Internal Family Systems (IFS) | Richard Schwartz, 1980s | A distinct model of "parts"; therapist-led; has its own emerging evidence **(not covered here)** |
| Gestalt empty-chair | Fritz Perls, 1950s–60s | Talk to a projected part in an empty chair; done in a therapy room |
| Dream group work | Jungian-inspired groups | Shared dream discussion |
| Psychodynamic journaling | Therapist-set homework | Writing about patterns in relationships |
| Self-compassion writing | Kristin Neff and others | Not shadow work, but often used alongside; better evidence |

## 12. Community practice

**What people actually do.** Reddit and social-media communities share prompts, "shadow work challenges" (30 days),
tarot or astrology crossovers, and cleansing rituals. Many write in the evening, often on heavy topics, often daily.
Some people report clarity and relief; others report spirals, guilt, obsession with "what's wrong with me" or trauma
memories surfacing. There are no reliable data on how common each is **(uncertain)**.

**Common customisations.** Moon-phase timing; a "safe word" at the top of the page; colour-coded feelings; pairing
with therapy homework; using only "golden shadow" prompts.

**Two camps.**
- **Ritual and aesthetic:** candles, crystals, tarot, "shadow self" branding, dark-themed journals.
- **Plain and psychological:** a prompt list, a timer, and a therapist. This is the safer end and the one this doc
  favours.

**Caution on the marketplace.** Words like "heal," "purge," "expose," "dark side," "shadow self" and "toxic" are used
loosely. There is no clinical standard for a shadow work coach; anyone can use the label.

## 13. Official products and formats

Not applicable as a single product line: there is no official shadow journal.
- **Books.** *Meeting the Shadow* (Abrams and Zweig, eds., J. P. Tarcher, 1991) is an anthology of essays by many
  authors. Robert Bly, *A Little Book on the Human Shadow* (1988). Jung's own *Aion* (Collected Works vol. 9 part 2,
  Princeton University Press) is dense reading and not a journaling guide.
- **Training.** Jungian analysts train at institutes affiliated with the IAAP; the IAAP lists member groups.
- **Price ranges.** Popular books $15–25; prompt journals $15–30; therapy varies, sliding scale in many areas.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Pen.** Anything comfortable; a pencil is fine for people who like to erase.
- **Paper.** Lined or dotted pages. A thick, unbranded notebook works; avoid anything you would feel bad tearing out.
- **Timer.** A kitchen timer or watch. A visible end is the best safety feature.
- **Comfort.** A blanket, a drink, an object to hold. Something for grounding (a smooth stone, a scent) is optional.
- **A "closer".** A second, lighter notebook or page for after ("small good things") helps you leave the heavy page.
- **Paper for privacy.** A lock, a folder, or an envelope. Consider a shred plan.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps and templates.** Many journaling apps carry "shadow work" prompt packs. Most are unreviewed. Read the
  privacy policy: these are among the most sensitive things you will write. Note that generative-AI "shadow work
  chatbots" exist; they are not therapists, and this project does not adopt them (section 25e).
- **E-ink** (reMarkable, Kindle Scribe, Boox). A good fit: slow, private, no notifications. Use a template with a
  fixed top (feelings scale) and bottom (closer). See [e-ink-templates](e-ink-templates.md).
- **Scanning.** Do not scan these pages into a cloud service casually. If you scan for your own archive, keep them
  offline, and in a KDP or Journalwright Studio book the pages stay private (see section 25).
- **Gained versus lost.** Digital: search, backup, prompt variety, easy privacy locks. Lost: the calming physical
  act, the sealing away of a closed notebook, and the ability to leave the thing in a drawer. Cloud storage adds a
  breach risk that paper does not have.

## 16. Evidence and research

### Summary
There are **no controlled trials of shadow work as a self-help journaling method** that I could find, and the
Jungian bodies themselves point to research on Jungian *psychotherapy*, not on shadow journaling. The sources below
cover four adjacent areas. None proves that shadow work works.

### 16.1 Jungian psychotherapy (therapist-led)
Christian Roesler's review (*Behavioral Sciences*, 2013) collects studies of Jungian psychotherapy, mostly from
Switzerland and Germany. As reported in the paper:
- **PAL-Schweiz** (Zurich): naturalistic prospective study, 37 cases and 26 therapists; effect sizes 0.94–1.48 for
  personality restructuring; stable at three-year follow-up. Limit stated by the author: no control group, so the
  sample may be unusually motivated.
- **San Francisco Psychotherapy Research Project:** 39 of 57 enrolled patients; reductions in symptoms and
  interpersonal problems; internal validity could not be secured.
- **Berlin Jungian study:** retrospective, 111 of 353 documented cases; 86.6% reported good or moderate well-being.
  Selection bias risk.
- **Konstanz study:** a retrospective consumer-survey design modelled on an earlier consumer-reports study.
- **PAP-S:** a quasi-experimental design with a control group, but only four Jungian cases.
- Roesler's evidence grading: **no Level I (randomised) studies**; effectiveness is shown in naturalistic settings;
  **efficacy** (that the method itself causes the change) is not established. Around 10–20% of patients showed no
  improvement across studies.

The IAAP research page lists the same body of work (outcome studies, sandplay meta-analysis, dream research) and, in
the summaries I opened, does not mention shadow work specifically. **Read this as: Jungian therapy has "some studies";
shadow work as a homework technique has none of its own.**

### 16.2 Projection and suppression (the mechanism)
- Jung and Freud both said people project disowned traits onto others. The Wikipedia summary of the research
  literature notes that Baumeister, Dale and Sommer (1998) concluded that the view that people defensively project
  specific bad traits of their own onto others as a way of denying them "is not well supported."
- Newman, Duff and Baumeister (1997, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) offered an alternative:
  suppressing a thought about an unwanted trait makes it more mentally available, so you then use it to interpret other
  people. Projection becomes a by-product of suppression, not a deliberate defence **(as described in the projection
  summary I opened; the original paper was not opened)**.
- Wang, Hagger and Chatzisarantis (2020, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*), a meta-analysis of 31 studies,
  found "rebound" effects after trying to suppress a thought, regardless of mental load; immediate increases during
  suppression appeared only under extra load. This lends some support to the idea that pushing things away has a
  cost. **Limit:** laboratory tasks with neutral or trivial thoughts, not personality traits or long-term repression.
- **Take-away:** "what you push away comes back" is partly supported; "what irritates you in others is always yours"
  is not.

### 16.3 Expressive writing (the writing part)
Frattaroli (2006, *Psychological Bulletin*) meta-analysed 146 randomised studies of experimental disclosure
(writing or speaking about personal, meaningful topics) and found a positive, significant average effect of
**r = .075** with moderators. That is small. Expressive writing is a specific protocol (usually 15–20 minutes on a few
consecutive days), not the same as a shadow prompt. See [expressive-writing](expressive-writing.md). **Limits:**
small average effect; results vary by population and topic; the studies are not shadow work.

### 16.4 Self-compassion (the kindness part)
Neff and Germer (2013, *Journal of Clinical Psychology*) tested the 8-week Mindful Self-Compassion program: a pilot
(N = 21) and a randomised trial (25 treated, 27 waitlist controls) found larger gains in self-compassion,
mindfulness and wellbeing than the waitlist group, maintained at 6- and 12-month follow-up. **Limits:** small samples,
mostly women in their fifties, waitlist control, self-report. It supports the *kindness* component I add to each
session, not shadow work.

### 16.5 Claimed versus supported

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Jungian psychotherapy helps people | Some naturalistic evidence; no RCTs; 10–20% no change |
| Shadow work journaling improves wellbeing | Not tested |
| Suppression backfires | Partly supported in lab studies |
| Strong reactions to others reveal disowned traits | Partly supported (accessibility route); often over-claimed |
| Integrating the shadow reduces harm to others | Untested |
| Expressive writing helps | Small average effect |
| Writing with self-compassion helps | Supported for self-compassion training generally |
| Shadow work "heals trauma" | Not supported; potentially harmful for some |

### 16.6 Scientific standing of the theory
Encyclopedia summaries note that Jung's wider concepts (archetypes, the collective unconscious) are hard to test
and face falsifiability criticism, though they influenced literature, religion studies and psychotherapy. The
shadow itself is closer to ideas in mainstream psychology (suppression, self-concept, self-serving bias) than the
archetype theory is.

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---|---|
| **Rumination:** going round and round the same shame | Timer, 15 minutes, and a fixed closing step; if it loops, stop and walk |
| **Self-attack:** "I am the villain" | Add the kindness line every time; write as if to a friend |
| **Over-reading:** "every irritation is my shadow" | Ask "is this partly just about them?" and "is this a boundary I need?" |
| **Blaming yourself for others' harm** | Some people really are unkind; some situations are unfair; not everything is projection |
| **Spiritual bypass** (calling anything "growth") | Ask whether it changes what you do on Tuesday |
| **Surfacing trauma** | Stop, ground, contact your therapist; do not push through |
| **Daily heavy writing before bed** | Move to daytime, two or three times a week |
| **Treating dark impulses as commands** | Integration means knowing, not acting; talk to a professional about any urge to hurt yourself or others |
| **Ritual over substance** | Drop the candles if they become the point |
| **No exit plan** | Write a stop rule on page one |
| **Using it instead of therapy** | Bring the pages to your clinician; do not replace them |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

### Safety first: trauma, depression and anxiety
- **Trauma.** SAMHSA's trauma-informed framework (I was not able to open the source page; treat this as general
  principle) stresses safety, choice, and avoiding re-traumatisation. Shadow prompts that ask you to dig into "what
  you are ashamed of" can do the opposite. If you have a trauma history, do this only with a trauma-informed
  therapist. In the practice, keep it present-focused, keep the timer short, and stop if you feel numb, panicky,
  floaty or flooded.
- **Depression.** In a depressive episode, harsh self-examination often feeds negative self-talk. Use behavioural
  or values-based tools first (see [cbt-thought-record](cbt-thought-record.md)); return to shadow work when stable,
  and preferably with a guide.
- **Anxiety.** Keep to concrete triggers and shorter sessions; avoid open-ended "explore your darkest fears."
- **Suicidal thoughts, self-harm, dissociation, psychosis, mania.** Do not do shadow work. Use your safety plan
  ([safety-planning](safety-planning.md)), the Support page in the book, **988** (call or text) or **Trans Lifeline
  (877) 565-8860**, and contact your clinician.
- **Use with a therapist for anything heavy.** NIMH's guidance on psychotherapy is a good starting point for finding
  and evaluating a therapist: ask about credentials, approach and evidence, and rapport. If you are not getting
  better with reasonable time, talk to the therapist about alternatives.

### ADHD
- Emotional dysregulation is common in ADHD (Shaw and colleagues, *American Journal of Psychiatry*, 2014, a review).
  Big reactions are not always "shadow"; they can be neurology. Do not over-interpret them.
- Keep sessions very short (5–10 minutes) with a timer and a single prompt on the page. Choose a tick-box or word
  circle over blank space. Do it right after an event, when you still remember it, not "later."
- Let missed weeks go without penalty.

### Autism and sensory needs
- **Alexithymia** (difficulty identifying and describing feelings) is common in autistic people: Kinnaird, Stewart
  and Tchanturia (2019, *European Psychiatry*; meta-analysis of 15 studies) found about half of autistic
  participants met criteria versus about 5% of neurotypical ones. Limits: most participants were cognitively able, and
  only one study controlled for anxiety and depression. Prompts like "what did you feel?" can be hard. Offer body
  and sensory words, a word list, or a scale.
- Use concrete, literal prompts ("What happened? What did I do? What did I need?") instead of metaphor about
  "dark selves."
- Keep a consistent, predictable page; low sensory load (quiet room, familiar pen).

### Chronic illness and low spoons
Do one short prompt, or do not do it. A session on a low-spoon day is a poor trade; write "not today" and move on.
See [spoon-theory-energy-accounting](spoon-theory-energy-accounting.md).

### Dyslexia and dysgraphia
Voice notes are fine, but keep them private and delete after. Use short fill-in phrases and circle-a-word lists
instead of paragraphs.

### Low vision and motor or hand pain
Large print, bold lines, a heavier pen; one box per prompt; or dictate.

### Trans and gender-diverse people
Read this section before you start.
- **Jung's gender ideas.** Jung's *anima* and *animus* (the inner opposite-sex figure) are built on a binary
  and on the gender roles of his era. Encyclopedia summaries note his traditional views on gender and his early
  pathologising of homosexuality. Writers in the Jungian world are revising these ideas, but a lot of popular
  shadow material repeats the older version. You do not need anima and animus to do shadow work; skip them.
- **Do not treat your gender or identity as "shadow."** Being trans is not a disowned trait to be integrated or a
  hidden "dark side." If a book or coach implies it is, put it down.
- **Shame from the outside.** Many trans people were told as children which parts of them were wrong. Those
  judgments came from other people and society, not from a hidden truth. The IAAP notes that a society labels one side
  "dark"; in shadow work, ask **whose voice** the judgment is. This fits Ilan Meyer's minority stress model
  (*Psychological Bulletin*, 2003; on lesbian, gay and bisexual populations): stigma, expectations of rejection,
  concealment and internalised negativity are stressors from a hostile environment, not personal defects. The model is
  not specific to trans people, but its logic carries over. **(Meyer's paper studies LGB populations; do not treat it
  as trans-specific evidence.)**
- **Projection can go both ways.** It is worth asking "is this my shadow?" and also "is this discrimination?"
  Not every irritation with a transphobic remark is projection.
- **Use trans-affirming therapists.** For therapist-led work look for someone with trans competence; a Jungian
  analyst who insists on binary gender archetypes is a poor fit.
- **Numbers.** **Trans Lifeline (877) 565-8860** is peer support run by trans people; it describes itself as a
  trans-led organisation and advocates against police involvement in crisis calls. **988** also offers support
  for LGBTQ+ people. Both are on the book's Trans support and Support pages.
- **Golden shadow.** Euphoria, courage and joy you denied yourself for safety count. See
  [gender-euphoria-and-hrt-log](gender-euphoria-and-hrt-log.md).

### Shift workers
Do sessions on a day off, daytime for you. Tired brains are harsher on themselves.

### Low income
The method is free. Do not pay for a "shadow work coach" or course. If you want a therapist, ask about sliding
scales and community clinics.

## 20. Comparison

| Method | Focus | Evidence | Where shadow work differs |
|---|---|---|---|
| [CBT thought record](cbt-thought-record.md) | Test a specific thought against evidence | Strong (CBT) | Shadow work is interpretive and psychodynamic; CBT is structured and checkable |
| [Expressive writing](expressive-writing.md) | Write about a stressful event for 15–20 min over days | Some studies, small effect | Expressive writing has a protocol and data; shadow work does not |
| [Gratitude journaling](gratitude-journaling.md) | Notice good things | Some studies | Shadow work goes toward difficult parts; gratitude toward positives |
| [DBT diary card](dbt-diary-card.md) | Track urges, emotions, skills | Strong for DBT | Diary card is skills-focused and safer for high distress |
| [Progoff Intensive Journal](progoff-intensive-journal.md) | Structured self-exploration with a Jungian flavour | Anecdotal | Progoff was a Jungian-trained psychologist; more structure, less "dark" framing |
| [Dream journal](dream-journal.md) | Record and reflect on dreams | Some studies (dreams and mood) | Overlaps in Jungian practice |

## 21. Combining

- **With a therapist.** Bring your notes; ask them to help you read them. Use it as homework only if they agree.
- **With CBT:** use a thought record for the specific hot thought ([cbt-thought-record](cbt-thought-record.md));
  use shadow prompts for a pattern across weeks.
- **With self-compassion:** end every session with a kindness line. This is the single most useful pairing.
- **With DBT:** use the diary card to track urges and skills ([dbt-diary-card](dbt-diary-card.md)); do not do shadow
  work during a spike.
- **With dream journaling:** a two-line dream log feeds a monthly question ([dream-journal](dream-journal.md)).
- **With gratitude / small good things:** as the closer ([gratitude-journaling](gratitude-journaling.md)).
- **With Progoff:** the Progoff dialogue sections cover similar ground with more structure
  ([progoff-intensive-journal](progoff-intensive-journal.md)).
- **With safety planning:** page one of your notebook should point to your safety plan
  ([safety-planning](safety-planning.md)).

## 23. Glossary

- **Active imagination.** Jung's method of waking dialogue with inner figures.
- **Anima / animus.** Jung's terms for the inner "opposite-sex" figure; built on a binary; see section 19.
- **Archetype.** Universal pattern or image in Jung's theory; hard to test.
- **Collective shadow.** What a group or culture calls dark and rejects.
- **Complex.** Emotionally charged cluster of associations (Jung's early term).
- **Ego.** The centre of consciousness, not of the whole personality (Jung).
- **Golden shadow.** Disowned positive traits.
- **Individuation.** Jung's word for lifelong development toward wholeness.
- **Integration.** Owning a trait without acting it out.
- **Persona.** The public mask.
- **Projection.** Seeing your own disowned trait in someone else; partly supported.
- **Rebound effect.** A thought coming back more after suppression.
- **Rumination.** Repetitive negative thinking.
- **Self.** In Jung, the centre and totality of the psyche.
- **Shadow.** The unconscious or disowned part of the personality.
- **Shadow work.** The popular practice of examining the shadow, usually by journaling.
- **Trigger.** In self-help, a strong reaction; in trauma care, a reminder of trauma.
- **Alexithymia.** Difficulty identifying and describing feelings.
- **Grounding.** Techniques to return attention to the present.

## 24. FAQ

**1. Is shadow work scientific?** No. It is based on Jung's theory, which is hard to test. Related pieces
(suppression, expressive writing, self-compassion) have studies; shadow work itself has none I could find.

**2. Does it work?** Nobody knows. Jungian psychotherapy has some naturalistic evidence and no randomised trials.
Self-guided shadow journaling has none.

**3. Can it hurt?** Yes, for some people: rumination, self-blame, and surfacing of trauma. That is why the doc says
short sessions, a closing step, and a therapist for anything heavy.

**4. Do I have to believe in the shadow?** No. "Parts I do not like to admit" works just as well.

**5. Is everything that annoys me my shadow?** No. Sometimes it is a boundary, sometimes the other person is
being unfair. The mainstream research on projection is mixed.

**6. Should I do it daily?** Probably not. Two to three short sessions a week is a better fit.

**7. I have PTSD or a trauma history. Can I do it?** Only with a trauma-informed therapist.

**8. I have depression. Can I do it?** Not during an episode. Use a behavioural tool first and ask your clinician.

**9. I am trans. Is this for me?** Possibly, if you skip the binary anima and animus material and never treat your
gender as a shadow. Use trans-affirming support if you need it.

**10. I am autistic or have ADHD. Any changes?** Use concrete prompts, word lists and very short sessions, and
do not read every big reaction as symbolic.

**11. Is it a replacement for therapy?** No.

**12. What if it makes me feel worse?** Stop, ground yourself, and talk to a professional. In crisis: 988, or Trans
Lifeline at (877) 565-8860.

**13. Should I use an AI chatbot for it?** No. Not a clinician, and nothing is private enough.

### e. Don't adopt

- **Generative AI "shadow coaches" or chat.** Out of scope for the companion's firmware, and unsafe for this material.
- **Notifications, reminders, streaks or "shadow score" badges.** Out of scope; they add pressure to a practice that
  should be paused when hard.
- **Daily shadow prompts on the X4 sleep screen or as a feed.** Heavy material should not arrive unasked.
- **Anima and animus prompts.** They rest on a gender binary and do not fit trans and gender-diverse readers.
- **Anything that calls gender, sexuality or neurodivergence a "shadow".** Never.
- **Ritual and occult branding** (spells, cleansing, tarot). It is not the psychology and it is off-tone for a calm book.
- **Deep-dive trauma prompts** ("your darkest memory"). Send people to a therapist.
- **Active imagination pages.** Vivid and destabilising; therapist-led only.
- **Scanning shadow pages to the app.** Keep them off scan and send paths; consider Keeper-only pages.
- **Health claims** such as "heals trauma" or "integrates your dark side" in book copy.

## 26. Open questions

- Is there any controlled study of self-guided shadow work journaling? None was found.
- What share of people who do shadow work report harm (rumination, trauma surfacing)? No data.
- How much of the popular "projection" idea survives replication beyond the suppression-and-accessibility account?
- Do Jungian outcome results hold under randomised designs? Roesler says efficacy is unproven.
- What would a trans-affirming, non-binary revision of shadow work look like in practice, and has it been tested?
  Revised Jungian gender theory exists in the literature, but I could not open a source to confirm details.
- Do prompts help or hurt people with alexithymia or ADHD-related emotional dysregulation? No studies found.
- Which of the Support page numbers should the shadow page print? Keep the same list as the Therapy blocks.
