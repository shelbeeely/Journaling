# Mood tracking and Year in Pixels

> **Not medical advice.** This is a research summary for a journaling system. Mood charts can support care, but they don't
> diagnose anything. If a chart shows a pattern that worries you (weeks of low mood, a run of "wired" days with little sleep,
> thoughts of self-harm), take it to a clinician, and use the Safety plan at the back of the book.

Research checked 2026-09-28. Uncertain points are marked **(uncertain)**. Sources are in section 28.

## 2. Summary

Mood tracking means giving each day (or each few hours) a rating on a fixed scale and keeping those ratings where you can see
them together. The clinical form is the NIMH Life Chart Method: a daily rating of mood from severe depression to severe mania,
scored by how much the mood got in the way of normal life, charted alongside sleep, medication and life events, so a clinician
and patient can see the shape of an illness over months. Research tools like ecological momentary assessment (EMA) push the same
idea further: several short ratings a day, in the moment, to beat the distortions of memory. The Year in Pixels is the
friendly, visual cousin: a 12 × 31 grid, one square per day, coloured by how the day felt as a whole. Camille of Passion Carnets
drew the first one in December 2015 and bullet journallers made it one of the most copied layouts of the decade. The evidence is
honest but modest: daily ratings are a reliable way to *measure* mood, and they help people notice feelings, but tracking alone
has not been shown to *improve* mood, and for some people staring at a column of bad days feeds rumination. The craft is in
small choices: a scale with clear anchors, a zero that means "steady", a pattern key that works without colour, and a review
habit that looks for shape (cycles, seasons, sleep before a dip) instead of grading yourself.

## 3. History and origin

### Clinical mood charting

- **Kraepelin and earlier.** Psychiatrists drew illness "courses" by hand long before apps: a line above and below a baseline
  for manic and depressive episodes. This is the root of every mood graph **(uncertain on exact firsts; common history, not
  opened as a primary source)**.
- **Late 1980s: the NIMH life chart.** Robert M. Post and colleagues at the NIMH Biological Psychiatry Branch developed a
  graphic method for mapping the life course of affective illness (Post and colleagues, late 1980s **(uncertain: exact citation not verified)**). Gabriele Leverich and Post then developed retrospective and prospective versions (LCM-r, LCM-p).
- **1997: first reliability data.** Denicoff and colleagues reported preliminary reliability and validity for the prospective
  life chart in 30 outpatients with bipolar I and II in a lithium/carbamazepine trial (*Journal of Psychiatric Research*
  31(5):593–603).
- **2000: validation.** Denicoff et al. validated the clinician and patient versions: daily ratings based on functional
  impairment correlated highly with standard cross-sectional mood scales (*Psychological Medicine* 30(6):1391–1397).
- **2014: self-rating confirmed.** Born et al. (*BMC Psychiatry*) showed in 108 German bipolar patients that the
  **patient self-rated** life chart had "highly significant correlation and good concordance" with clinician ratings, so a paper
  chart kept at home is a valid, cheap way to track bipolar illness.
- **1990s–2000s: EMA.** Saul Shiffman, Arthur Stone and Michael Hufford named and organised ecological momentary assessment
  (Shiffman, Stone & Hufford, 2008, *Annual Review of Clinical Psychology*; the paper was referenced but its publisher page
  blocked access). Burke et al. (2017, *JMIR*) give the working definition used here: EMA "assesses individuals' current
  experiences, behaviors, and moods as they occur in real time and in their natural environment."
- **2010s: phones.** Mood apps arrive. eMoods (Yottaram LLC) grew out of bipolar charting; Daylio (Habitics s.r.o., EU)
  launched in 2015 with a 5-face scale and later added its own "Year in Pixels" view. Trials test whether phone self-monitoring
  *treats* anything (MONARCA I 2015, MONARCA II 2019; Kauer 2012).
- **2016–2017: premenstrual charting gets rules.** The Carolina Premenstrual Assessment Scoring System (C-PASS; Eisenlohr-Moul
  et al., *Am J Psychiatry* 2017) turned two cycles of daily ratings into a standard way to diagnose PMDD.

### Year in Pixels

- **Who:** Camille, French, of the blog and Instagram account Passion Carnets (@passion.carnets / @passioncarnets). The
  Bullet Journal blog describes her as a community manager and faculty teacher.
- **When:** She started bullet journalling in July 2015. In December 2015, trackers were the big trend in the community; she
  drew a 12-column × 31-row grid for 2016, one small square ("pixel") per day, and coloured each by the day as a whole.
- **Inspiration:** Tim Urban's "Your Life in Weeks" (Wait But Why, 7 May 2014), a poster of the ~4,680 weeks of a 90-year life
  as small boxes.
- **Spread:** The idea went viral through Instagram, Pinterest and Tumblr in 2016–2017. Ryder Carroll's official Bullet Journal
  blog ran a "Deep dive: Year in Pixels" interview with Camille on 1 July 2017. Planner companies (Passion Planner's free
  printable in three sizes) and apps (Daylio) later adopted the name.
- **Cultural context:** Mid-2010s bullet journalling moved from task lists to trackers (habit, sleep, water, mood). The Year in
  Pixels fit that mood perfectly: one mark a day, very little writing, and a big satisfying picture by December.

### Key quotes

- Camille, on what she rates: "I don't track my different emotions, but rather how I perceive the day in its entirety:
  emotions, feelings and events." (Bullet Journal blog, 2017)
- Tim Urban, on the grid that inspired it: "Those are your weeks and they're all you've got." (2014)
- Born et al. on self-rated life charts: a "time and cost-effective alternative" to clinician ratings (paraphrase of their
  conclusion, 2014).

## 4. Philosophy and principles

**Stated goals**

- *Clinical charting:* catch the start of an episode early; see whether a treatment helps; replace "how have you been?" at a
  15-minute appointment with a picture of the last three months.
- *Year in Pixels:* notice the year you actually had, not the one memory edits; enjoy a small ritual; see that bad stretches
  end.

**Core beliefs**

1. **Memory is a bad historian.** Retrospective recall is biased by today's mood and by peaks and endings. Rating in the moment
   (or at least the same evening) beats remembering on Sunday.
2. **Rate the day, not your worth.** A number is weather, not a grade. Camille rates "the day in its entirety."
3. **Anchor the scale to something you can observe.** The NIMH-LCM scores severity by how much mood got in the way of usual
   roles, not by how intense it felt. That makes ratings more consistent from day to day.
4. **Shape over single points.** One bad day tells you almost nothing. Four weeks show a cycle; a year shows a season.
5. **Context makes the chart readable.** Sleep, meds, cycle day, work shifts and big events on the same row turn a coloured
   grid into something you can reason about.

**Problems it solves**

- "I can't remember how the last month was" at a prescriber visit.
- Not noticing a slow slide (winter, a med wearing off, burnout building).
- For bipolar disorder: missing early warning signs of mania or depression.
- For premenstrual and hormone-linked mood: showing (or ruling out) a link to cycle days.

### Daily rating

One mark, same time each day. Evening works for most people because the day is done. Shift workers should rate at the end of
*their* day, not at midnight (see section 19). Life charts ask for the **worst impairment** of the day; Year in Pixels asks for the
**overall feel**. Pick one and write it on the key, because mixing them makes the year unreadable.

### Scale and anchors

Three common designs:

| Scale | Pros | Cons | Good anchors |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1–5** (Daylio: awful, bad, meh, good, rad) | Fast, easy, fits 5 patterns | No true "neutral" if you don't treat 3 as one; can't show elevation separately from "good" | 1 awful · 2 bad · 3 okay · 4 good · 5 great |
| **−3..+3** (Journalwright Studio default, bipolar charts) | 0 = steady; the sign carries direction; +3 can mean "wired", not "great" | Negative numbers can feel harsh; 7 levels are hard to draw in B&W | −3 can't function · −2 hard to do basics · −1 low but managing · 0 steady · +1 bright · +2 revved · +3 wired, not sleeping |
| **0–10** | Fine-grained, familiar from pain scales | People use 5–8 only; hard to draw | Pin 0, 5, 10 with words |
| **NIMH-LCM** (−4..+4 by impairment: mild, moderate-low, moderate-high, severe) | Validated; anchored on function | Clinical, needs training; built for bipolar | "Mild: some effort, no loss of function" … "Severe: unable to function" (paraphrase) |

Design rules that the research and practice agree on:

- **Anchor the ends and the middle with words**, not just faces. Faces are culture- and alexithymia-dependent.
- **Keep the scale fixed for the whole year.** Changing it in June breaks every comparison.
- **Say what "high" means.** In a bipolar scale, +3 is a warning (little sleep, racing), not a great day. In a wellbeing scale, 5 is
  a great day. The Studio's default key says "+3 very high/wired", which is the bipolar meaning; if you want "great day", use a
  separate "good things" line, not +3.
- **Separate dimensions when they diverge.** Mood, anxiety and irritability move separately. eMoods rates depressed, elevated,
  irritable and anxious on their own rows. The Studio already separates mood (on paper) from anxiety (on the X4 companion, or a Scale block on paper).
- **Allow a range.** On mixed or up-and-down days, mark the low and the high (split cell, or two dots joined by a line).

### Pattern and colour key

Colour is the classic Year in Pixels look, but Journalwright Studio books print black-and-white, and about 1 in 12 men (fewer women) have
colour-vision deficiency, mostly red-green (National Eye Institute). Three B&W encodings that work:

1. **Fill density** (best for 5 levels): empty □ · one dot ⊡ · one diagonal ▨ · cross-hatch ▩ · solid ■.
2. **Numbers in the cell** (best for 7 levels or −3..+3): write −2, 0, +1. Readable, scannable, zero ambiguity.
3. **Position in the cell** (life-chart style): a short tick high in the cell for up, low for down, middle for steady.

Rule of thumb: darker = further from steady. For a signed scale, use hatch direction for sign (/ for low, \ for high) and
density for size, so −1 is one light slash and +3 is dense back-slashes. Test it: a stranger should read the key in 10 seconds.

### Range and split cells

The NIMH-LCM lets a day carry both a depressive and a manic rating and tracks "ultradian" (within-day) cycling. On paper, draw a
diagonal through the cell: top-left triangle = the day's high, bottom-right = the day's low.

### Context rows

The life chart plots medication doses, sleep hours, life events, substance use, irritability and menstrual cycle beside mood (Born
et al., 2014). You don't need all of them. Pick the two or three that you suspect matter: **sleep** almost always; **cycle day**
if you menstruate; **HRT dose day** if you inject; **work shift** if you work nights.

### Anxiety and irritability rows

A single mood line hides a lot. Anxiety 0–3 (the X4 companion's check-in), irritability 0–3 (eMoods-style) are cheap to add.

### Notes

A single word ("argument", "sun!", "no sleep") is enough. Notes make the pattern readable in December.

### EMA pings

Research EMA uses random prompts several times a day. Burke et al. found 8 prompts a day too many; 5 was acceptable. Kauer et al.
used 4 a day for 2–4 weeks. For a journal, **one to three check-ins a day** is realistic (morning, after work, bedtime). The X4
check-in is a gentle, pull-only version: you open it; it never pings.

### Month overview

31 rows, one per day, with the mood cells and context columns side by side. This is where most patterns show up first.

### Year in Pixels

The iconic page: months across the top, days 1–31 down the side, one cell per day. Grey out days that don't exist (Feb 30,
Apr 31).

### Weekly line

Seven dots joined by a line on a −3..+3 grid. The week spread already prints this "Mood line".

### Review ritual

See section 8. The chart only helps if you look at it on purpose, briefly, with a question in mind.

## 6. Setting it up

**First setup (15–20 minutes, once)**

1. **Decide why.** Write one line: "I'm tracking to see whether ___." (sleep affects mood; winter hits me; my new med works; my
   cycle matters). A reason keeps the chart small.
2. **Choose one scale** and write its anchors on the key page. If you have (or might have) bipolar disorder, use a signed scale
   where + means elevated. Otherwise 1–5 is fine.
3. **Choose the encoding**: patterns for 5 levels, numbers for 7.
4. **Pick two context rows** at most (sleep plus one).
5. **Pick the time**: "when I brush my teeth at night" or "end of shift".
6. **Draw or find the pages**: a month tracker for each month, and one Year in Pixels page if you want the big picture.
7. **Write your stop rule.** "If I notice I'm checking the chart to beat myself up, I'll switch to words only for two weeks."

**First day**

- Rate once, at your chosen time. Takes 10 seconds. Add one word if something stood out.
- Don't go back and fill earlier days from memory. Start today.

**First week**

- Rate every day you remember. Leave misses **blank**, never guessed. A blank is data ("I was busy or away").
- On day 7, look at the week line for 1 minute. Ask: "Did the anchors fit? Is +3 ever real for me? Is anything missing?"
- Adjust the anchor *words* now if needed, then freeze the scale for the year.

## 7. Daily practice

**Order of operations (evening, about 60 seconds)**

1. Pause, one breath. Ask: "Taking the whole day, where was I, mostly?" (Or, life-chart style: "How much did mood get in the way
   today?")
2. Mark the number or pattern. On a mixed day, mark low and high.
3. Mark anxiety 0–3 if you track it.
4. Fill context: sleep hours last night, meds taken, cycle day, shift.
5. One word note, optional.
6. Close the page. Don't look back over the month tonight.

**Prompts for the note**

- What moved the needle today?
- Body first: tired, hungry, in pain, dysphoric?
- One thing that helped even a little.

**Timing tips**

- Same time daily keeps ratings comparable.
- If you missed the evening, rate the next morning only if you can recall clearly; otherwise leave it blank.
- EMA-style extra check-ins (morning and mid-shift) are optional, and most useful when a clinician asks for them or you're testing
  a within-day pattern (for example, afternoon crashes).

## 8. Rhythms

**Weekly (5 minutes, weekly review)**

- Look at the 7-day line. One question only: "What came before the lowest day?" Often sleep, a hard shift, or a social load.
- Don't calculate averages weekly; the noise is too high.

**Monthly (10–15 minutes, Closing the month)**

- Count days at each level, or average the numbers (the Closing the month page has an "Avg mood" box filled in from the paper tracker).
- Scan for shapes (see "How to read patterns" below).
- Copy the month average to the Keeper's Year at a glance.
- Write one sentence: "This month the chart showed ___."

**Quarterly (20 minutes)**

- Compare three months side by side. Look for trend (slowly lower?), and whether a change you made (new med, new schedule) shows up
  2–6 weeks later.
- If you see a worrying run, book the appointment and bring the pages.

**Yearly (30–60 minutes, Looking back)**

- Finish the Year in Pixels. Look from arm's length first: bands of dark (seasons), stripes (weekly or cyclical), dots (one-offs).
- Archive the year: photograph or scan the grid; keep the Keeper.
- Decide whether to track next year, and whether to change the scale (only at year boundaries).

### How to read patterns

| Pattern | What it looks like | What to check | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Weekly** | Same weekday dark every week | Work schedule, a standing appointment, the day after a long shift | Very common and usually boring, which is useful |
| **Monthly/cyclical** | Dips about every 4 weeks | Menstrual cycle day (luteal phase), injection cycle for HRT | Needs 2–3 cycles before you trust it; C-PASS uses 2 cycles of daily ratings |
| **Seasonal** | A dark band Nov–Feb (winter pattern) or Jun–Aug (summer pattern, rarer) | Daylight, sleep, light therapy timing | Seasonal affective disorder is real (NIMH) but one bad winter isn't a pattern; you need 2+ years |
| **Sleep-led** | Short sleep 1–3 nights before a high or low | Sleep column vs mood | For bipolar disorder, shrinking sleep with rising mood is a classic warning sign |
| **Event-led** | Isolated dark pixels | Notes column | One-offs aren't patterns; don't over-read |
| **Slow drift** | Monthly averages sliding | Med changes, burnout, grief | Averages hide variance; look at spread too |
| **Rapid swings** | Alternating light and dark within days | Mixed states, ultradian cycling, sleep chaos | Bring to a clinician |
| **Moon phase** | Suspected link to full moon | The Studio's sky and moon pages print the moon | Research evidence for lunar effects on mood is weak and inconsistent **(uncertain; not reviewed here)**; treat any link as personal and provisional |

**Menstrual cycle and HRT.** For people who menstruate, the DRSP (Daily Record of Severity of Problems) and C-PASS rely on
prospective daily ratings across at least two cycles, comparing the week before bleeding with the week after. Track "cycle day"
(day 1 = first day of bleeding) in a context column. For trans people on injectable estradiol or testosterone, community reports
describe mood shifts near the trough before the next dose; a "dose day" tick in the tracker lets you test that for yourself
**(anecdotal; not a researched finding in this doc)**.

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Creator | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| **NIMH Life Chart (LCM-p, LCM-r, self-rated)** | Post, Leverich, Denicoff and colleagues, NIMH, 1988–2000 | Severity by impairment; ± for depression/mania; with meds, sleep, events |
| **Daily mood chart (bipolar clinics)** | Many clinics and charities (for example DBSA, Black Dog Institute) **(not opened)** | Simplified life chart: mood −3..+3, sleep, meds, notes |
| **EMA / experience sampling** | Shiffman, Stone, Hufford; Csikszentmihalyi's experience sampling before that | Several random prompts a day; research tool |
| **Year in Pixels** | Camille, Passion Carnets, Dec 2015 | Whole-year 12 × 31 grid, one colour per day |
| **Mood mandala / circle tracker** | Bullet journal community | Days arranged as petals or rings; decorative |
| **Mood bar / mood jar** | Community | Colour a strip or a jar shape per day; playful |
| **Daylio-style 5-face log** | Daylio (2015) | Faces plus activity tags; app shows correlations and a pixel view |
| **eMoods multi-row** | eMoods (Yottaram) | Separate rows for depressed, elevated, irritable, anxious; PDF for doctor |
| **DRSP / C-PASS premenstrual chart** | Endicott et al. (DRSP); Eisenlohr-Moul et al. 2017 (C-PASS) | Many symptoms rated daily over two cycles, scored against cycle phase |
| **Affect grid** | Russell and colleagues (1989) **(not opened)** | Two axes: pleasant–unpleasant and calm–activated; one dot per rating |
| **Mood meter** | Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence (RULER) **(not opened)** | Four coloured quadrants from the same two axes; word lists per quadrant |
| **DBT diary card** | Marsha Linehan | Emotions, urges and skills rated daily; see [dbt-diary-card](dbt-diary-card.md) |

## 12. Community practice

**What people actually do**

- **Pinterest and Instagram:** Year in Pixels spreads with elaborate borders, shaped grids (a heart, a tree whose leaves are days),
  and 5–10 colour keys. Posts peak in late December (setup) and January (first month filled).
- **Reddit (r/bulletjournal, r/BipolarReddit, r/PMDD, r/ADHD):** practical threads: "I kept forgetting, so I rate at teeth
  time"; "my pixels were all the same colour so I switched to 1–10"; "tracking made me obsess, I stopped"; clinicians asking for
  printouts; people with PMDD overlaying cycle days.
- **YouTube:** "Plan with me" videos build the grid; "year end flip-through" videos show the finished picture and talk about
  what it revealed (often winter dips).
- **Common drop-off:** many Year in Pixels grids stop in March. People who finish usually keep it next to something they already
  open daily.

**Common customisations**

- Two colours per pixel (split diagonally) for mixed days.
- A second grid for sleep, anxiety or pain, sharing the same layout.
- Words instead of colours ("tired", "calm") with one colour per word.
- Weekly pixels (52 cells) for people who can't do daily.
- Shape grids (circles, hexagons) for the look.

**Aesthetic versus minimalist camps**

- *Aesthetic:* colour, themed keys, decorative grids; enjoyment keeps them going; risk is setup fatigue.
- *Minimalist:* pencil numbers in a plain 31-row column; fast and durable; risk is boredom. Journalwright Studio books sit firmly here: black
  and white, numbers or hatching, no ornament.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Pencil (HB or 2B):** erasable, never bleeds, scans well. The best default.
- **Fine liner 0.3–0.5 mm:** for hatch patterns. Test for bleed on the book's paper.
- **Colour (optional):** coloured pencils don't bleed; highlighters and alcohol markers ghost or bleed on 60–80 gsm paper.
- **Ruler:** only if you draw your own grid.
- **Stamps:** a small square stamp or a set of pattern stamps can make pixels quick and even.
- **Paper:** KDP paperback white paper is about 55 lb (~90 gsm) **(uncertain; check KDP spec)**; solid fills with markers can
  show through. Hatching shows through less than solid fills.

## 16. Evidence and research

**Measurement: good**

| Study | Sample | Finding | Strength / limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Denicoff et al. 1997, *J Psychiatr Res* | 30 bipolar I/II outpatients in a 2-year drug trial | Preliminary reliability and validity of prospective life chart | Small; one trial setting |
| Denicoff et al. 2000, *Psychol Med* | Bipolar patients (clinician and patient versions) | Daily impairment-based ratings correlated highly with standard mood scales | Validation of measurement, not of benefit |
| Born et al. 2014, *BMC Psychiatry* | 108 bipolar I/II, German network, 1999–2002 | Self-rated life chart concordant with clinician ratings | Bipolar only; older data |
| Burke et al. 2017, *JMIR* | EMA study (smoking lapse) | 88% prompt completion early on; 8 prompts/day too many, 5 acceptable | Shows the burden limit of in-the-moment tracking |

**Self-monitoring as an intervention: mixed**

| Study | Sample | Finding | Strength / limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kauer et al. 2012, *JMIR* (RCT) | 114 people aged 14–24 in Australian GP clinics; 4 prompts/day for 2–4 weeks | Monitoring mood raised emotional self-awareness more than monitoring only activities; ESA predicted lower depression (indirect effect) | No direct effect on depression; both groups improved; under-recruited |
| Faurholt-Jepsen et al. 2015, MONARCA I, *Psychol Med* (RCT) | 78 bipolar patients, 6 months | No significant effect on depressive or manic symptoms; a trend toward *sustained* depressive symptoms in the monitoring group | Single-blind; authors urge "critical consideration" before routine use |
| Faurholt-Jepsen et al. 2019/2020, MONARCA II (RCT) | 129 bipolar patients, 9 months | No difference in symptoms; better quality of life and lower perceived stress; secondary: more depressive episodes, fewer manic | Mixed; subgroup results are exploratory |
| Dubad et al. 2018, *Psychol Med* (systematic review) | 25 articles, ages 10–24 | Good usability; emotional self-awareness up; patient–clinician communication better; psychometrics sparse; adult literature suggests apps "may increase negative reactivity" | Few high-quality studies; only one primary RCT |
| Wright et al. 2025, *npj Digital Medicine* (qualitative meta-synthesis) | 14 studies, 457 people with depression | Benefits: self-awareness, insight, useful in therapy. Harms in a majority of studies: rumination, compulsive checking, reminders of illness, "the results would indicate I was in a much worse mood than I thought" | Qualitative; can't size the effects |

**Claimed versus supported**

- *Supported:* daily self-ratings are a valid way to record mood (especially bipolar); tracking raises emotional self-awareness;
  charts help conversations with clinicians.
- *Plausible, weakly supported:* seeing patterns leads to useful changes (sleep, light, timing of meds).
- *Not supported:* that tracking by itself lifts mood or prevents episodes.
- *Real risk:* rumination and distress for some people with depression; burden with too many prompts.

## 17. Benefits

- Replaces fuzzy recall with a record. **Evidenced** (life-chart validation; EMA rationale).
- Raises emotional self-awareness. **Evidenced** (Kauer 2012; Dubad 2018).
- Improves conversations with prescribers and therapists. **Evidenced** (qualitative; Dubad 2018; Wright 2025).
- Shows early warning signs of bipolar episodes (sleep drops, rising scores). **Claimed/clinical practice**; trials don't show that
  monitoring alone prevents episodes.
- Reveals seasonal and cycle patterns. **Claimed** for individuals; the conditions are evidenced (NIMH on SAD; C-PASS for PMDD).
- Shows that bad stretches end ("the dark band stopped in March"). **Claimed**.
- Quality of life and stress improved with monitoring in MONARCA II. **Evidenced (one trial)**.
- A calming 10-second ritual. **Claimed**.
- A beautiful artefact at year's end. **Claimed** (and obvious).

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stops in March | Novelty fades; the grid lives somewhere you don't open daily | Put the daily mark in the day page or X4; copy to the year grid monthly, not daily |
| Everything is "3" | Scale too coarse or anchors vague | Rewrite anchors with observable behaviour; or switch to signed −3..+3 |
| Ruminating on dark pixels | Tracking turns into self-judgment | Rate, close the book; look only at the weekly review; take a two-week "words only" break; talk to a clinician |
| Checking obsessively (apps) | Graphs are always there | Paper; or look at stats once a month |
| Filling gaps from memory | Wanting a complete grid | Leave blanks; blanks are honest data |
| Changing the scale mid-year | Early anchors didn't fit | Adjust anchors in week 1 only; otherwise wait for January |
| Over-reading coincidences | One full moon, one bad day | Need 2–3 repeats before calling it a pattern |
| Colour doesn't print | B&W book | Hatch patterns or numbers |
| "Good" and "elevated" mixed up | 1–5 wellbeing scale used for bipolar | Signed scale where + = revved; track good things separately |
| Scale mismatch across tools | Paper 5 steps, device −3..+3 | Pick 7 steps on paper (signed −3…+3) to match a signed X4 Scale |
| Mixed days flattened | One mark per day | Split cell or low/high dots |
| Shift-work days don't fit midnight | Days cross midnight | Rate at the end of your waking day; write the date the day started |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** Put the rating where you already look (day page top, or an X4 check-in). Use a physical anchor habit (teeth). Accept
  gaps. Numbers are faster than colouring. See [adhd-journaling](adhd-journaling.md).
- **Autism and sensory needs:** Alexithymia makes "how do you feel?" hard. Rate observable things instead: energy, sensory load,
  sleep, how much you could do. Use word lists (Words to circle). Keep the grid visually quiet. See
  [autistic-sensory-journaling](autistic-sensory-journaling.md).
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** One mark is enough. Pair mood with spoons to separate "sad" from "depleted". See
  [spoon-theory-energy-accounting](spoon-theory-energy-accounting.md) and [symptom-tracking](symptom-tracking.md).
- **Depression and anxiety:** The biggest risk group for rumination (Wright 2025). Use a "rate and close" rule, avoid looking at
  the year grid during a low patch, add one "small good thing" line so the page isn't only a score. If you notice suicidal
  thoughts, go to the Safety plan, not the tracker.
- **Trauma:** Anniversaries show up as dark pixels; that is expected, not failure. Neutral anchors ("low energy" rather than
  "terrible") feel safer. Allow a "skip" mark.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** Circles and patterns, no writing required. Large cells.
- **Low vision:** 8.5×11 trim, large cells, numbers instead of fine hatching, high contrast; the X4 font can be large.
- **Motor or hand pain:** One tick or dot, not colouring a whole cell; pixel stamps; the X4's buttons.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** Add a "dose day" tick for HRT and a separate dysphoria scale (the editor has a Dysphoria
  scale preset and a "Cycle & dose" checkbox preset) so gender stress doesn't vanish inside a general mood number. Cycle tracking for people who menstruate, without
  gendered labels.
- **Shift workers:** Rate at the end of your waking day; add a shift column; expect weekday patterns to follow the rota, not the
  calendar.
- **Low income:** Pencil and any paper. Free printables. No app subscription needed.

## 23. Glossary

- **Affect:** the feeling tone of experience; in research, often split into valence (pleasant–unpleasant) and arousal (calm–activated).
- **Anchor:** the words tied to a scale point so the number means the same thing each day.
- **Alexithymia:** difficulty identifying and describing one's own emotions.
- **Bipolar I / II:** mood disorders with episodes of mania (I) or hypomania (II) and depression.
- **C-PASS:** Carolina Premenstrual Assessment Scoring System; scores two cycles of daily ratings to diagnose PMDD.
- **Cycle day:** days since the first day of menstrual bleeding (day 1).
- **DRSP:** Daily Record of Severity of Problems; a daily premenstrual symptom form.
- **EMA (ecological momentary assessment):** repeated, in-the-moment ratings in everyday life.
- **Emotional self-awareness (ESA):** noticing and naming one's emotions; raised by mood monitoring in Kauer 2012.
- **Euthymia:** steady, neither depressed nor elevated (0 on a signed scale).
- **Experience sampling:** an older name for EMA-style random prompting.
- **Hypomania / mania:** elevated or irritable mood with more energy and less need for sleep; mania is more severe.
- **Life chart (NIMH-LCM):** a daily chart of mood severity (by impairment), with meds, sleep and events.
- **Luteal phase:** the part of the menstrual cycle after ovulation, before bleeding; premenstrual symptoms cluster here.
- **Mixed state:** depressive and elevated features at once.
- **Pixel:** one day's cell in a Year in Pixels.
- **PMDD:** premenstrual dysphoric disorder.
- **Reactivity:** when the act of measuring changes the thing measured.
- **Rumination:** repetitive, passive dwelling on distress and its causes.
- **SAD:** seasonal affective disorder; winter pattern or summer pattern.
- **Signed scale:** a scale with a zero in the middle and negative and positive values.
- **Split cell:** a pixel divided diagonally to show a day's low and high.
- **Ultradian cycling:** mood shifts within a single day.

## 24. FAQ

1. **Is 1–5 or −3..+3 better?** For general wellbeing, 1–5 is fine. If elevated mood is a risk for you (bipolar, some meds), use a
   signed scale where + means revved, not great. The Studio's default mood scale is −3..+3.
2. **Morning or evening?** Evening, for "the day as a whole." Morning ratings mostly capture sleep.
3. **I missed three days. Should I fill them in?** No. Leave them blank. Blanks keep the rest honest.
4. **Can I do Year in Pixels without colour?** Yes: hatch patterns for 5 levels, numbers for 7. It's easier to read and scan.
5. **Will tracking make me feel better?** Maybe more aware, not necessarily better. Trials show no average improvement in mood from
   tracking alone.
6. **It's making me feel worse. What now?** Stop looking back; rate and close; try words only for two weeks; or stop. Tell your
   clinician. That's a known effect, not a personal failure (Wright 2025).
7. **How long before I can trust a pattern?** Weekly: 3–4 weeks. Cycle: 2–3 cycles. Seasonal: 2 years.
8. **Should my doctor see it?** If you have a mood condition, a monthly summary is often welcome. Bring the month page and one sentence.
9. **What about mixed days?** Split the cell, or mark both low and high.
10. **Does the moon matter?** Evidence is weak. The Studio prints the moon for interest; treat any link as a personal hunch.
11. **Can I track mood on the X4 companion and on paper?** The built-in X4 check-ins keep mood on paper, but a Scale block set to Signed (−3…+3) also exports to the X4 as a custom check-in. Use 7 steps on paper so both are −3..+3.

### e. Don't adopt

- **Colour-only keys.** The book is B&W and 1 in 12 men can't tell red from green.
- **Streaks, badges, "you logged 30 days!"** Gamifies feelings; the firmware scope forbids badges.
- **Automatic mood insights or correlations on the device.** Out of scope (no AI) and risks telling someone what they feel.
- **Reminder pings.** No notifications. The chart must be pull-only.
- **Mood on the sleep screen.** Waking to yesterday's −2 is a rumination trigger.
- **Emoji faces as the only anchors.** Hard for alexithymia and in B&W; also Type 3 font risk unless drawn as SVG.
- **Shaped decorative grids (hearts, trees).** Busy ornament; breaks the calm design and makes scan reading harder.
- **Many mood rows by default.** One mood and one anxiety is the default; the rest are opt-in.

## 26. Open questions

- Does a *paper* chart carry the same rumination risk as an app? The harms evidence (Wright 2025) is mostly from apps and EMA.
- Is daily mood tracking helpful for autistic people with alexithymia, or does energy/sensory tracking work better? Little direct
  research found.
- Evidence on mood cycles linked to injectable HRT troughs in trans people: not reviewed here; community reports only.
- Lunar effects: not reviewed in depth; widely believed, weakly supported **(uncertain)**.
- Best number of scale points for paper self-rating: no strong answer; 5 vs 7 trade speed for detail.
- Whether the MONARCA finding (more depressive episodes with monitoring) generalises beyond one Danish clinic.
- The official home for NIMH-LCM forms today: not found.
