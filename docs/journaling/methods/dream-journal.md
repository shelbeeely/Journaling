# Dream journal (dream diaries, recall, lucid logs, nightmare rescripting)

Research doc for Journalwright Studio. Sources checked 2026-09-28. Anything marked **[uncertain]** came from memory or a source I could not open; verify before relying on it.

**Health note.** Nightmares and sleep problems can be signs of something a clinician should look at. This doc describes research, not treatment. If you are in crisis, use your safety plan ([safety-planning.md](safety-planning.md)) or call or text 988 (US).

## 1. At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Creator** | No single creator. Old practice; modern research lineage includes Calvin Hall and Robert Van de Castle (content analysis), G. William Domhoff (DreamBank), Stephen LaBerge (lucid dreaming, MILD), Barry Krakow (Imagery Rehearsal Therapy) |
| **Year** | Ancient practice. Hall/Van de Castle coding is 1960s **[uncertain on date; not opened]**. IRT trial 2001. DreamBank is 2000s |
| **Type** | Journal / log (a morning capture practice, with optional analysis, lucid-dream training or nightmare rescripting) |
| **Time per day** | 2-10 minutes on waking. Lucid training adds evening and night steps. IRT is 10-20 minutes a day (AASM 2018) |
| **Time per week** | 15-70 minutes for a basic diary |
| **Cost** | Free. A notebook and a pen by the bed |
| **Formats and sizes** | Pocket notebook, a page in a day book, voice memo. Keep it small enough to reach without sitting up |
| **Best for** | Curiosity about your inner life; tracking sleep and mood patterns; learning lucid dreaming; managing recurring nightmares (with the right method) |
| **Not ideal for** | Acute trauma with frequent nightmares and no support (see IRT and section 19); anyone for whom broken sleep is already a problem (WBTB lucid training cuts sleep); people who find dream content distressing to revisit |
| **Learning curve** | Low for the diary; medium for MILD; IRT is best learned with a clinician if trauma-related |
| **Supplies** | Notebook, pen with a light or dim lamp, optional voice recorder |
| **Official site** | None. DreamBank (dreambank.net) is the main public research archive; dreams.ucsc.edu hosts the Hall/Van de Castle coding rules and norms |
| **Evidence level** | Mixed. **Strong** for IRT for nightmares (AASM Level A) and for the fact that logs capture more dreams; **some studies** for lucid induction (low quality, small success rates); **none** for "dream diaries improve your life" in general |

## 2. Summary

A dream journal is a habit of writing down dreams right after waking. It does three separate jobs, and it matters which one you want. First, it trains recall: most people forget almost everything within minutes, and a log makes you notice more (though researchers are still arguing about whether logs enhance recall or just measure it better; Aspy et al. 2015). Second, it makes dreams into data: coding systems like Hall and Van de Castle's, and the 20,000-plus reports on DreamBank (the site says over 20,000; a 2008 paper said over 22,000), show that dream content follows waking concerns in ways you can count. Third, it is the base for two trainable skills: lucid dreaming (MILD and related techniques; success rates in the largest field study I opened were about 16% in the second week) and imagery rehearsal for nightmares (rewrite the nightmare while awake and rehearse the new version; the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends this). It is not a proven mood or wellbeing intervention, and the evidence that lucid dreaming helps mental health is thin and comes with cautions about sleep disruption. In a Journalwright Studio book it fits as a small, optional morning block plus a bed-side Keeper insert, with a clear line between "capture" and "rescripting."

## 3. History and origin

### Ancient to modern
People have recorded dreams for millennia: temple incubation, letters, diaries, Freud's *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1899-1900), Jung's *Red Book* **[uncertain; general history, not opened]**. Freud treated dreams as coded wishes. Modern research mostly rejects one-to-one symbol dictionaries and looks at content, continuity with waking life and brain state.

### Cultural context
The dream diary sits between mystical tradition and lab science. The fashion side (dream dictionaries, interpretation apps) mostly has no evidence. The research side is about counting content, understanding sleep stages and treating nightmares.

### Quote
Hall and Van de Castle's site note about coding: "the system needs to be understood and mastered through hours of practice, but it need not be memorized" (dreams.ucsc.edu/Coding, paraphrase of the site's summary; I could not confirm exact wording).

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- **Capture first.** Write before you interpret. Dreams fade fast.
- **Dreams are not a code.** Research supports content patterns and continuity with waking concerns, not fixed symbol meanings **[the continuity claim is from the Domhoff line of work; I did not open a source that states it directly, so treat as uncertain]**.
- **Frequency is not the point.** Some people recall five or more dreams a week and some fewer than two a month (Vallat 2022); both are normal ranges.
- **Nightmares are treatable.** Rewriting and rehearsing is a first-line treatment (AASM).
- **Lucid dreaming is a skill, not a lifestyle.** Techniques exist, success is modest, and there are trade-offs for sleep (Soffer-Dudek 2019).
- **Sleep comes first.** A dream journal must never cost you the sleep it is about.
- **The problem it solves.** Curiosity, self-knowledge, pattern spotting, and a way to work with distressing dreams.

## 5. Core components

| Element | What it is |
|---|---|
| Bedside capture | Notebook, pen, dim light, within seconds of waking |
| Dream entry | Date, title, what happened, feelings, people and places |
| Recall rating | How much you remember, simple scale |
| Dreamsigns | Recurring odd features (flying, teeth, a certain place) that can cue lucidity |
| Emotion tags | The main feeling, which may not be fear even in nightmares (Robert & Zadra 2014) |
| Waking-life link | Yesterday's events, stress, medications |
| Coding (optional) | Hall/Van de Castle categories or your own tally |
| Lucid log | Techniques used, time to fall back asleep, lucid yes or no |
| Nightmare log | Frequency, distress, wakeups; then the rescripted version |
| Sleep context | Bed and wake times, alcohol, caffeine, stress |
| Review | Weekly or monthly scan for patterns |

### 5.1 Bedside capture
Keep the notebook where you can reach it without standing. Do not check your phone first; screens pull you into waking mode **[uncertain: common advice, not tied to a study I opened]**. Stay still, keep eyes closed, run through the dream once, then write.

### 5.2 Dream entry
Fields that work: date; night's sleep; the dream in the present tense, plain words; the feeling at the end; any "this is odd" details.

### 5.3 Recall rating
A 0-3 scale (nothing, a fragment, a scene, a full story) is enough. Logs and one-question retrospective estimates give different recall rates, and researchers debate why (Aspy et al. 2015). Personal ratings are for your own tracking.

### 5.4 Dreamsigns
A list of things that repeat or are impossible in waking life. In MILD you rehearse recognising them (LaBerge et al. 2018 describe visualising "anomalies characteristic of their dreams").

### 5.5 Emotion tags
In 9,796 reports from 331 adults over 2-5 weeks, 35% of nightmares and 55% of bad dreams had a primary emotion other than fear (anger, sadness, frustration) (Robert & Zadra 2014). So do not assume "scary."

### 5.6 Coding
Hall and Van de Castle have nine general categories on the coding page (dreams.ucsc.edu/Coding) and ten in the Chapter 2 text (which adds Food and Eating and Elements from the Past). The combined list: Characters; Social Interactions (aggression, friendliness, sexuality); Activities; Striving (success and failure); Misfortunes and Good Fortunes; Emotions; Settings/Physical Surroundings; Objects; Descriptive Elements; and, in the chapter, Food and Eating and Elements from the Past. The two pages count differently, so treat "nine or ten" as unresolved.

### 5.7 Lucid log
Date, technique, time awake in the middle of the night, minutes to fall asleep, lucid or not, how long, what you did.

### 5.8 Nightmare log
Frequency per week; wakeups; distress 0-10; whether you were able to rescript and rehearse. Note: prospective logs may overestimate nightmare frequency by increasing recall (AASM 2010).

### 5.9 Sleep context
Bed time, wake time, alcohol, caffeine, screens, stress. Helps separate "dream" problems from "sleep" problems.

### 5.10 Review
Weekly: count entries. Monthly: read them, pick one pattern. Yearly: reread and note what changed.

## 6. Setting it up

1. **Notebook.** Small, bound, with a pen tied or clipped on. Or a page you can find in the dark.
2. **Light.** A dim lamp or a small clip light. Avoid bright overhead light.
3. **Location.** Within arm's reach of the pillow.
4. **Intention (optional).** Before sleep, tell yourself "I will remember my dreams" (a common instruction in recall training **[uncertain: not tied to an opened source]**).
5. **Alarm.** A gentle one. Sudden alarms can wipe recall. Write whatever remains.
6. **Privacy.** Decide who sees this. Dream content can be intimate and can include trauma. Keep it in the Keeper if you want no scan codes (section 25).
7. **A stop line.** Decide beforehand: if a dream is distressing, you write three lines and no more, then get up and do something grounding.

**First day:** even a fragment counts: "woke with a feeling of a long staircase." Date it.
**First week:** aim for 3+ entries, without pressure. Fragments are fine.

## 7. Daily practice

| Step | Time | What |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Wake still | 10-30 s | Do not move much. Replay the dream |
| 2. Note | 1 min | Date, a title, the last scene first (often easiest) |
| 3. Expand | 2-5 min | People, places, actions, what you felt |
| 4. Tag | 30 s | Recall 0-3; main emotion; nightmare y/n; lucid y/n; dreamsigns |
| 5. Sleep context | 30 s | Bedtime, wake time, caffeine, alcohol |
| 6. Leave it | 0 | Get up. Do not analyse before coffee |

If you remember nothing, write "no recall" and the date. Logs count blanks too.

**Nights you do not want to write:** skip. A missed day costs nothing.

**Stop rule (distressing dream):** write three lines, put the notebook down, get out of bed, light on, drink water. If it repeats and you feel unsafe, use your safety plan.

---

## 8. Rhythms

- **Daily:** capture.
- **Weekly:** count entries; pick one dreamsign; look at sleep times.
- **Monthly:** a summary page: recall days, nightmares, lucid dreams, the most common setting, character, feeling.
- **Quarterly:** a longer reread for continuity with waking life. Domhoff and Schneider (2008) show that a single person's dream series can be studied for characters, activities and emotions; do a light version of that.
- **Yearly:** archive the notebook. Decide whether to keep it, or keep only a page of highlights.
- **Migration:** move the "dreamsigns" list forward; leave the rest.
- **Index:** a tag page (flying, school, water, chase, people) at the back.

```
+------------------------------------------+
| NIGHTMARE   date ____   distress 0-10 ___|
| Original (short): ______________________ |
| ________________________________________ |
+------------------------------------------+
| CHANGE anything you like:                |
| New version: ____________________________|
```

## 10. Worked examples

All invented sample content.

### 10.1 A day
```
DATE 2026-03-14   Slept 11:30 pm - 6:45 am
TITLE  The bus that did not stop
Recall: 2   Nightmare: no   Lucid: no

I am at the bus stop on my old street. The 33 arrives and does
not open its doors. The driver is my former teacher. People
inside wave but I cannot hear them. I feel slightly embarrassed,
not scared. I wake up before the bus leaves.

Feeling at end: embarrassed, a bit sad
Odd: the bus had no windows on one side
Link: I missed a real bus yesterday.
Caffeine: coffee at 3 pm
```

### 10.2 A week
| Day | Recall | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mon | 0 | "No recall" |
| Tue | 1 | Fragment: staircase |
| Wed | 2 | Bus dream |
| Thu | 3 | Long dream about a house I lived in |
| Fri | 0 | Worked late; short sleep |
| Sat | 2 | Water, a lake, calm |
| Sun | 1 | Fragment |

Summary: recall on 5 of 7 days. Dreamsign: places from my past.

### 10.3 A month
- Recall days: 19 of 31.
- Nightmares: 2 (both after a hard week).
- Lucid: 0 (not practising).
- Most common setting: old house, water.
- Most common feeling: embarrassed, anxious.
- Note: nightmares clustered after 3 nights of short sleep.

### 10.4 A nightmare rescript (IRT-style, sample)
Original: "I am in a dark parking lot and something follows me. I run but my legs are slow. I wake with my heart pounding."
New version: "The parking lot has bright lights. The thing following me is a large dog that turns out to be friendly. I walk to my car and drive home."
Rehearse the new version for 10-20 minutes a day while awake (AASM 2018 describes rehearsing "the rewritten dream scenario for 10-20 minutes per day while awake").

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Who | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| Free dream diary | Common practice | Write, no analysis |
| Dream series analysis | Domhoff and colleagues | Code many dreams from one person for characters, activities, emotions (Domhoff & Schneider 2008) |
| Hall/Van de Castle coding | Hall and Van de Castle | Formal categories with norms (100 men, 100 women) |
| DreamBank searching | Schneider and Domhoff | Keyword search of a public archive, for research |
| MILD (Mnemonic Induction of Lucid Dreams) | LaBerge | Wake, recall a dream, visualise noticing you are dreaming, repeat "next time I'm dreaming, I will remember I'm dreaming" (LaBerge et al. 2018; ILDIS 2020) |
| WBTB (wake back to bed) | Common with MILD | Sleep about 4.5-5 hours, stay awake briefly, return to sleep (LaBerge 2018; ILDIS) |
| Reality testing | Widely taught | Check "am I dreaming?" during the day. In ILDIS, reality testing added no significant benefit to MILD |
| SSILD (senses-initiated lucid dreaming) | Community technique | One of the tested groups in ILDIS (16.9% success) |
| Imagery Rehearsal Therapy | Krakow and colleagues | Rewrite the nightmare and rehearse it awake (AASM) |
| Lucid dreaming treatment for nightmares | Spoormaker & van den Bout; Zadra & Pihl | Use lucidity to change a nightmare; also helped without lucidity |
| Galantamine plus MILD | LaBerge et al. 2018 | A drug; do not self-experiment (section 19) |
| Dream incubation and dream sharing groups | Community | Not evidenced here |

### 11.1 MILD details (from ILDIS and LaBerge 2018)
- Practise after about 5 hours of sleep (ILDIS) or about 4.5 hours (LaBerge).
- Recall a recent dream, visualise being back in it, imagine noticing something unusual that triggers "I'm dreaming."
- Repeat the intention.
- Fall back asleep soon. In ILDIS, lucid dreams occurred more often (18.3% vs 11.1%) when people fell asleep within 10 minutes.

### 11.2 Lucid dreaming and nightmares
Spoormaker & van den Bout (2006, 23 sufferers): both treatment groups had fewer nightmares; sleep quality and PTSD symptoms did not change; lucidity was not needed for nightmare reduction. Gieselmann et al. (2019) note only 6 of 16 patients achieved lucidity in one trial.

## 12. Community practice

I did not survey forums in this session, so treat this as general awareness **[uncertain]**.

- **What people do:** keep a notebook or a phone note; some use voice memos; many use apps with tags.
- **Common customisations:** a "dreamsigns" page, hand-drawn sketches, a moon-phase tracker, colour tags.
- **Camps:** the aesthetic camp draws elaborate spreads; the minimalist camp writes three lines and a date. For research purposes, the minimalist version captures more days.
- **Lucid communities:** share MILD and WBTB routines, alarm schedules and supplements. The evidence (section 16) is more modest than the enthusiasm; supplements carry risks.
- **Warning:** dream-interpretation dictionaries have no support in the studies I read.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Pen.** A pen that writes in the dark: a gel pen or a clip-on light. Pencil works too.
- **Paper.** A small notebook, 5.5 x 8.5 or smaller, dot grid or lines. Unlined if you draw.
- **Light.** A dim clip light or a red-tinted lamp **[uncertain: common advice]**.
- **Voice recorder.** A phone in airplane mode set to record; transcribe later.
- **Alarm.** A gentle, gradual one.
- **Tagging.** Symbols (moon, star, eye) instead of words.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps.** Many dream-journal apps exist. Check the privacy policy; dream logs are intimate.
- **Voice.** Speaking straight after waking keeps you still and keeps the light dim. Transcribe when awake.
- **E-ink (reMarkable, Kindle Scribe, Boox).** Backlit-free e-ink is easy on waking eyes, but a stylus in the dark is slower than a pen.
- **Scanning.** Do not scan private dream pages into shared systems.
- **Gained:** searchable tags, easy counts, backup.
- **Lost:** the physical ritual and privacy.
- **Research note.** DreamBank shows the value of anonymised, searchable archives (Domhoff and Schneider). Your own archive can be searched the same way if typed.

## 16. Evidence and research

### 16.1 Studies

| Study | Sample | Finding | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aspy, Delfabbro, Proeve 2015 (review) | Review | Retrospective single-question estimates give lower dream recall than logbooks. Either retrospective measures underestimate recall, or logbooks enhance it, or both | The review states the question is not settled |
| Robert & Zadra 2014 | 331 adults; 9,796 reports; 253 nightmares and 431 bad dreams | Physical aggression dominated nightmare themes; interpersonal conflict dominated bad dreams. 35% of nightmares and 55% of bad dreams had a primary emotion other than fear. Prospective logs capture typical dreams | Mostly women (275 of 331) |
| Vallat et al. 2022 | 55 French students (28 high recallers over 5 a week; 27 low under 2 a month) | High recallers scored higher on an alternative-uses creativity task (fluency 8.2 vs 7.2) and showed higher default-mode connectivity (0.64 vs 0.56) | Small; students; correlational |
| Siclari et al. 2017 | Serial awakening studies | Up to 70% of NREM awakenings produced dream reports. A posterior "hot zone" tracks dreaming, with or without recall | Lab studies |
| Domhoff & Schneider 2008 | 22,000-plus reports in DreamBank | Search engine and coding reproduce findings from traditional content analysis | Depends on which series are archived |
| Krakow et al. 2001 | 168 women with sexual assault-related PTSD; 3 IRT sessions vs waitlist | Fewer nightmares, better sleep, less PTSD; PTSD dropped by at least 1 level of severity in 65% of treated women vs worse or unchanged in 69% of controls; gains held at 6 months | Waitlist control, not active control |
| Casement & Swanson 2012 | Meta-analysis, 13 studies, N = 511 | Between-group Hedges g: nightmare frequency 0.59, sleep quality 0.64, PTSD 0.67. Effects lasted at 6-12 months. Only 5 of 13 were RCTs; only one had an active control, and its effect was small | Low-quality mix |
| Harb et al. 2019 | 108 combat veterans; IR plus CBT-I vs CBT-I alone | Adding IR did not give greater gains overall; both improved | Suggests context matters (combat trauma, severe baseline) |
| Yücel et al. 2020 | 8 IRT and 7 prazosin trials; N = 1,078 | IRT g = 0.51 on nightmare frequency, 0.31 on PTSD symptoms, 0.51 on sleep quality; no significant difference from prazosin | Small numbers |
| AASM 2018 position paper | Task-force review | IRT is recommended for PTSD-related and non-PTSD nightmares; lucid dreaming therapy and others "may be used"; clonazepam and venlafaxine not recommended for nightmare disorder; prazosin "may be used" | Position paper |
| AASM 2010 best practice guide | Task-force | IRT Level A; notes that prospective logs may overestimate nightmare frequency by boosting recall | Old |
| Gieselmann et al. 2019 | Review | General population prevalence of nightmares 3.5-8.3%; 15.6-66.7% in psychiatric patients; up to 67% of PTSD patients; IRT g = 1.04 on frequency in the review's pooled estimate; low-contact formats (self-help books, internet) can work | Review numbers differ from Casement's |
| Stumbrys et al. 2012 | 35 studies (11 lab, 24 field) | No induction technique verified to induce lucid dreams reliably; methodological quality relatively low | Old |
| LaBerge et al. 2018 | 121 experienced participants; galantamine 0, 4, 8 mg | Lucid dreams on 14% (placebo, with MILD and sleep interruption), 27% (4 mg), 42% (8 mg). Side effects 12% vs 3% (stomach upset, insomnia, fatigue). MILD plus interruption alone was above baseline (14% vs about 4%) | Selected sample of workshop attendees |

| Denis et al. 2018 | 42 studies | Sleep paralysis linked to stress and trauma, sleep problems, substance use, and especially PTSD | Correlational |

### 16.2 What is supported

**Reasonably supported**
- IRT reduces nightmares and is recommended by the AASM (Level A in 2010; recommended in 2018).
- Keeping a log captures more dreams than a single retrospective question, though whether logging boosts real recall is unresolved.
- Dreaming occurs in NREM as well as REM, with up to 70% of NREM awakenings yielding reports (Siclari).
- Lucid dreaming can be induced some of the time with cognitive techniques, at modest rates, and can be verified in the lab.

**Weak, mixed or unsupported**
- Any single technique reliably inducing lucidity (Stumbrys 2012).
- That IRT beats a good insomnia treatment for combat PTSD nightmares (Harb 2019).
- That lucid dreaming improves mental health. Soffer-Dudek calls for caution.
- Dream dictionaries and symbol meanings.
- That dream logging by itself improves mood or wellbeing. I found no study.

### 16.3 Mechanism notes
- **Recall:** a mix of trait (brain connectivity, arousal in sleep) and state (waking soon after a dream). Vallat et al. propose that high recallers have more wakefulness inside sleep, based on their earlier work as summarised in the 2022 paper.
- **IRT:** cognitive restructuring; changing the imagery may reduce its emotional hold. The AASM defines it as creating a new set of positive images and rehearsing them awake.
- **Lucid dreaming:** metacognition in sleep. Brain regions linked to self-reflection are more active during lucid REM (Baird 2019, from a single-subject fMRI case study; EEG results inconsistent).

### 16.4 Limits
- Many lucid dreaming studies are small, self-selected and self-reported.
- Log-based studies may boost recall and so overestimate nightmares (AASM 2010).
- Effect sizes for IRT vary widely across sources: g about 0.5-0.7 in meta-analyses, 1.04 in another summary. Treat "large" cautiously.

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---|---|
| Forgetting within minutes | Write before moving or checking your phone. Even a fragment counts |
| Waking with no recall | Log "no recall." Do not force it |
| Broken sleep from alarms and WBTB | Cap lucid training to a few nights a week and drop it if you feel tired. ILDIS found no drop in sleep quality on successful nights, but Soffer-Dudek cautions about deliberate induction |
| Overanalysing | Separate capture from interpretation |
| Logging nightmares makes them worse | Prospective logs can increase recall (AASM 2010). Use a short line and rescript, do not write pages of gore |
| Believing a dictionary | Prefer your own associations |
| Blurring dream and reality | Soffer-Dudek notes lucid dreamers showed more confabulatory errors. If this happens, stop induction and talk to a clinician |
| Sleep paralysis frightening you | It is linked to stress, trauma and poor sleep (Denis 2018). Improve sleep and seek help if frequent |
| Chasing lucidity | Success rates are modest (about 16%); do not treat failure as a failing |
| Taking supplements or drugs | Galantamine had side effects (12% vs 3%). Do not self-experiment; several sleep aids interact with medications |
| Privacy | Dreams can involve real people or trauma. Store carefully |
| Making it a chore | Two lines is enough |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD.** Keep the notebook and pen physically by the bed, one line only. Voice memo helps. Link: [adhd-journaling.md](adhd-journaling.md).
- **Autism and sensory needs.** Dim, quiet, predictable capture. Use a pen and paper you like. Avoid bright screens. Link: [autistic-sensory-journaling.md](autistic-sensory-journaling.md).
- **Chronic illness and low spoons.** Voice memo and a single-line log. Skip lucid training. Sleep is a limited resource.
- **Depression and anxiety.** Dream logs may bring up distressing content. Keep entries short. If nightmares are frequent, talk to a clinician. Recurrent nightmares increase risk of suicide attempts even after controlling for depression and other factors (Gieselmann 2019), so treat frequent nightmares as a reason to seek care.
- **Trauma.** See below.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia.** Voice recording, or keywords only. Symbols instead of words.
- **Low vision.** Bold pen, high-contrast notebook, large print, or voice.
- **Motor or hand pain.** Voice memo; a large-grip pen.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people.** Dreams about body, name or gender can be affirming or distressing, and hormone therapy can change sleep and dreams **[uncertain: I did not open a source on HRT and dreams]**. Log freely and privately; tag with an icon rather than words if others may see the book. See [gender-euphoria-and-hrt-log.md](gender-euphoria-and-hrt-log.md).
- **Shift workers.** Define "morning" as after your main sleep, whatever the time. Do not add WBTB on top of split sleep. Protect sleep first.
- **Low income.** Free. Any notebook works.

### Trauma considerations
- Trauma-related nightmares are common: up to 67% of people with PTSD experience distressing trauma-related dreams (Gieselmann 2019).
- IRT is recommended, and there are structured programs; if nightmares relate to trauma, work with a trained clinician when possible. Some sources advise starting with a less distressing nightmare **[uncertain: the practice guide page redirected and I could not open it]**.
- Do not force yourself to write in detail about trauma dreams. Three lines and a rescripted ending is enough. For deliberate trauma writing, see [expressive-writing.md](expressive-writing.md), which explains why that needs its own structure and aftercare.
- Lucid dreaming: PTSD sufferers showed "high dream awareness and low control" in one summary (Soffer-Dudek). Induction may not suit people with high dissociation or psychotic symptoms.
- If a dream leaves you shaky: ground yourself (name five things you see), use your safety plan ([safety-planning.md](safety-planning.md)).

### Sleep hygiene cautions
- Sleep comes before dreams. Lucid induction that interrupts sleep works against sleep hygiene (Soffer-Dudek; WBTB requires waking on purpose).
- The strongest general advice I can support from opened sources is limited: AASM's 2010 guide lists sleep hygiene as a component of some nightmare therapies (Exposure, Relaxation and Rescripting Therapy) and Sleep Dynamic Therapy. I did not retrieve a full sleep-hygiene checklist, so I do not list one; use your clinician's or the AASM's own consumer site.
- CBT-I combined with IR gave bigger sleep-quality gains than IR alone in the 2012 meta-analysis (g = 1.09 vs 0.27); in the 2019 veteran RCT, CBT-I alone was enough. Sleep treatment matters for nightmares.
- Alcohol, caffeine and some medications can change dreams. Note them in the log, and talk to a clinician before changing any medication.

## 23. Glossary

- **AASM:** American Academy of Sleep Medicine.
- **Bad dream:** a disturbing dream that does not wake you (Robert & Zadra definition).
- **CBT-I:** cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia.
- **Content analysis:** counting features of dream reports.
- **Dreamsign:** a recurring odd feature that can cue lucidity.
- **DreamBank:** public archive of dream reports.
- **ERRT:** Exposure, Relaxation and Rescripting Therapy.
- **Hall/Van de Castle system:** a coding system for dream content with published norms.
- **IRT:** Imagery Rehearsal Therapy.
- **LDT:** lucid dreaming therapy.
- **Lucid dream:** a dream in which you know you are dreaming.
- **LRLR:** left-right-left-right eye signal used to mark lucidity.
- **MILD:** Mnemonic Induction of Lucid Dreams.
- **Nightmare:** a very disturbing dream that wakes the dreamer (Robert & Zadra definition).
- **Nightmare disorder:** repeated dysphoric, well-remembered dreams causing distress or impairment (DSM-5 as summarised by Gieselmann).
- **NREM / REM:** non-rapid and rapid eye movement sleep.
- **Prospective log vs retrospective estimate:** daily record versus a single recall question.
- **Reality testing:** habit of checking whether you are dreaming.
- **SSILD:** senses-initiated lucid dream technique.
- **WBTB:** wake back to bed.

## 24. FAQ

**1. I never remember dreams. Am I doing it wrong?**
Not necessarily. Recall varies: some people report more than five a week and others fewer than two a month (Vallat 2022). Logging "no recall" is fine. Whether keeping a log raises recall is still debated (Aspy 2015).

**2. Do dreams predict anything?**
I found no evidence for that in the sources I opened. Research finds themes that reflect waking concerns.

**3. Do dream symbols have fixed meanings?**
Not according to the content-analysis approach, which counts characters, emotions and settings instead of decoding.

**4. Do nightmares always mean fear?**
No. In one large log study, 35% of nightmares and 55% of bad dreams had a primary emotion other than fear (Robert & Zadra 2014).

**5. Does Imagery Rehearsal Therapy work?**
It is recommended by the AASM. Meta-analyses show moderate to large effects, but the trials are low in quality and in one veteran RCT adding IR to CBT-I added nothing (Harb 2019).

**6. Can I do IRT myself?**
Low-contact formats (self-help books, internet) have worked in some reviews (Gieselmann 2019). If your nightmares are trauma-related or frequent, use a clinician if you can.

**7. Is lucid dreaming safe?**
For most people, probably, but the evidence is thin. Deliberate induction can disturb sleep and, in vulnerable groups (dissociation, schizotypy, psychosis), may be a problem (Soffer-Dudek 2019). ILDIS found no sleep-quality loss on successful nights.

**8. What is the best lucid dreaming technique?**
None is reliable. In ILDIS, MILD and SSILD groups each had about 16-17% success in week 2 (Adventure-Heart 2020), and a 2012 review said none was verified as reliable (Stumbrys).

**9. Should I take supplements or drugs?**
Galantamine raised lucid dream rates in a study of experienced dreamers but had side effects (12% vs 3%). Talk to a clinician; do not self-experiment.

**10. Do gadgets work?**
Only DreamLight had been validated as of a 2019 review (Mota-Rolim). Most commercial devices had not.

**11. Can dream journaling replace therapy for nightmares?**
No. A log is a measurement tool. IRT is the treatment.

**12. Is dreaming only in REM?**
No. Up to 70% of NREM awakenings produced dream reports in one paradigm (Siclari 2017).

**13. What if I am a shift worker?**
Treat the wake-up after your main sleep as "morning." Protect sleep.
