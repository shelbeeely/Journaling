# Time blocking and The Time-Block Planner

Cal Newport's method of giving every work minute a job, re-planning when the day breaks, and closing work with a shutdown ritual. It also covers generic time blocking and timeboxing.

Sources checked 2026-09-28. Uncertain points are marked **(unsure)**.

## 2. Summary

Time blocking means drawing your working hours as a column and assigning every part of it to a specific task or kind of task, instead of working from a loose list. Cal Newport has used and written about it since at least 2013, when he described a notebook page with two lines per hour and blocks labelled with assignments. The plan is expected to break. When it does, you don't abandon it; you re-block the rest of the day in the next column. At the end of work you do a shutdown ritual: check your lists and calendar, make sure nothing is loose, then say "Schedule shutdown, complete." Newport sells the method as a planner (2020, redesigned 2023) with a daily two-page spread for capture and blocks, weekly planning pages, and a shutdown checkbox. He claims a time-blocked 40-hour week matches 60+ unstructured hours. No study has tested that claim, but the method is built from well-studied parts: specific when-and-where plans (implementation intentions), plan-making that quiets unfinished goals, and mental detachment from work after hours.

## 3. History and origin

### Who created it

Cal Newport is a computer scientist (Georgetown) who has blogged at Study Hacks / calnewport.com since the 2000s. His books include *So Good They Can't Ignore You* (2012), *Deep Work* (2016), *Digital Minimalism* (2019), *A World Without Email* (2021) and *Slow Productivity* (2024). He hosts the *Deep Questions* podcast. He did not invent time blocking; he gave it a name, a set of rules and a product.

Time blocking itself is old. Retailer copy for the planner names Benjamin Franklin and Peter Drucker as earlier users or promoters **(unsure; marketing copy, not checked against their writing)**. In software, "timeboxing" (a fixed time budget for a piece of work, with scope cut to fit) appears in Rapid Application Development and later in Scrum.

### Cultural context

Time blocking rose with the "deep work" and anti-distraction movement of the late 2010s: open-plan offices, Slack, always-on email. Newport frames it as the opposite of reactive work. Around 2018–2023 it spread on YouTube and TikTok, often mixed with timeboxing and the "calendar everything" trend. Critics point out it assumes a worker with schedule autonomy.

### Key quotes

- "A 40 hour time-blocked work week, I estimate, produces the same amount of output as a 60+ hour work week pursued without structure." (2013)
- "Time blockers accomplish roughly twice as much work per week as compared to those who use more reactive methods." (2020 launch post; his estimate, not a measured result)
- "I think of the planner like a gym membership or Peleton [sic] subscription for your time and attention." (2020)
- On the shutdown: "I've basically eliminated stressful work-related thoughts from my evenings and weekends." (2009)
- The planner's promise: give "every minute a job."

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- **A plan beats a list.** A task list doesn't tell you when anything happens. Putting tasks into time forces you to face how much time you have.
- **Every minute gets a job, but the plan can change.** The goal isn't to follow the first plan; it's to always be working from *a* plan. When knocked off course, make a new one for the remaining time.
- **Protect deep work.** Blocks for hard, focused tasks go in first, in the best hours. Shallow work (email, admin) gets batched into its own blocks.
- **Plan at several scales.** Quarterly (or seasonal) goals, a weekly plan, then the daily blocks. Each level feeds the next.
- **Reactive work is still planned.** If your job is responding, block time for responding, and give the block a secondary purpose ("process client requests; if I have downtime, work on project X").
- **End work on purpose.** The shutdown ritual gives your brain permission to stop thinking about work, because everything is captured.
- **Structure helps creativity.** Newport argues that controlled time allows both consistent deep work and the low stress that creativity needs.

**Problem it solves:** the reactive day where you answer messages until 5 p.m. and the important thing never started; and the evening where work worries follow you home.

## 5. Core components

| Element | Kind | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Block column | Page area | Hours down the side; blocks drawn and labelled |
| Re-block columns | Page area | Extra columns to redraw the rest of the day when plans change |
| Capture area (tasks and ideas) | Page area | Collect tasks and notes that come up, to process later |
| Notes beside blocks | Page area | Explanations, sub-steps, "if time left, do X" |
| Shutdown checkbox | Page element | Ticked at the end of the workday |
| Weekly plan page | Page type | Big picture for the week; includes a productivity idea from Newport |
| Task list and calendar | External tools | Feed the plan; the planner doesn't replace them |
| Evening or morning planning | Ritual | Build tomorrow's (or today's) blocks |
| Re-blocking | Ritual | Redraw when the plan breaks |
| Shutdown ritual | Ritual | Review, capture, close, say the phrase |
| Multi-scale planning | System | Quarterly, weekly, daily plans |

### 5.1 Block column

Newport's 2013 notebook used two lines per hour. You draw boxes covering chunks of time and label them: "Chapter 3 draft," "Email + admin," "Lunch," "Meeting prep." A common rule of thumb in write-ups is a 30-minute minimum block to avoid constant switching. Small tasks get batched into one "admin block."

### 5.2 Re-block columns

The planner's time grid has several columns (a reviewer counted four). The first holds the morning plan. When the day breaks (a meeting runs long, a crisis arrives), you cross out the rest of the first column and draw a new plan for the remaining hours in the next column. This is the key design idea: the page expects change.

### 5.3 Capture area

In the planner, the left page of each daily spread is for tasks and ideas that come up (college-ruled, per one reviewer). You don't act on them during a block; you note them and decide later. In the 2013 notebook this role was played by the right-hand column.

### 5.4 Notes beside blocks ("metadata")

Next to a block you can write what exactly to do, links, sub-steps, or conditions ("if call ends early, start slides"). This keeps blocks short and legible.

### 5.5 Shutdown checkbox

A small box on each daily spread. Ticking it marks the ritual as done.

### 5.6 Weekly planning pages

At the start of each week. Include a "big idea" about productivity from Newport. Used to look at the calendar, set the week's priorities, and protect time for big projects. The 2023 edition merges the weekend with the weekly plan into one condensed spread.

### 5.7 Multi-scale planning

- **Quarterly / seasonal:** big goals and projects.
- **Weekly:** check calendar and task list; reserve blocks for major projects; move meetings to "defragment" the week.
- **Daily:** time block plan built from the weekly plan, the calendar's fixed events and the task list.

### 5.8 Shutdown ritual

Newport's 2009 steps:

1. Update master task lists (collect everything from notebooks, inboxes, scraps).
2. Review the whole list; set dates for anything urgent.
3. Review the calendar for the next two weeks.
4. Review the weekly plan and note progress.
5. Close the computer and say "Schedule shutdown, complete."

Afterwards, if a work worry appears, the answer is: I did the shutdown, so it's captured. No need to think about it now.

## 6. Setting it up

### First setup (30 minutes)

1. **Choose paper.** The planner, or any notebook. Draw a vertical line about a third from the left; hours go down the left side, two lines per hour.
2. **Gather your inputs.** One task list (paper or app) and one calendar. Time blocking pulls from both.
3. **Write your quarterly goals** on the inside cover or first page: 2–5 things this season is for.
4. **Pick your planning time.** Evening (Newport's habit) or first thing in the morning.
5. **Pick your shutdown phrase and time.** Newport's is "Schedule shutdown, complete." Use your own words if you like.

### First day

1. Put fixed events in first (meetings, appointments, bus, meals).
2. Put your most important deep task into the best open slot (often morning).
3. Batch small tasks into one or two admin blocks.
4. Leave a buffer block or two (30 minutes) for overruns.
5. Work. When something comes up, write it in the capture area.
6. When the plan breaks, re-block the rest of the day in the next column.
7. Shutdown: update lists, check tomorrow's calendar, tick the box.

### First week

- Days 1–2: expect to re-block two or three times a day. That's normal.
- Day 3: notice which blocks overran; make similar blocks longer tomorrow.
- Day 4: try a weekly plan for next week.
- Day 5: compare first columns to last columns. What kept breaking the plan?
- Weekend: do a proper weekly plan.

## 7. Daily practice

| When | Step | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Evening (or morning) | Look at calendar, weekly plan, task list; draw tomorrow's blocks | 10–20 min |
| Start of day | Re-read the plan; adjust for anything new | 1–2 min |
| During blocks | Work on the block's task only; capture anything else | — |
| When knocked off course | Cross out the rest; re-block in the next column | 1–2 min |
| After meetings | A short processing block to turn notes into tasks | 15–30 min |
| End of work | Shutdown ritual; tick the box; say the phrase | 5 min |

**Order of operations when planning:**
1. Fixed commitments.
2. Deep work in the best energy window.
3. Shallow work batched.
4. Buffers.
5. Conditional blocks ("if X finishes early, do Y").

**Prompts:**
- What must happen today for the week's plan to stay on track?
- What's my deep-work block and when is my brain best?
- What small tasks can I batch into one block?
- Where's my buffer?
- At shutdown: is everything captured? What's tomorrow's first block?

## 8. Rhythms

- **Daily:** plan, re-block, shutdown.
- **Weekly:** weekly plan page. Review last week's pages (how often did you re-block? which blocks overran?), look at the next two weeks of calendar, reserve deep-work blocks, move or decline meetings to leave long gaps.
- **Monthly:** not a formal step in Newport's system **(unsure)**. Many users do a quick check of quarterly goals.
- **Quarterly / seasonal:** set or revise big goals; each planner edition holds 3–4 months, so a new planner often lines up with a new season.
- **Yearly:** some people keep the planners as a record; the second edition's four months means three planners a year.
- **Archiving:** planners are undated, so write the dates. Keep them on a shelf; the re-block columns are a record of how days really went.

## 12. Community practice

- **What people do:** many use only the first column and never re-block on paper; others use a different colour for each re-block. Many keep the weekly plan in an app and the daily blocks on paper.
- **Complaints (Goodreads and blog reviews):** first-edition binding didn't lie flat; the instructions could fit in a blog post; unused capture pages and empty re-block columns feel wasteful; the method fails when your time is controlled by others. One developer ran out of room "within the first two hours of the first day" as production problems arrived. One reviewer with POTS found the planner "out of sight, out of mind" once put away, and imagined a wall-mounted time-blocking notepad instead.
- **Praise:** less decision fatigue, clearer focus, and (for some) relief that re-blocking removes the guilt of a broken plan.
- **Customisations:** colour-coding blocks (deep, shallow, personal); a "buffer" block after every meeting; a block for "shutdown"; post-meeting processing blocks; day themes.
- **Aesthetic versus minimalist:** time blocking is mostly minimalist. Decorative versions exist in bullet-journal circles (colour washes for blocks), but Newport's own planner is plain.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Paper:** any notebook works. Newport used a Black n' Red twin-wire notebook with 24 lb paper (lies flat, takes ink well).
- **Pens:** one pen is enough. A second colour for re-blocks makes changes easy to read.
- **Ruler:** only if you draw your own grid.
- **Timer:** optional; a watch or phone to notice block ends.
- **Calendar and task list:** required inputs; can be paper or digital.
- **Sticky notes:** some people put a movable block on a sticky note so it can be shifted without rewriting.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps:** any calendar (drag tasks into time), dedicated time-blocking apps, timeboxing apps, note apps with a two-column template. Scheduling apps that auto-place tasks are a different idea (the app decides, you don't).
- **E-ink:** reMarkable, Kindle Scribe and Boox handle a daily time-block PDF well; re-blocking by crossing out works like paper. Layers can hold re-blocks.
- **Scanning:** a single daily page scans fine; blocks are hand-drawn so OCR is rough.
- **Lost with digital:** the visible history of re-blocks (apps overwrite the old plan); the calm of a device-free page.
- **Gained:** reminders, shared calendars, drag-to-move, automatic totals.
- **Hybrid (common):** weekly plan in a calendar, daily blocks on paper, shutdown checklist on a card.

## 16. Evidence and research

No controlled study tests time blocking or the planner. Newport's "2× output" and "40 = 60+" figures are his estimates. The method rests on these findings:

| Study | Sample | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38 | 94 independent tests | If-then plans ("when X, I'll do Y") improve goal attainment, d = .65 (medium-to-large). Help with starting, shielding from distraction and disengaging from bad strategies. | Strong meta-analysis. A time block ("at 9:00 I draft chapter 3") is an implementation intention; blocks aren't the tested form but closely related. |
| Masicampo & Baumeister (2011), *J. Personality and Social Psychology* | Several lab studies | Unfinished goals cause intrusive thoughts and worse performance on other tasks. Making a specific plan for the goal removed these effects. | Lab, student samples; replication debated in wider ego-depletion literature (Baumeister) **(unsure how well this paper replicates)**. Supports the shutdown ritual and capture area. |
| Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994), *J. Personality and Social Psychology* 67(3) | Study 1: 37 thesis students; more studies | Predicted 33.9 days, took 55.5. People plan from best-case scenarios. | Classic, replicated. Explains why re-blocking is needed and why buffers help. |
| Leroy (2009), *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 109(2) | Two experiments | Switching away from an unfinished task leaves "attention residue" that hurts the next task. | Lab. Supports longer blocks, batching and finishing before switching. |
| Sonnentag & Kruel (2006), *European Journal of Work and Organizational Psychology* 15(2) | 148 school teachers | Workload, job involvement and recovery self-efficacy predicted psychological detachment from work after hours (rated by self and family). Detachment matters for recovery. | Cross-sectional. Supports the aim of the shutdown ritual, not the ritual itself. |
| Aeon, Faber & Panaccio (2021), *PLOS ONE* | 158 studies, 53,957 people | Time management relates moderately to job performance (r ≈ .25), more to wellbeing, and negatively to distress (r ≈ −.36). | Mostly correlational; not specific to blocking. |
| Halder et al. (2026), arXiv preprint | 957 app users, 24,265 tasks | Over three quarters of time-specific tasks were done after their scheduled time; a third of tasks abandoned. | Preprint, one app. Shows plans slip for nearly everyone; supports re-blocking as normal. |
| Zao-Sanders (2018), *Harvard Business Review* | A survey of 100 productivity hacks | Timeboxing ranked most useful. | Opinion piece; survey method not published in the article. Weak evidence. |

**Claimed versus supported**

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| 2× output / 40 h = 60 h | Claimed (Newport's estimate). Not tested. |
| Specific time plans increase follow-through | Supported (implementation intentions) |
| Capture + plan reduces intrusive work thoughts | Supported in lab (Masicampo & Baumeister) |
| Shutdown ritual reduces evening stress | Claimed; detachment research supports the goal |
| Batching reduces switching costs | Supported in lab (attention residue) |
| Re-blocking keeps plans useful | Plausible; planning-fallacy research says first plans will be optimistic |

## 17. Benefits

- Specific plans raise follow-through. **(evidenced, via implementation intentions)**
- Shows the real size of the day before you commit. **(claimed)**
- Protects time for hard, focused work. **(claimed)**
- Batching shallow work cuts switching costs. **(evidenced in lab, attention residue)**
- Planning an unfinished goal quiets intrusive thoughts. **(evidenced in lab)**
- A clear end to work supports detachment and recovery. **(claimed for the ritual; detachment itself evidenced)**
- Re-block columns turn a broken plan into information, not failure. **(claimed)**
- More output per hour. **(claimed; Newport's estimate)**
- Time-management habits go with better wellbeing and less distress. **(evidenced, correlational)**

## 18. Pitfalls

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Plan breaks by 10 a.m.; you give up | Re-blocking is the method. Draw the next column. Count re-blocks as normal. |
| Every block overruns | Add 25–50% to your estimates (planning fallacy). Add buffer blocks after meetings. |
| Day controlled by others | Block only the edges of the day, or use reactive blocks with a secondary purpose. |
| Too many tiny blocks | 30-minute minimum; batch small tasks into one admin block. |
| Planning takes 30+ minutes | Cap at 10. Fixed events, one deep block, one admin block, buffer. Done. |
| No rest in the plan | Block rest and meals first; they're not leftover time. |
| Shutdown skipped; work thoughts at night | Make the shutdown its own block at a fixed time. Keep a 5-item checklist on a card. |
| Planner feels wasteful (empty columns) | Use a plain notebook or a single page; draw re-block columns only when needed. |
| Rigid, anxious perfectionism | Use pencil. Aim for "working from a plan," not "following the plan." |
| Out of sight, out of mind | Keep the page open on the desk, or pin it to the wall. |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** blocks make time visible, which helps time blindness, but long rigid plans can backfire. Use short blocks with body-doubling or a visible timer, plan "a block of any one of these three tasks," expect lots of re-blocks, and schedule transition buffers. Block hyperfocus time deliberately for interesting work.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** a predictable day helps. Mark transitions and sensory recovery blocks (quiet, dark room). Write change plans in advance ("if the meeting moves, then…").
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** plan by energy, not hours. Block rest first. Keep one "deep" block at most on low days. A spoon cost beside each block helps. Re-blocking to rest counts as success.
- **Depression and anxiety:** a small plan reduces decisions. Avoid a plan that's a wall of failure; plan two blocks and leave the rest open. The shutdown ritual can help anxious evenings; the phrase is a cue to stop checking.
- **Trauma:** control over time can feel safe; rigid monitoring can feel like surveillance. Keep plans private, flexible and kind.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** use symbols or colours for block types; short labels; typed calendar blocks.
- **Low vision:** use a large-print grid (8.5×11), thick lines, one block per line, a thick marker.
- **Motor or hand pain:** use a digital calendar with drag-and-drop, or pre-printed block stickers; re-block by crossing out with one line.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** block HRT, appointments, voice practice or rest after hard social contact as real blocks.
- **Shift workers:** plan only the off-shift hours; use the shift as one fixed block; plan sleep and recovery around rotating shifts; treat the weekly plan as the anchor.
- **Low income:** any notebook works; draw a line and write hours. No planner purchase needed.

## 23. Glossary

- **Batching:** grouping small similar tasks into one block.
- **Block:** a stretch of time assigned to one task or type of task.
- **Buffer:** an empty block to absorb overruns.
- **Capture:** writing down tasks and ideas as they come, to handle later.
- **Conditional block:** a block with a fallback ("if X ends early, do Y").
- **Deep work:** focused, cognitively demanding work without distraction (Newport).
- **Defragment:** move meetings so free time forms long blocks.
- **Implementation intention:** an if-then plan linking a situation or time to an action.
- **Metadata / notes column:** space beside blocks for details.
- **Multi-scale planning:** quarterly, weekly and daily plans that feed each other.
- **Planning fallacy:** the tendency to underestimate how long your own tasks take.
- **Re-blocking:** redrawing the rest of the day's plan in a new column.
- **Shallow work:** logistical, low-focus tasks (email, admin).
- **Shutdown ritual:** end-of-work review with a completion phrase.
- **Timeboxing:** a fixed time budget for a piece of work; scope shrinks to fit.
- **Time blocking:** assigning all working time to blocks in advance.
- **Attention residue:** leftover thinking about a previous task that lowers performance on the next.

## 24. FAQ

1. **Do I have to plan every minute?** Newport says every work minute gets a job, but you can plan only the parts of the day you control.
2. **What if I fall behind by 10 a.m.?** Re-block. That's expected, not a failure.
3. **Evening or morning planning?** Newport plans in the evening. Morning works too; evening means you start without deciding.
4. **How long should blocks be?** At least 30 minutes is a common rule; deep work often 60–120 minutes. Seah's ETP notes focus fading past about 90 minutes.
5. **Do I need the planner?** No. Any notebook works; the planner is a convenience.
6. **Which edition?** The second (2023): spiral, lies flat, four months.
7. **Does it work for shift work?** Plan the hours outside the shift; treat the shift as one fixed block.
8. **What's the difference between time blocking and timeboxing?** Blocking plans the whole day into slots. Timeboxing caps the time for one task and cuts scope to fit. They often overlap.
9. **Is there evidence?** Not for the method directly. Its parts (specific plans, capture, detachment) have research support.
10. **What if the ritual phrase feels silly?** Use any words, or just tick the box. The point is a clear end.

### e. Don't adopt

- **"Every minute gets a job" as a rule.** It can turn into self-surveillance on low-spoon days. Keep blocks optional and allow open time.
- **Newport's quotes or "big idea" essays in the book.** Copyrighted, and busy text breaks the calm design.
- **Productivity claims (2×, 40 = 60).** Unproven; don't print them as promises.
- **Alarms or block reminders on the X4 companion.** Notifications are out of scope.
- **Re-block counts as a score.** Scores drift toward badges and shame.
- **Full-page hourly grids on every day.** Wasteful on shift days; the editor should let people turn them off.

## 26. Open questions

- The exact first-edition daily page layout (pre-printed hours or blank, number of columns, time range) couldn't be confirmed from text sources; reviewers mention four columns in the grid.
- No controlled study of time blocking versus to-do lists exists that we could find.
- Whether Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) has been directly replicated.
- How well time blocking works for rotating shift workers, disabled people or caregivers: no research found.
- The official planner site (timeblockplanner.com) loads as a script-only page and couldn't be read as text on the check date.
