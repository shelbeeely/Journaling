# Stoic journaling

A morning plan and an evening review, built on three Roman writers who kept notes to train how they judged the day. This doc covers the ancient sources, the modern forms, the link to CBT, what the research does and doesn't show, and what Journalwright Studio can take from it.

> Quotes come from public-domain translations: Seneca (Aubrey Stewart 1900; Richard Gummere, Loeb), Marcus Aurelius (Meric Casaubon 1634, as on Project Gutenberg), Epictetus (George Long; Elizabeth Carter). Book and section numbers follow the standard modern numbering unless marked otherwise.

## 1. At a glance

|---|---|

| **Year** | Evening review described in Seneca's *De Ira* (c. 40s–50s CE); *Meditations* written c. 171–175 CE; modern revival from the 1990s (Hadot), boom after 2012 (Stoic Week, Daily Stoic) |

| **Time per day** | 5–15 minutes: 2–5 in the morning, 5–10 at night |
| **Time per week** | 10–20 minutes extra for a weekly look back (modern add-on, not ancient) |
| **Cost** | Free (any notebook). Guided journals about $15–30 |
| **Formats and sizes** | Any. Guided books are hardcover, roughly A5 / 5.5×8.5 |

| **Evidence level** | **Some studies.** Small trials and many uncontrolled pre/post surveys; one small RCT; none test journaling alone. Strong indirect support through CBT, which borrowed Stoic ideas |

## 2. Summary

Stoic journaling is the habit of checking your own judgments in writing, usually twice a day. In the morning you run through what might go wrong and remind yourself what is and isn't up to you. At night you replay the day, like Seneca did after his lamp went out: what did I do badly, what did I do well, what will I do differently. The ancient Stoics didn't call it journaling, and Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* was never meant to be read by anyone else. It was a private notebook of reminders to himself. Modern versions add daily prompts, weekly themes and quotes. The core ideas (you're upset by your view of events more than the events; sort what you control from what you don't; rehearse trouble before it comes) fed straight into Albert Ellis's REBT and Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy. Research on Stoic courses shows modest gains in well-being, mostly from self-selected volunteers without control groups. Research also shows that pop "stoicism" (bottle it up, feel less) is linked to *worse* well-being, so how you frame it matters.

## 3. History and origin

### Who, and their background

- **Zeno of Citium** founded the Stoic school in Athens around 300 BCE. It was named after the *Stoa Poikile*, the painted porch where he taught. The Stoics split philosophy into logic, physics and ethics and thought of them as one whole (SEP, *Stoicism*).
- **Quintus Sextius** (1st century BCE), a Roman philosopher with Stoic and Pythagorean leanings, is the first person we know of who questioned himself every night. We only know this because Seneca tells us.
- **Seneca** (c. 1 BCE – 65 CE), born in Córdoba, exiled to Corsica in 41 CE, then adviser to the young Nero, and forced to kill himself in 65 CE (SEP, *Seneca*). He describes his own evening review in *On Anger* (*De Ira*) 3.36 and returns to self-watching in the *Letters to Lucilius* (c. 62–65 CE).
- **Epictetus** (c. 50–135 CE) was born enslaved in Hierapolis, was freed, studied under Musonius Rufus, and ran a school at Nicopolis in Greece from about 89 CE. He wrote nothing. His student Arrian recorded the *Discourses* and the short *Handbook* (*Encheiridion*) (SEP, *Epictetus*, rev. 2025).
- **Marcus Aurelius** (121–180 CE), emperor from 161, wrote the notebook we call the *Meditations* around 171–175 CE, much of it in army camps on the Danube frontier. Its Greek title is *ta eis heauton*, "to himself". It reads like a private notebook, not a book for readers (IEP, *Marcus Aurelius*).

### Cultural context

For the Stoics, philosophy wasn't a subject. It was training, like a gym for judgment. Writing was one of the drills. Epictetus tells students these ideas are what "philosophers should meditate on, which they should write daily, in which they should exercise themselves" (*Discourses* 1.1), and "Let these thoughts be ready to hand by night and by day; these you should write, these you should read" (*Discourses* 3.24). Marcus wasn't recording events. He was re-writing the same few principles in fresh words so they'd be ready when he needed them. Hadot's big point is that the *Meditations* repeats itself on purpose.

The modern revival sits in a different culture: self-help, productivity and, lately, "stoic" as a macho meme (never show pain). That last one is close to the opposite of what the texts say, and the research below shows it hurts.

### Key quotes from the sources

- Seneca, *De Ira* 3.36 (Stewart): "The spirit ought to be brought up for examination daily. It was the custom of Sextius when the day was over... to inquire of his spirit: 'What bad habit of yours have you cured to-day? what vice have you checked? in what respect are you better?'"
- Same passage: "how sweet is the sleep which follows this self-examination?"
- Same passage: "I pass the whole day in review before myself, and repeat all that I have said and done: I conceal nothing from myself, and omit nothing: for why should I be afraid of any of my shortcomings, when it is in my power to say, 'I pardon you this time'."
- Seneca, *Letters* 83.2 (Gummere): "I shall keep watching myself continually, and—a most useful habit—shall review each day."
- Seneca, *Letters* 91.4: "we should consider, not what is wont to happen, but what can happen."
- Epictetus, *Handbook* 1 (Carter): "Some things are in our control and others not."
- Epictetus, *Handbook* 5: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things."
- Pythagorean verses in Epictetus, *Discourses* 3.10 (Long): "Let sleep not come upon thy languid eyes / Before each daily action thou hast scann'd; / What's done amiss, what done, what left undone."
- Marcus, *Meditations* 2.1 (Casaubon): "Betimes in the morning say to thyself, This day I shall have to do with an idle curious man, with an unthankful man, a railer..."
- Marcus, 4.3: "At what time soever thou wilt, it is in thy power to retire into thyself, and to be at rest."

## 4. Philosophy and principles

**Stated goal.** A calm, steady mind (*ataraxia*, freedom from disturbance) that comes from acting well, not from things going well. The Stoics held that virtue (wisdom, justice, courage, self-control) is the only true good. Health, money and reputation are "preferred indifferents": fine to want, not the source of a good life (SEP, *Stoicism*).

**Core beliefs that shape the writing:**

1. **Judgments cause most distress.** An event arrives as an "impression". You then assent to a judgment about it ("this is a disaster"). The judgment is up to you; the event often isn't. Writing slows the moment of assent down. Epictetus: "Impression, wait for me a little. Let me see what you are" (*Discourses* 2.18, via SEP).
2. **Sort what's up to you.** The dichotomy of control (*Handbook* 1). Your judgments, aims, choices and effort are yours. Other people, the body, the weather and outcomes are not fully yours.
3. **Rehearse before, review after.** Morning: think through what's coming, including what could go wrong. Night: replay and correct. Both are practice, not punishment.
4. **Kind correction, not self-hate.** Seneca's reviewer says "I pardon you this time." The aim is to do better tomorrow.
5. **Zoom out.** Seeing your day from far above (the cosmos, the length of history) shrinks petty upset and reminds you that you're part of a whole.
6. **Emotions aren't banned.** The Stoics wanted to be free of *passions*, which they defined as feelings built on false judgments. They kept "good feelings" (*eupatheiai*: joy, caution, wishing well). "Stoic" meaning "unfeeling" is a later misreading.

**The problem it solves.** Reactivity and rumination: getting knocked sideways by events, replaying them for hours, blaming yourself or others with no path forward. The evening review gives the replay a shape and an ending, so you can sleep.

**Hadot's three disciplines** (from Epictetus's three *topoi*), a useful frame for prompts:

| Discipline | About | Journal question |
|---|---|---|
| Assent (perception) | Judging impressions accurately | "What story did I tell myself about that? Is it true?" |
| Desire (acceptance) | Wanting what's up to you; accepting the rest | "What am I fighting that isn't mine to change?" |
| Action (impulse) | Acting fairly for others | "Did I act for the common good today? Who did I treat badly?" |

## 5. Core components

| Element | Ancient source | When | Minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Morning preparation (*praemeditatio*) | Marcus 2.1; Seneca *Letters* 91 | Morning | 2–5 |
| Premeditation of adversity (*premeditatio malorum*) | Seneca *Letters* 91; Epictetus *Handbook* 21 | Morning or weekly | 3–10 |
| Dichotomy of control sort | Epictetus *Handbook* 1 | Any time a worry hits | 2–5 |
| Impression check ("wait for me a little") | Epictetus *Discourses* 2.18 | In the moment, logged later | 1–3 |
| Evening review | Seneca *De Ira* 3.36; Pythagorean verses | Bedtime | 5–10 |
| View from above | Marcus 7.48, 9.30, 12.24 | Weekly or when stuck | 5 |
| Maxims (copying principles) | Epictetus *Disc.* 1.1, 3.24; Marcus throughout | Daily | 1–2 |
| Retreat into yourself | Marcus 4.3 | Any time | 1–5 |
| Memento mori | Epictetus *Handbook* 21; Marcus throughout | Weekly or monthly | 2–5 |
| Contemplating the sage / role model | Seneca *Letters* 11 (choose a Cato to watch you) | Evening | 2 |
| Weekly/monthly theme (modern) | *The Daily Stoic Journal* | Weekly | 5 |

### Morning preparation

Marcus's 2.1 is the model: he tells himself he'll meet rude, ungrateful and dishonest people today, and then reminds himself why they can't really harm him and why he won't hate them. It's a short, honest forecast plus a chosen stance. Modern form: "What's coming today? What's hard? How do I want to meet it?"

### Premeditation of adversity (*premeditatio malorum*)

Seneca: "Our minds should be sent forward in advance to meet all problems" (*Letters* 91.4). You picture a setback (bus doesn't come, a shift runs long, a hard phone call) and plan your response. Robertson maps this to imaginal exposure and stress inoculation in CBT. It's not doom-scrolling your own future. Two rules keep it safe: keep it specific and likely, and always end with a plan or a "what's still mine".

### Dichotomy of control sort

Split a worry into two columns: **mine** and **not mine**. Modern writers (for example William Irvine) add a middle "partly mine" column, where you control your effort but not the outcome. Put your effort there and let go of the result.

### Impression check

Catch the first thought ("she's ignoring me") and name it as an impression, not a fact. Epictetus's "wait for me a little" is a pause, the same move CBT calls catching an automatic thought. In a journal it becomes a two-line log: *impression → a truer version*.

### Evening review

Seneca's three questions, via Sextius: what bad habit did I cure, what fault did I resist, in what way am I better? The Pythagorean version: what did I do wrong, what did I do, what did I leave undone? Then blame what was wrong and enjoy what was right. The review ends with a verdict and forgiveness, and then sleep.

### View from above

Marcus repeatedly looks at life "from on high as it were, looking down" (12.24, Casaubon; also 7.48, 9.30): crowds, armies, farms, weddings, deaths, all small and brief. On the page: describe your day as if seen from a plane, then from space, then from 100 years later. Hadot calls this the "point of view of the cosmos".

### Maxims

Copy one short principle and write it again in your own words. This is closest to what Marcus actually did. It's the Stoic cousin of the [commonplace book](./commonplace-book.md).

### Retreat into yourself

Marcus 4.3: people seek country houses and beaches to get away, but "it is in thy power to retire into thyself". A one-minute written pause: three slow breaths, one line on what's steady right now.

### Memento mori

"Let death and exile... be daily before your eyes" (*Handbook* 21). Used to cut through small worries and to value today. **Caution:** skip this for anyone with suicidal thoughts or fresh grief (see section 19).

### The sage as witness

Seneca advises picking a person you respect and imagining them watching you (*Letters* 11). In the review: "What would ___ say about how I handled that?" A kind mentor works better than a harsh judge.

## 6. Setting it up

**First setup (15 minutes):**

1. Pick a small notebook or a page layout with a morning box and an evening box. A day page works.
2. Write the three evening questions on a card or the inside cover:
   - What did I do well?
   - Where did I fall short?
   - What will I do differently tomorrow?
3. Write the control split on the same card: **mine / not mine**.
4. Choose one sentence to start with. Suggested: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the notions they form about things." Say it in your own words.
5. Decide on a trigger: morning = with coffee or meds; evening = when you plug in your phone or put the light out.
6. Decide in advance what you'll do on a bad-spoon day: one line only ("today was hard; tomorrow: rest").

**First day:**

- Morning (3 min): "Today I'll probably deal with ___. It might get hard when ___. I want to meet it by ___."
- Evening (5 min): the three questions. One or two lines each. End with one kind line to yourself.

**First week:**

- Days 1–3: just the morning line and the three evening questions.
- Day 4: add a control sort for one worry.
- Day 5: add one premeditation ("If the bus is late, I will...").
- Day 7: read the week back. Circle one pattern. Write one line about it. That's your first weekly review.

## 7. Daily practice

**Morning (2–5 minutes), in this order:**

1. **Read** one principle or quote (30 s).
2. **Forecast** the day: who and what you'll meet (1 min).
3. **Premeditate** one likely snag and your response (1 min).
4. **Choose** one intention, phrased as something that's up to you: "stay patient on the phone", not "get the refund" (30 s).

**During the day (optional, 30 s each):**

- When something stings, write the impression and a truer version in the margin, or just tick a box.

**Evening (5–10 minutes), in this order:**

1. **Replay** the day in order, morning to night, like Seneca. Don't write it all; just walk through it (2 min).
2. **Went well:** one or two things you did well by your own values.
3. **Fell short:** one or two things. Facts, not insults.
4. **Tomorrow:** one concrete change.
5. **Control check:** anything still bothering you? Split it into mine / not mine.
6. **Pardon:** one line that closes the case ("I forgive myself for ___").
7. **Lights out.** Seneca's point: sleep comes easier after the review.

**Prompts by time:**

| Time | Prompt |
|---|---|
| Morning | What's the hardest thing likely today, and what's my part in it? |
| Morning | Who will I meet who might test me, and how do I want to treat them? |
| Morning | What can I let go of before it starts? |
| Evening | What did I do well? |
| Evening | Where did I fall short, and what would a kind coach say? |
| Evening | What am I still carrying that isn't mine to carry? |
| Evening | What will I do differently tomorrow? |

## 8. Rhythms

The ancients describe daily practice. Longer rhythms are modern add-ons, but they fit.

**Weekly (10–15 min):**
- Read the week's evening reviews.
- Count repeats: what fell short more than once?
- One **view from above** entry on the week.
- Pick next week's principle or theme.

**Monthly (20 min):**
- A **premeditation** page for the month ahead: known hard dates, likely snags, a plan for each.
- A **control audit**: list the month's biggest worries; mark mine / partly / not mine.
- Pick one virtue to practise (patience, honesty, courage, fairness).

**Quarterly (30 min):**
- Reread three months of reviews. What changed? What didn't?
- Revise your personal "maxims" list (5–10 lines you want ready at hand).

**Yearly:**
- A **memento mori** letter, only if it's safe for you: "If this were my last year, what would I stop, start, keep?"
- Archive: keep the maxims list, the monthly audits and anything you'd want to reread. Most daily entries don't need keeping.
- Index: a single back page listing where the good weekly reviews are.

### Sample day (Wednesday, outdoor shift worker)

```
MORNING
Principle: I'm upset by my take on things, not the things.
Today I'll meet: supervisor, rain, the 6:40 bus, a noisy crew.
Likely snag -> response
  Bus late        -> leave at 6:25, text lead if late, no panic
  Rude comment    -> "that's their day, not mine", walk away
Intention (mine): take my break on time.

MINE                        | NOT MINE
when I leave                | the bus schedule
how I answer                | what he says
eating lunch                | the rain

EVENING
(+) Did well: left early, took full break, ate lunch.
(-) Fell short: snapped at J. when he moved my tools.
(>) Tomorrow: say "please leave my kit" before I'm angry.
Still carrying: the comment about my voice. Mine = how long I
  replay it. Not mine = what he thinks. Setting it down.

```

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Who | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| Seneca's evening review | Seneca, after Sextius | Night only; courtroom metaphor; ends with pardon |
| Pythagorean review | Golden Verses, quoted by Epictetus | Three questions: done wrong, done, left undone |
| Marcus-style notebook | Marcus Aurelius | No template; you rewrite principles and talk to yourself in the second person |
| Epictetan maxims | Epictetus via Arrian | Short rules copied and reread; the *Handbook* itself is this |
| *The Daily Stoic Journal* | Ryan Holiday, Stephen Hanselman (2017) | Dated, one prompt per day with morning and evening space; weekly themes with an intro essay; organised by the three disciplines (perception, action, will) |
| Stoic Week handbook | Modern Stoicism (since 2012) | A free one-week course with morning and evening meditations (morning preview based on *Meditations* 2.1) and short reflections; paired with a questionnaire |
| SeRenE | Brown et al. (2022) | 12-day online course for medical students: negative visualisation, Stoic mindfulness, empathy exercises, evening journaling |
| Stoic training (research) | MacLellan & Derakshan (2021) | 8 sessions for high worriers; tested for rumination and self-efficacy |
| Robertson's Stoic CBT exercises | Donald Robertson (2010 on) | Maps each Stoic technique to a CBT one; adds "postponement" and "contrasting consequences" |
| Stoic "control journal" | Common online | Just the two (or three) column sort each day |
| Religious crossovers | Christian *examen* (Ignatius of Loyola, 1500s) | A structurally similar evening review of the day with gratitude and prayer; not Stoic in origin, but readers often combine them |

## 12. Community practice

What people actually do (from forum threads, Stoic Week reports and the Daily Stoic community; anecdotal):

- **The evening review is the core.** Most people keep only the three questions. Morning pages tend to drop off first.
- **Quote-a-day.** Many start with a daily quote book (*The Daily Stoic*) and write two lines under it.
- **Control columns** are the most copied layout, often drawn as a circle inside a circle.
- **Minimalist camp:** a pocket notebook, three questions, no decoration. Many say this is the "authentic" way, since Marcus had no template.
- **Structured camp:** dated guided journals, apps with prompts, streak counters.
- **Aesthetic camp:** Greek or Latin headings, marble textures, busts. Low-value for practice; fine if it helps.
- **The "bro Stoicism" problem.** Some online spaces use "stoic" to mean "don't show emotion" or "grind". Philosophers and therapists push back on this; the Karl et al. (2022) data backs them.
- **Common customisations:** adding gratitude, adding a sleep or mood line, merging with bullet journal daily logs, a weekly "view from above" in place of a formal review.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Pen:** anything that doesn't smear. Night writing is often in bed, so a pencil or a fast-drying gel pen helps.
- **Paper:** 80–100 gsm is fine; there's little ink. Lined or dot grid.
- **Notebook size:** pocket (A6) for the minimalist form; A5 or 5.5×8.5 for a day page with morning and evening boxes.
- **A card** with the three questions and the control split, as a bookmark.
- **No stickers or stamps needed.** A small sun and moon mark is enough to split the page.
- **Book light** for the evening review, warm and dim, so it doesn't wake you up.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps:** Daily Stoic has an app with prompts; general journal apps (Day One, others) have Stoic templates. Many habit apps offer "evening review" reminders. Prices and features change; check before recommending.
- **Templates:** printable morning/evening PDFs are common; the two-column control sort converts well to any grid.
- **E-ink:** reMarkable, Kindle Scribe and Boox all work for a template PDF with a morning box and an evening box. The low glare suits bedtime.
- **Scanning:** the short answers (did well / fell short / tomorrow) are easy to capture. The free writing isn't needed digitally.
- **Lost vs paper:** phones at bedtime bring light, alerts and scrolling, which undercut Seneca's "sweet sleep". Typing is faster but you remember less of it (not firmly established for this practice; plausible from note-taking research).
- **Gained:** search ("how many times did I write *snapped*?"), streak counts, easy quote lookup.

## 16. Evidence and research

No study has tested Stoic *journaling* alone. The research tests Stoic *courses* that include morning and evening reflection, plus surveys of Stoic attitudes.

| Study | Sample | Design | Finding | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Stoic Week reports**, LeBon et al. (yearly since 2012; 2024 report) | 2024: 4,753 signed up, 1,636 did the pre-survey, 354 matched pre/post | Pre/post, **no control group** | 2024: life satisfaction +12.7%, positive emotions +12.4%, negative emotions −18.5%, depression vulnerability −13.3%, anxiety vulnerability −12.5% | Self-selected Stoicism fans; ~78% dropout from pre to matched post; no control; the team is also the course provider; self-report |
| **MacLellan & Derakshan (2021)**, *Cognitive Therapy and Research* 45:730–744 | 45 high worriers | Randomised, 3 arms (active control, Stoic + working memory training, Stoic only), 8 sessions | Stoic training improved rumination and self-efficacy; less anxious language in written tasks | Small; short follow-up; not journaling alone |
| **Brown et al. (2022)**, *BMC Medical Education* (SeRenE) | 24 third-year medical students | Mixed-methods pilot, pre/post/2 months, 12 days × 15–20 min, includes evening journaling | Empathy and resilience scores rose; gains correlated (r = 0.48) | No control; volunteers; small; COVID changed clinical exposure |
| **Karl et al. (2022)**, *Journal of Happiness Studies* 23:3531–3547 | New Zealand 636, Norway 290, US 381 | Pre-registered cross-sectional | "Stoic ideology" (lower-case: not expressing or feeling emotion, enduring) was linked to **lower** hedonic and eudaimonic well-being, most for Taciturnity and Serenity | Measures pop stoicism, not the practice; correlational |
| **CBT evidence** (Beck, Ellis and after) | Very large | Many RCTs of CBT | CBT works well for depression and anxiety | CBT isn't Stoicism; this is support by descent, not a test of the journal |
| **Adjacent: expressive writing** (see [expressive writing](./expressive-writing.md)) | Many | Meta-analysis (Frattaroli 2006) | Small average benefit from writing about feelings | Different practice: feeling-focused, not judgment-focused |

**How strong is it?** Weak to moderate. The ideas behind the journal are the same ones CBT is built on, and CBT is well evidenced. But the Stoic journal specifically has only small or uncontrolled studies. The strongest caution is Karl et al.: if someone takes "Stoic" to mean "don't show or feel emotion", it's linked to worse outcomes.

**Claimed vs supported:**

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Better well-being after a week of practice | Supported weakly (uncontrolled pre/post) |
| Less rumination | Some support (one small RCT) |
| More resilience and empathy | Weak support (one pilot) |
| Better sleep after an evening review | Claimed (Seneca); not directly tested for this practice |
| Less anger | Claimed; Stoic Week surveys link higher Stoic scores with lower anger (correlational); untested in trials |
| Makes you stoic, as in unfeeling | Not the aim; the unfeeling version is linked to harm |

## 17. Benefits

- A clear end to the day's replay, so rumination has a stopping point. **Claimed** (some support for rumination: MacLellan & Derakshan 2021).
- Better life satisfaction and mood over a week of practice. **Evidenced, weakly** (Stoic Week pre/post).
- Less rumination, more self-efficacy. **Evidenced, small RCT**.
- Resilience and empathy. **Evidenced, pilot only** (Brown et al. 2022).
- Calmer response to known stressors through rehearsal. **Claimed**, backed indirectly by CBT exposure and stress-inoculation research.
- Better sleep. **Claimed** (Seneca). Untested here.
- A habit of kinder self-correction. **Claimed**.
- Short: fits a low-spoon day. **Claimed**, practical.
- Free, needs no product. **Fact**.

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Review turns into self-attack | "Judge" framing, perfectionism | Always end with Seneca's pardon line; use "fell short", not "failed"; pair each minus with a plus |
| Using it to suppress feelings | Pop "stoic" meaning | Name the feeling first, then check the judgment. Feelings are data, not faults |
| Premeditation spirals into worry | Anxiety likes rehearsal | One snag, one plan, then stop. Time-box to 2 minutes. Skip on high-anxiety days |
| Memento mori feels dark or unsafe | Grief, depression, suicidal thoughts | Skip it. Use "what matters this week?" instead |
| Too many prompts | Guided books ask a new question daily | Keep the three evening questions; ignore the rest on bad days |
| Morning never happens | Early shifts, rushing | Do the morning plan the night before, after the review |
| Feels preachy or macho | Online culture | Read the primary texts; they're warmer and humbler than the memes |
| Nothing changes | Reviews without an action | Force one concrete "tomorrow" line; check it the next night |
| Control sort feels dismissive of injustice | "Not mine" can sound like "don't care" | Add "partly mine": what small, fair action can I take? The Stoics valued justice |
| Skipped days → quit | All-or-nothing | A one-word review counts. Mark missed days with a dot, not guilt |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** keep it to three tick-and-word lines. Use icons (+, −, →). Put the card in the same spot as meds. Allow the review at any time, not just bedtime.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** the fixed structure suits many. Add a sensory line ("what was loud today?"). Avoid vague prompts; use concrete ones ("one thing I did well at work").
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** body limits go in "not mine". Don't judge rest as a failure. A one-line version: "Today: ___. Kind to myself by ___."
- **Depression:** the "fell short" line can feed self-blame. Swap it for "what was hard?" and add a small good thing. Skip memento mori.
- **Anxiety:** premeditation can backfire. Use it only for real, near events and always end with a plan. The control sort is often the most useful part.
- **Trauma:** don't replay traumatic moments in detail at bedtime. Review actions, not memories. Use "view from above" gently or not at all if dissociation is an issue. Seek a trauma-informed therapist for trauma work.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** tick boxes and single words; voice notes; the page can be mostly icons.
- **Low vision:** large print card, high contrast, 14pt+ prompts, thick lines.
- **Motor or hand pain:** fewer lines; circle words instead of writing; dictation.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** misgendering and dysphoria are often in "not mine" (others' words) and "partly mine" (how I respond, who I tell, what care I seek). Don't let "accept what you can't change" slide into accepting mistreatment. The Stoics treated justice as a virtue; standing up for yourself counts as acting well.
- **Shift workers:** "morning" and "evening" mean start and end of *your* day, not the clock. Do the review after the shift, before sleep, whenever that is.
- **Low income:** no cost; any paper works; the public-domain texts are free online and in libraries.

## 21. Combining

- **With the [CBT thought record](./cbt-thought-record.md):** use the Stoic day frame, and switch to a full thought record when one thing hits hard. Same idea, more steps.
- **With a [DBT diary card](./dbt-diary-card.md):** the card tracks urges and skills; the Stoic review adds meaning. Keep the card for data, the review for words.
- **With [expressive writing](./expressive-writing.md):** feel first (expressive), judge later (Stoic). Don't do both at once at bedtime.
- **With the [commonplace book](./commonplace-book.md):** keep your Stoic maxims there.
- **With the [Progoff Intensive Journal](./progoff-intensive-journal.md):** Progoff's Daily Log records without judging; the Stoic review judges. Use Progoff for the long view of your life, Stoic for the day.
- **With a bullet journal daily log:** bullets for the day's facts; three Stoic lines at the bottom.
- **With the [dream journal](./dream-journal.md):** Stoic at night, dreams in the morning. They share a bedside notebook well.
- **With Journalwright Studio's existing blocks:** "Went well / Was hard / Tomorrow" is already a Stoic evening review in all but name.

## 24. FAQ

**1. Do I have to believe in Stoic philosophy?**
No. The three evening questions and the control sort work on their own. The philosophy explains why.

**2. Isn't "Stoic" about not having feelings?**
No. Classical Stoics wanted to drop feelings based on false judgments, not all feeling. The "don't feel, don't show" version is linked to lower well-being (Karl et al. 2022).

**3. Morning or evening, if I can only do one?**
Evening. It's the best-documented ancient practice (Seneca, Sextius, the Pythagoreans) and it closes the day.

**4. How long should it take?**
Five minutes is plenty. Seneca's review is a mental walk-through; you only write the verdicts.

**5. What if my review is all negatives?**
Start with "did well" every time, even if it's "I got through it". End with a pardon line. If it stays all negative for two weeks, that's a mood sign worth talking to someone about.

**6. Is it the same as CBT?**
Close relatives. Ellis and Beck both named Stoicism as a root. CBT adds structure, a therapist and a big evidence base. Stoic journaling is a daily habit, not treatment.

**7. Is premeditation just worrying?**
It's worry with a stop and a plan. Keep it to one likely snag and your response. If it raises anxiety, drop it.

**8. Which translation should I read?**
Free: Long or Casaubon (Marcus), Long or Carter (Epictetus), Stewart or Gummere (Seneca). Easier modern ones: Robin Hard, Gregory Hays (Marcus), Robin Waterfield. Modern ones cost money but read more easily.

**9. What about Marcus's own format?**
No format. He wrote short notes to himself, often "you" rather than "I". Try writing your review in the second person; many find it kinder.

**10. Can I use it for anger?**
That's what Seneca wrote it for. *De Ira* is literally "On Anger". Log what set you off, the judgment under it, and the pause you could take next time.

### e. Don't adopt

- **Memento mori pages or daily death reminders.** Risky for readers with suicidal thoughts or fresh grief; Journalwright Studio books already carry a safety plan page. Offer "What matters this week?" instead.
- **"Fell short" as a mandatory prompt.** Keep "Was hard" wording from the existing `review` block; it's kinder and avoids self-blame.
- **Marble busts, Greek columns, Latin mottos.** Busy ornament against the calm design rules.
- **Streaks or "days in a row".** Badge-like; bad for low-energy days and against the companion's firmware scope.
- **Pop "stoic" language** (tough it out, don't complain). Linked to worse well-being.
- **Long daily quotes.** Take space and add reading load. One short line, occasionally, at most.

## 26. Open questions

- Does the evening review, on its own, improve sleep? No study isolates it.
- Does written review beat mental review? Seneca's was mostly mental. Untested.
- Stoic Week results are uncontrolled and heavily filtered by dropout. Would a controlled trial show the same gains?
- How often does the review turn into self-criticism for people with depression? No data.
- Which component carries the effect: control sort, premeditation, or review? Unknown.
- Exact date of *De Ira* (40s vs early 50s CE) and of Arrian's records are debated; this doc gives ranges.
- *The Daily Stoic Journal*'s internal month themes weren't checked against the book for this doc (only the publisher and archive records were); treat any list of them as unverified.
