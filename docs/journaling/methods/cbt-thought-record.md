# CBT thought record (and behavioural activation logs)

> **Not medical advice.** This is a research summary to help design journal pages. It is not therapy and does not
> replace a clinician. Thought records are a core CBT tool; they work best learned with a therapist or a structured
> self-help programme.

> Canada, weekdays 10 am–6 pm Pacific. Outside the US and Canada, check your local services. A thought record is not the right tool for a crisis; a safety plan is (see

## 2. Summary

A thought record is a worksheet for catching a thought that made you feel worse, and checking it. You write down the
situation, the mood and how strong it was (0–100%), the automatic thought that went with it, the evidence for and
against that thought, a more balanced alternative, and then how strong the mood is now. Aaron Beck's version (1979) had
five columns after the date; *Mind Over Mood* split the evidence into two columns to make seven. The point isn't
positive thinking: it's accurate thinking, and noticing that a feeling is not a fact. CBT pairs thought records with
behavioural work. In **behavioural activation** you log what you do hour by hour, rate each activity for mastery and
pleasure, then schedule more of what helps, which on its own works about as well as full CBT for depression. Together
they give a journal two complementary pages: one for thinking about a hard moment, and one for doing things even before
the mood lifts.

## 3. History and origin

### Who created it

**Aaron T. Beck** (born 18 July 1921, Providence, Rhode Island; died 1 November 2021, aged 100) was a psychiatrist at the
University of Pennsylvania for nearly 70 years. Trained in psychoanalysis, he set out in the late 1950s to test
psychoanalytic ideas about depression and instead found that depressed patients had quick, negative, often unnoticed
thoughts about themselves, the world and the future. He called these **automatic thoughts**, and built cognitive
therapy around identifying and testing them. He developed the Beck Depression Inventory (1961) and co-founded the Beck
Institute with his daughter, Judith S. Beck, in 1994. The *American Psychologist* named him one of the five most
influential psychotherapists of all time (as quoted by the Beck Institute).

**Albert Ellis** developed Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) in the mid-1950s, with an "ABC" model (Activating
event → Beliefs → Consequences), a parallel line that fed the ABC thought diaries still used today **(dates from general
knowledge; not re-checked for this doc)**.

**David D. Burns**, a psychiatrist who trained with Beck, wrote *Feeling Good: The New Mood Therapy* (1980), which
popularised a list of ten cognitive distortions and the "triple-column technique" and the "Daily Mood Log". His later
model is TEAM-CBT; *Feeling Great* came out on 15 September 2020.

**Dennis Greenberger and Christine A. Padesky** wrote *Mind Over Mood* (1995; 2nd ed. 2015), a self-help workbook built
around the 7-column thought record. Guilford reports more than 1.3 million copies in print; the British Association for
Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapies called it "the most influential cognitive-behavioral therapy publication".

**Behavioural activation** comes from a separate, behavioural line: Peter Lewinsohn's 1970s work on loss of rewarding
activity in depression, activity scheduling in Beck's 1979 manual, and then Neil Jacobson's 1996 component study, which
found the activation part of CBT alone worked as well as the full package. Christopher Martell, Michael Addis and
Jacobson wrote it up as a standalone therapy (*Depression in Context*, 2001), and Carl Lejuez and colleagues made a brief
version (BATD) the same year **(both 2001 dates from general knowledge)**.

### Cultural context

CBT arose as a practical, testable alternative to long psychoanalysis. Its brief, structured, homework-based format
suited research trials and later public health services (for example NHS Talking Therapies), which is why it has more
trial evidence than most therapies. Self-help books turned the thought record into something people do at kitchen
tables, not only in clinics.

### Key quotes

- Beck Institute on the model: CBT rests on the idea that "people's thoughts and perceptions influence the way they feel
  and behave."
- Beck: "The stronger person is not the one making the most noise but the one who can quietly direct the conversation
  toward defining and solving problems." (quoted by the Beck Institute)
- *Mind Over Mood*'s subtitle states the idea plainly: "Change how you feel by changing the way you think."

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- **The cognitive model.** A situation doesn't make you feel a certain way by itself; your interpretation of it does.
  Situation → automatic thought → emotion, body sensations and behaviour.
- **Levels of thinking.** Automatic thoughts sit on top of intermediate beliefs (rules and assumptions, often "if…then"
  or "should") and core beliefs (deep beliefs about self, others and the world, such as "I'm unlovable") (Judith Beck's
  formulation, widely taught **(from general knowledge)**).
- **The cognitive triad** in depression: negative views of self, world and future (Beck, 1979).
- **Collaborative empiricism.** Therapist and client treat thoughts as hypotheses to test, together, with evidence and
  experiments. Not "you're wrong", but "let's find out".
- **Accuracy, not positivity.** A balanced thought includes the true bad parts.
- **Behaviour changes feelings too.** Doing things (activation, experiments) gives the brain new evidence faster than
  arguing does.
- **Homework matters.** Practice between sessions is expected; more and better homework predicts better outcomes
  (Kazantzis et al., 2016).
- **Problem it solves:** feelings that seem to come from nowhere and spiral; depression's pull toward withdrawal and
  inactivity; not knowing what to do with a hard thought except believe it or push it away.

## 5. Core components

| Element | What it is | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | Who, what, when, where; the facts a camera would see | Column 1 |
| Moods | One word each, rated 0–100% | Column 2 |
| Automatic thoughts (and images) | What went through your mind; circle the "hot thought" | Column 3 |
| Evidence for the hot thought | Facts that support it | Column 4 (7-column) |
| Evidence against | Facts that don't fit it | Column 5 (7-column) |
| Alternative / balanced thought | A statement that fits all the evidence; rate belief 0–100% | Column 6 (or 4 in the 5-column) |
| Re-rate moods | Same moods, rated now; any new moods | Column 7 (or 5) |
| Belief ratings | How much you believe a thought, 0–100% | Columns 3 and 6 |
| Cognitive distortions | Named thinking patterns to spot | Checklist beside column 3 |
| Triple-column technique | Thought → distortion → rational response (Burns) | Short form |
| ABC / ABCDE diary | Activating event, Belief, Consequence (+ Dispute, Effective new belief) | Short form (Ellis/CCI) |
| Activity monitoring | Hour-by-hour log of what you did, with mastery and pleasure ratings | Weekly grid |
| Activity scheduling | Planning specific activities into specific times | Weekly grid |
| Values / life areas | What matters, used to choose activities (BATD) | Separate list |
| Behavioural experiment | Testing a prediction by doing something | Separate form |
| Homework review | Going over the week's records in session | Ritual |

### The 5-column record (Beck, 1979)

Beck's "Daily Record of Dysfunctional Thoughts" (DRDT) had a date column and then five: **Situation**, **Emotion(s)**
(name, rate 1–100), **Automatic thought(s)** (rate belief 0–100%), **Rational response** (rate belief 0–100%) and
**Outcome** (re-rate belief in the automatic thought, and the emotions). Later writers renamed "dysfunctional" and
"rational response" to softer words like "unhelpful" and "balanced" **(column wording from general knowledge of the 1979
manual; not re-checked against the book)**.

### The 7-column record (*Mind Over Mood*)

1. **Situation**: who, what, when, where.
2. **Moods**: describe each in one word; rate intensity 0–100%.
3. **Automatic thoughts (images)**: answer "What was going through my mind just before I started to feel this way?";
   circle the **hot thought** (the one most linked to the strongest mood).
4. **Evidence that supports the hot thought.**
5. **Evidence that does not support the hot thought.**
6. **Alternative / balanced thoughts**: rate how much you believe each, 0–100%.
7. **Rate moods now**: re-rate column 2's moods and any new ones 0–100%.

The book teaches the columns one or two at a time over several chapters before putting them together; the author site
keeps a dedicated notes page on the 7-column record **(page existed in the site menu; its URL returned "not found" when
checked)**.

### Evidence questions (commonly taught)

- What would I tell a friend who had this thought?
- Have I had experiences that show this isn't completely true?
- When I'm not feeling this way, how do I think about this?
- Am I ignoring strengths or anything that went okay?
- Am I jumping to conclusions without facts?
- Five years from now, how will I see this?
- What's the most likely outcome, not just the worst?

### Cognitive distortions

Beck's 1963 paper named five: arbitrary inference, selective abstraction, overgeneralisation, magnification and
minimisation, and inexact labelling; personalisation and dichotomous thinking were added by 1979 (Psychology Tools
summary). Burns (1980) gave the ten plain-language names most people know:

| # | Distortion | Plain meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | All-or-nothing thinking | Only two boxes: perfect or failure | "I burned dinner; I can't do anything right." |
| 2 | Overgeneralisation | One event becomes "always" or "never" | "I always get the late bus." |
| 3 | Mental filter | Dwelling on one negative detail | Rereading the one critical comment |
| 4 | Disqualifying the positive | Good things "don't count" | "They only said that to be nice." |
| 5 | Jumping to conclusions (mind reading, fortune telling) | Assuming what others think or what will happen | "She didn't text; she's angry." |
| 6 | Magnification and minimisation | Blowing up mistakes, shrinking strengths | "That typo ruined the whole report." |
| 7 | Emotional reasoning | "I feel it, so it's true" | "I feel stupid, so I am." |
| 8 | "Should" statements | Rigid rules for self or others | "I should be over this by now." |
| 9 | Labelling and mislabelling | A name instead of a description | "I'm a loser." |
| 10 | Personalisation and blame | Taking all the blame, or putting it all elsewhere | "The shift went badly because of me." |

Later additions include thought–action fusion (Rachman and Shafran, 1999) and social comparison (Psychology Tools).

### Behavioural activation (BA)

- **Activity monitoring:** for a few days, write what you did each hour (or each 2–3 hours), with **M** (mastery or
  accomplishment) and **P** (pleasure) each rated 0–10. Beck's 1979 manual used mastery and pleasure ratings; some
  worksheets use 0–5 or mood 0–10 instead **(scale varies)**.
- **Spot patterns:** when is mood lowest? Which activities give any mastery or pleasure? What gets avoided?
- **Activity scheduling:** plan specific, small activities at specific times, especially ones linked to values;
  grade them (start small). Ekers et al. defined BA as including "key elements of self-monitoring and activity
  scheduling".
- **TRAP → TRAC:** Martell and colleagues describe Trigger, Response, Avoidance Pattern, and replacing avoidance with
  Alternative Coping **(from general knowledge of the 2001 book)**.
- **Values and life areas:** relationships, work or study, recreation, body and health, spirituality, daily tasks;
  choose one small action in each (BATD) **(from general knowledge)**.

### Homework and review

CBT sessions typically set practice between sessions and start the next session by reviewing it. The NHS describes
CBT as usually 5–15 sessions and says practising between sessions is "important… for CBT to work". A 2016 meta-analysis
of 17 studies (2,312 people) found both the amount and the quality of homework were linked to better outcomes
(Hedges' g about 0.8 at post-treatment).

## 6. Setting it up

### With a therapist

Your therapist will usually introduce the model with your own example, then assign the first two or three columns
(situation, mood, thought) for a week before adding evidence and alternatives. Activity monitoring often comes first if
you're depressed and low on energy.

### Self-guided

1. Pick a structured guide: *Mind Over Mood* or the free CCI workbooks ("Back from the Bluez" for depression).
2. Print or rule a few thought records (portrait, one per page) and one weekly activity grid.
3. **Week 1:** activity monitoring only, plus columns 1–3 of a thought record for one upset each day if there is one.
4. **Week 2:** add columns 4–5 (evidence).
5. **Week 3:** add columns 6–7 (balanced thought and re-rating).
6. **Week 4 on:** schedule activities from what you learned; use full thought records for the day's hottest moment.
7. Keep a list of the distortions on a bookmark.
8. Decide when you'd get help: if two weeks of practice change nothing, or if mood keeps falling, or if you have any
   thoughts of suicide, talk to a clinician.

### First day

Notice one moment where your mood dropped. Write the situation in one line, name the mood, rate it, and write the exact
words in your head. Stop there. That's a complete first record.

### First week

Aim for three to five partial records and a few activity logs. Don't worry about balanced thoughts yet. At the end of
the week, look for patterns: same situations, same thoughts, same times of day.

## 7. Daily practice

### Thought record (10–20 minutes, as soon as practical after an upset, or in the evening)

1. **Situation** (1 line, facts only).
2. **Moods**: 1–3 words, each rated 0–100%.
3. **Thoughts**: write them all; then circle the hot one. Ask: "What does this say about me, my life, my future?"
4. **Evidence for**: facts, not feelings or interpretations.
5. **Evidence against**: use the evidence questions.
6. **Balanced thought**: often "Even though… also…" Rate belief 0–100%.
7. **Re-rate moods.** A drop of 10–20 points is a useful change; zero change is also data (the hot thought may be
   elsewhere, or the problem may be real and need action).
8. **Next step:** if the problem is real, write one action (problem-solving, ask for help, an experiment).

### Activity log (1–2 minutes each time)

At set points (e.g. lunch, end of shift, bedtime), fill in the hours since the last entry: what you did, M 0–10,
P 0–10, and optionally mood 0–10.

### Activity scheduling (5 minutes, evening)

Choose 1–3 activities for tomorrow: one small mastery, one small pleasure, one values-linked (e.g. text a friend).
Put each in a time slot. Tomorrow, tick what happened and rate it.

## 8. Rhythms

| Rhythm | What happens |
|---|---|
| Daily | Activity log or schedule; a thought record when a mood dips (0–1 a day) |
| Weekly | Review records: common hot thoughts, distortions, situations; M/P patterns; plan next week's activities. In therapy, the session starts here. |
| Monthly | Look for rules and core beliefs behind repeated hot thoughts; move to behavioural experiments or "core belief records"; track a mood measure (e.g. *Mind Over Mood* depression and anxiety inventories) |
| Quarterly | Relapse-prevention plan: warning signs, what worked, which pages to go back to |
| Yearly | Keep a short "what I learned" page; archive records privately |
| Archiving | Records hold personal material; keep them private, or shred the details and keep the balanced thoughts |

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Creator | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| Daily Record of Dysfunctional Thoughts (5-column) | Beck, Rush, Shaw, Emery (1979) | The original; no separate evidence columns |
| 7-column thought record | Greenberger and Padesky (1995) | Splits evidence into for and against; hot thought |
| Triple-column technique | Burns (1980) | Thought, distortion, rational response; fast |
| Daily Mood Log | Burns | Upsetting event, emotions rated, thoughts with belief %, distortions, positive thoughts **(from general knowledge)** |
| ABC / ABCDE diary | Ellis (REBT); CCI worksheets | Beliefs and disputation; ABC, ABCD and ABCDE versions on CCI |
| Trial-based thought record | de Oliveira (trial-based cognitive therapy) | A "courtroom" format to address core beliefs; small RCT in social anxiety |
| Core belief record / positive data log | Padesky and others | Collects evidence for a new belief over weeks |
| Behavioural experiment record | Bennett-Levy and others | Prediction, experiment, result, what I learned; a small trial found faster belief change than thought records |
| Behavioural activation (BA) | Jacobson, Martell, Addis; Lejuez (BATD) | Activity-first; little or no thought challenging |
| Worry log / worry time | CBT for generalised anxiety | Postpones worry to a set time **(general knowledge)** |
| Recovery-Oriented Cognitive Therapy (CT-R) | Aaron Beck's later work | Focuses on aspirations and strengths for serious mental illness |
| TEAM-CBT Daily Mood Log | Burns | Adds testing, empathy, assessment of resistance |

## 12. Community practice

- **What people do:** self-help forums and CBT subreddits share filled-in examples, phone notes versions, and
  bookmark lists of distortions. Many say the first weeks feel mechanical, and it helps to do a few with someone.
- **Common customisations:** shortening to 3–5 columns; adding "what I'll do next"; a "self-compassion" line; tagging
  distortions with codes; using a notebook with one record per page; combining with a gratitude line.
- **Aesthetic vs minimalist:** some use bullet-journal spreads with colour-coded distortion keys; most keep plain
  printed forms or apps. The method needs no decoration.
- **Common complaints:** "arguing with myself", "I know it's irrational but still feel it" (head vs heart), and
  thought records that turn into rumination when done too late at night.
- **BA in practice:** people often report activity scheduling is easier to start with during low mood than thought
  records.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Paper:** letter or half-letter; 80–100 gsm. Portrait stacked boxes are easier in a bound journal than landscape.
- **Pen and pencil:** pencil for planned activities, pen for done ones (a common tip).
- **A bookmark** with the ten distortions and the evidence questions.
- **A watch or phone alarm** for activity logs (or an e-ink reader clock).
- Nothing decorative needed.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps:** many thought record and mood apps exist, some with guided prompts. Check privacy and whether data is sold
  or shared. Guided internet CBT programmes are offered by health services in some countries.
- **Templates:** CCI PDFs; *Mind Over Mood* worksheets; printable activity grids.
- **E-ink:** fillable PDFs on reMarkable, Kindle Scribe or Boox work well; the handwriting pace suits the method.
- **Scanning:** photograph records for a therapist; keep in a private folder.
- **Gained digitally:** reminders, searchable hot thoughts, automatic charts of mood before and after. **Lost:** the
  slower, reflective pace of writing by hand, and privacy if synced.

## 16. Evidence and research

| Study | Sample | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hofmann et al., 2012, *Cognit Ther Res* (review of meta-analyses) | 269 meta-analyses | Strongest support for anxiety disorders, somatoform disorders, bulimia, anger and general stress; depression results mixed vs other active therapies | Broad; publication bias possible; few studies in low-income and minority groups |
| Jacobson et al., 1996, *J Consult Clin Psychol* | 150 depressed outpatients; three conditions | BA alone as effective as full CT, maintained at follow-up | Classic; challenged the need for cognitive work |
| Dimidjian et al., 2006, *J Consult Clin Psychol* | 241 adults with major depression | In more severe depression, BA comparable to medication; both beat CT | Single trial; later work mixed on CT vs BA |
| Cuijpers, van Straten and Warmerdam, 2007, *Clin Psychol Rev* | 16 RCTs, 780 people | Activity scheduling vs control: effect size 0.87; vs CT: 0.02 (no difference) | Small trials |
| Ekers et al., 2014, *PLoS ONE* | 26 RCTs, 1,524 people | BA vs controls SMD −0.74; vs medication −0.42 | Only 6 studies of good quality; small arms; short follow-up |
| Richards et al., 2016 (COBRA), *Lancet* | 440 adults | BA by junior workers non-inferior to CBT by therapists at 12 months; lower cost | Large, pragmatic; UK services |
| Kazantzis et al., 2016, *Behav Ther* | 17 studies, 2,312 people | Homework quantity and quality both linked to outcome (g ≈ 0.8) | Correlational |
| McManus, Van Doorn and Yiend, 2012, *J Behav Ther Exp Psychiatry* | 91 non-clinical participants | Thought records and behavioural experiments both beat control on belief and anxiety; experiments changed belief sooner | Non-clinical, single session design |
| Rozek, Smith and Simons, 2018, *Biol Psychol* | 100 participants, lab stressor | Thought record group had a higher peak cortisol response; no group difference in affect | Single-use lab study; unclear meaning |
| de Oliveira et al., 2012, *J Clin Pharm Ther* | 36 with social anxiety | Trial-based thought record at least as good as conventional CT (which used the 7-column record) | Small |
| NICE NG222 (2022) | Guideline | Guided self-help, CBT and BA among first-line options for less severe depression **(guideline page blocked automated reading; details from search summaries, uncertain)** | — |

**Claimed vs supported:**
- *Supported:* CBT works for depression and anxiety; BA alone works for depression about as well as CBT; homework
  practice is linked to better outcomes; thought records can shift beliefs (at least in the lab).
- *Partly supported:* the thought record as the key active ingredient. Component studies suggest behavioural parts do
  much of the work in depression.
- *Claimed:* thought records alone, without guidance, reliably improve mood. Self-help CBT books have trial support as
  a package **(uncertain for thought records alone)**.

## 17. Benefits

- Lower depression and anxiety symptoms (CBT as a package): **evidenced**.
- BA improves depression, including more severe depression: **evidenced**.
- BA can be delivered simply and cheaply: **evidenced** (COBRA).
- Doing the practice is linked to better outcomes: **evidenced** (correlational).
- Belief in unhelpful thoughts goes down: **evidenced** (lab and small trials).
- Separates feelings from facts; a skill for life: **claimed**.
- Shows patterns (situations, times, people): **claimed**.
- Gives a concrete thing to do with a spiralling thought: **claimed**.
- Activity logs show that small things lift mood a little: **claimed**, widely reported.

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---|---|
| Too long; skipped when most needed | Do columns 1–3 only in the moment; finish later, or use the 3-column form |
| Evidence "against" feels fake | Use facts only; include true negatives in the balanced thought |
| The thought is accurate (real discrimination, real money trouble) | Switch to problem-solving and support; CBT isn't meant to talk you out of real problems |
| Turns into rumination at night | Set a 15-minute limit; do records earlier; switch to activity scheduling on low nights |
| "I know it's not true but still feel it" | Normal. Test it with a behavioural experiment; re-rate after acting |
| Too depressed to write | Start with BA: one small scheduled activity a day |
| Perfectionism about the "right" distortion | Distortions overlap; any fitting label is fine |
| Feels invalidating ("my feelings are wrong") | Feelings are valid; the record checks thoughts, not whether you're allowed to feel |
| Only done in therapy weeks | Anchor to a daily cue; put a blank record in the day page |
| Activity plans too big | Grade tasks; split into 10-minute pieces |
| Records left lying around | Private notebook; initials or code words for people |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** use the 3-column or ABC form; circle-the-distortion lists instead of writing; do it at the same time as an
  existing habit; activity scheduling with time circles helps with time blindness. See
  [adhd-journaling](adhd-journaling.md).
- **Autism and sensory needs:** name the sensory part of the situation (noise, light) as a fact; emotion words can be
  hard: use body cues and a words-to-circle list; be careful with "mind reading" as a distortion, since social
  uncertainty is a real part of autistic life, not only a distortion. See
  [autistic-sensory-journaling](autistic-sensory-journaling.md).
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** BA must respect energy limits (pacing): count planned rest as an activity; rate
  mastery generously; one record per week is enough.
- **Depression and anxiety:** BA first when energy is low; thought records when there's a bit more capacity.
- **Trauma:** trauma-related thoughts are better worked on with a trauma-focused therapist (for example cognitive
  processing therapy); keep details off the page.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** voice notes, circle-the-word lists, fewer columns; symbols for moods.
- **Low vision:** large print portrait form, one box per page region; 14 pt+; thick rules.
- **Motor or hand pain:** bubble ratings instead of numbers; dictation; the 3-column form.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** minority stress is real. Thoughts like "people are staring at me" may be
  accurate; the record should allow "true, and…" balanced thoughts and move to coping and safety. Look for
  gender-affirming CBT providers. Dysphoria is not a distortion to be argued away.
- **Shift workers:** use a "waking day" for logs; activity grids with shift blocks; schedule pleasant activities on
  days off and short ones on work days.
- **Low income:** free CCI workbooks; NHS Talking Therapies (England); library copies of *Mind Over Mood*; paper
  forms only need a pen.

## 21. Combining

- **DBT diary card:** use a thought record for the moment behind the day's highest number. See
  [dbt-diary-card](dbt-diary-card.md).
- **Mood tracking:** a daily mood pixel shows the big picture; thought records explain the dips. See
  [mood-tracking-year-in-pixels](mood-tracking-year-in-pixels.md).
- **Habit tracking:** scheduled activities can become habit dots once they stick. See
  [habit-tracking](habit-tracking.md).
- **Symptom tracking:** sleep, pain and energy affect both mood and activity plans. See
  [symptom-tracking](symptom-tracking.md).
- **Stoic journaling:** the dichotomy of control pairs well with "is this thought about something I can change?" See
  [stoic-journaling](stoic-journaling.md).
- **Expressive writing:** freewrite first, then pick the hot thought out of it for a record. See
  [expressive-writing](expressive-writing.md).
- **Safety plan:** if a hot thought is about suicide, switch to the safety plan, not a record. See
  [safety-planning](safety-planning.md).
- **ADHD and sensory needs:** see [adhd-journaling](adhd-journaling.md) and
  [autistic-sensory-journaling](autistic-sensory-journaling.md).

## 24. FAQ

**5 or 7 columns?**
Seven while learning, because splitting evidence into for and against is where the change happens. Five or three once
it's familiar.

**How many should I do?**
While learning, most programmes suggest about one a day when something upsets you. Quality matters more than number.

**What if my mood doesn't change?**
Check: is this the hot thought? Is the evidence facts? Is the problem real and needing action? Zero change is common
early on.

**Isn't this just positive thinking?**
No. Balanced thoughts include the hard truths. The aim is accuracy.

**What if the thought is true?**
Then the record's job is done: move to problem-solving, support, or acceptance. CBT is not about denying real
problems, including discrimination.

**Should I start with thought records or activity scheduling?**
If you're depressed and low on energy, activity scheduling first is well supported by BA trials.

**Can I do CBT on my own?**
Self-help books and guided programmes have evidence, and health services use them as a first step. If you're not
improving after a few weeks, or you feel worse, get professional help.

**Why rate things in percentages?**
To see change. Moving from 80% to 60% is progress even if the mood is still there.

**Where do I keep records?**
Somewhere private. The page can use initials or code words for people.

**Does it help anxiety as well as depression?**
Yes, CBT has strong evidence for anxiety disorders; anxiety often uses behavioural experiments and exposure more than
thought records.

### e. Don't adopt

- **AI-generated "balanced thoughts" or chat.** Out of scope, and the value is in doing it yourself.
- **Scoring, streaks or "cognitive fitness" badges.** Pressure and shame; out of scope.
- **Labelling every negative thought a distortion.** Some are accurate (minority stress, real risk). Keep "true,
  and…" language and a next-step line.
- **Copying *Mind Over Mood* or CCI forms verbatim** into commercial KDP books; they're copyrighted. Use original
  wording and cite them in back matter as further reading.
- **Thought records on the X4 screen.** Writing belongs on paper; the X4 should hold only quick numbers and ticks.
- **Hourly prompts to log activity.** No notifications; the paper grid and the sleep screen are enough.
- **Using a thought record in a crisis.** Point to the safety plan instead.

## 26. Open questions

- How much do thought records add on top of behavioural activation for depression? Component studies suggest not much;
  it may differ for anxiety.
- Do self-guided thought records (no therapist, no programme) help on their own? Little direct evidence found.
- Best rating format for paper: 0–100 in tens, 0–10, or words? No head-to-head data found.
- The exact column wording of Beck's 1979 DRDT and the *Mind Over Mood* 2nd ed. teaching order were not re-checked
  against the books.
- NICE NG222 wording could not be opened (blocked); summary relies on search results.
- Whether the Rozek et al. cortisol finding means anything for daily journaling is unclear.
- How to present a 0–100% scale on a 1-bit X4 screen calmly: design question.
