# Paper and Grids (page ruling as a method)

A reference for the "hidden method" under every journal: the ruling printed on the page (lines, dots, squares, Cornell, isometric, blank) and the paper it sits on (weight, bleed, ghosting, Tomoe River). What each is for, where the numbers come from, how they print on Amazon KDP black-and-white paper, how they survive a phone scan, and what Journalwright Studio should take from them.

Checked against sources on 2026-09-28. Where a detail couldn't be confirmed from a primary source, it's marked **(unconfirmed)**. Numbers about Journalwright Studio come from `journal/render.mjs` and `journal/daypage.mjs` on `main` at that date.

## 2. Summary

Every journal method borrows a ruling, and the ruling quietly shapes what you write. Lines say "write sentences here"; dots say "write, draw or make a table, your choice"; squares say "align, count and chart"; a Cornell page says "take notes, then question them"; blank says "anything". The pitch (distance between lines or dots) has to match your handwriting: US college rule is 7.1 mm, wide rule 8.7 mm, the Bullet Journal dot grid 5 mm, Hobonichi's grid 3.7 mm. The paper underneath decides whether ink feathers, bleeds or ghosts: heavier paper (100–120 gsm) hides the back of the page, coated thin paper like Tomoe River resists ink without being heavy, and cheap book paper (KDP's 74–90 gsm) is fine for ballpoint and gel but not for wet fountain pens. For printing and scanning, the rule is "faint enough to ignore, dark enough to print": KDP asks for lines of at least 0.75 pt and fills of at least 10% grey, while a phone scanner's black-and-white filter tends to drop anything very pale. Journalwright Studio already prints a 5 mm dot grid, 6.6 mm body lines, 5.6 mm block lines and a 4 mm grid; this doc checks them against those limits and proposes a few additions (a line-pitch option, a large-print ruling, a Cornell block and a pen test page).

## 3. History and origin

### Who made it

No single creator. The rulings come from four streams:

- **School and office paper.** Ruled lines helped children and clerks keep writing level and even. The US settled on three standards: wide (legal) rule at 11/32 in (8.7 mm), college (medium) rule at 9/32 in (7.1 mm) and narrow rule at 1/4 in (6.4 mm), each usually with a vertical margin about 1¼ in (32 mm) from the left edge. Specialist rulings came from shorthand: Gregg paper has three lines to the inch and a centre line; Pitman paper has 1/2 in (12.7 mm) spacing and a centre margin. [Ruled paper, Wikipedia]
- **Graph and squared paper.** The earliest known grid paper dates to about 1596 (held at the Metropolitan Museum of Art). A "Dr. Buxton of England" patented printed coordinate paper in 1794. By the early 1900s mathematicians pushed squared paper into schools. [Graph paper, Wikipedia]
- **European school rulings.** France uses Seyès ("grands carreaux"): heavy lines 8 mm apart with three faint lines 2 mm apart between them, plus vertical lines, so children learn letter heights. "Petits carreaux" is plain 5 × 5 mm squares. In Britain the usual adult ruling is 8 mm, narrow is 6 mm and maths paper is 5 mm squares. [Ruled paper, Wikipedia]
- **Journaling brands.** Dot grid existed for decades as technical and "dot paper", but it became the journaling default when Leuchtturm1917 made the official Bullet Journal notebook with Ryder Carroll. The current Edition 2 is dot-grid only, on 120 g/m² paper. [Leuchtturm1917, Bullet Journal Edition 2; bulletjournal.com] In Japan, Hobonichi (founded around Shigesato Itoi's website) built its planner around graph paper and spent years tuning the square size. [Hobonichi, History]

### Cultural context

- **Lines are school.** Many adults associate lined paper with being marked wrong. Dot grid spread partly because it looks less like homework.
- **Dots are "permission".** The dot grid became the badge of the Bullet Journal and the planner-art community: structure when you want it, freedom when you don't.
- **Grids are Japanese planners.** Hobonichi's customers write Japanese, where a square per character is natural. Its 3.7 mm size was "the result of extensive tests for an ideal size for writing in Japanese", although "the size also allows comfortable writing in any language". [Hobonichi Techo Original 2027]
- **Paper is a hobby.** Fountain-pen users compare papers the way others compare coffee. Tomoe River's 2021 change of maker was news in that world.

### Key quotes

- Hobonichi on the grid: 3.7 mm graph paper "was the result of extensive tests for an ideal size for writing in Japanese". [Hobonichi Techo Original 2027]
- Leuchtturm1917 on 120 g/m² paper: "due to its low transparency, previous entries do not shine through and the focus is entirely on the new page." [Leuchtturm1917 Bullet Journal Edition 2]
- Goulet Pens: "Your ink, paper, and pen make the trifecta of your writing experience." [Goulet, Why Paper Matters]
- KDP: give lines "a minimum thickness/weight of 0.75 point or 0.01" (0.3 mm)". [KDP Paperback Submission Guidelines]

## 4. Philosophy and principles

There's no manifesto, but the practice has clear principles:

1. **The page should guide, then disappear.** A ruling earns its place if you stop noticing it after a minute. Heavy, dark rulings fight the writing.
2. **Match pitch to hand.** Line or dot spacing should be a bit taller than your natural lowercase letters plus room for ascenders and descenders. Too tight cramps, too wide makes writing look shaky and wastes space.
3. **Structure is a spectrum.** Blank → dots → squares → lines → forms. Pick the least structure that still keeps you steady.
4. **Paper and pen are one system.** A ruling is only as good as the ink that sits cleanly on it.
5. **Design for the back of the page too.** Ghosting and bleed make the next page harder to use.
6. **If it will be scanned, design for the camera.** Faint guides help the hand and should vanish in a scan; marks meant to be read (codes, frames, filled bubbles) must be dark and solid.

The problem it solves: "my journal looks messy", "I can't make tables", "I can't read my own writing", "the pen bleeds through" and "the scan looks grey and muddy" are nearly always paper and ruling problems, not discipline problems.

## 5. Core components

| Element | What it is | Typical numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Lined (ruled) | Horizontal lines, sometimes a margin line | Wide 8.7 mm, college 7.1 mm, narrow 6.4 mm; UK 8 mm / 6 mm |
| Dot grid | A grid of small dots at the crossing points | 5 mm (Leuchtturm1917, Bullet Journal) |
| Square grid (graph, quad) | Full grid of faint lines | 5 mm (Europe), 1/4 in (US quad), 4 mm, 3.7 mm (Hobonichi daily), 3.45 mm (Hobonichi monthly) |
| Seyès | Heavy lines every 8 mm with 2 mm sub-lines and verticals | 8 / 2 mm |
| Cornell | Cue column, note area, summary band | Cue 2.5 in, summary 2 in (Cornell LSC) |
| Isometric | Triangular grid at 60°, for 3D sketches | Often 5 mm triangles |
| Blank | No ruling | — |
| Bold-line / large print | Thick dark lines, wide pitch | APH: 7/16 in (11.1 mm) or 9/16 in (14.3 mm) |
| Margin line | Vertical line, often red or grey | About 1¼ in (32 mm) from left on US paper |
| Paper weight | Grams per square metre (gsm) | KDP 74–90; Leuchtturm1917 80–100; Bullet Journal Ed. 2 120 |
| Coating / sizing | Surface treatment that stops ink soaking in | Tomoe River, Rhodia, Clairefontaine |
| Line weight and colour | How thick and dark the ruling prints | KDP minimum 0.75 pt line, 10% grey fill |

### 5.1 Lined paper

- **What it's for:** prose. Journals, letters, morning pages, lists.
- **Pitch guide (for adults):**
  - small, tight handwriting: 6–6.4 mm (narrow);
  - average: 7–7.1 mm (college);
  - large or loose handwriting, or writing on your knee: 8–8.7 mm (wide);
  - low vision or hand tremor: 11–14 mm bold-line.
- **Colour:** grey or pale blue is calm; black is harsh except on bold-line paper, where contrast is the point.
- **Margin:** a left margin gives a place for dates, bullets or symbols (the Bullet Journal uses this idea without a printed line).
- **Weakness:** tables and drawings fight the lines.

### 5.2 Dot grid

- **What it's for:** mixed use. Text, tables, trackers, sketches, arrows.
- **How it works:** dots mark the crossing points of an invisible 5 mm grid, so you can write on the "lines", draw boxes along the "columns", or ignore both.
- **Why 5 mm:** it roughly matches a comfortable adult line height for small-to-average handwriting and makes easy 1 cm tracker squares.
- **Weakness:** some people find large, dark dots distracting; others find pale dots too faint to follow. Dot size and grey level matter more than spacing.
- **Numbers:** Leuchtturm1917's dotted pages are widely described as 5 mm dots (retailer listings; the official product page opened for this doc doesn't state the spacing, so **(unconfirmed from the maker)**).

### 5.3 Square grid

- **What it's for:** maths, charts, habit grids, floor plans, Japanese writing, tidy tables.
- **Sizes:**
  - **5 mm:** European standard; two squares per line of average writing if you skip a row, or one square per line for small hands.
  - **1/4 in (6.35 mm):** US quad ruled; four squares per inch.
  - **4 mm:** a common planner size (the editor's grid option).
  - **3.7 mm:** Hobonichi daily pages. One character per square in Japanese; in English, most people write across about two squares per line of text or one square per line in very small writing.
  - **Engineering paper:** faint green or tan grid printed on the back so it shows through but disappears in photocopies. [Graph paper, Wikipedia]
- **Weakness:** a full grid is the busiest ruling. Make it faint, dashed or dotted.

### 5.4 Seyès (French school ruling)

- Heavy line every 8 mm, three light lines at 2 mm, vertical lines every 8 mm.
- Teaches letter height: x-height takes one 2 mm band, ascenders reach up two or three.
- Journalers use it rarely, but it's the best example of a ruling that encodes handwriting size.

### 5.5 Cornell notes

- **Layout:** a cue column 2.5 in wide on the left, a summary band 2 in tall at the bottom, notes in the rest. [Cornell LSC]
- **Process:** record, reduce (write questions or cues in the left column), recite, reflect, review. [Cornell LSC]
- **Creator:** Walter Pauk, a Cornell education professor. [Cornell LSC]
- **Why it matters for journals:** it's a ruling that carries a *ritual*: write first, question later, summarise last. It adapts neatly to a day page: events in the main area, "what this meant" in the cue column, one line of summary at the bottom.

### 5.6 Isometric

- Triangular grid: lines at 30°, 90° and 150°, or dots in a triangular pattern.
- For drawing 3D objects, room plans, knitting and hexagon patterns.
- Almost never used for writing. Niche for journals.

### 5.7 Blank

- No guidance at all. Best for art journaling and sketching, and for people who find any printed mark too much.
- Weakness: writing drifts; lists get uneven. A guide sheet (a dark-lined sheet placed underneath) solves this on thin paper.

### 5.8 Paper weight (gsm)

- **gsm** means grams per square metre. US "pound" weights depend on the paper grade, so compare in gsm.
- Common weights:
  - copy paper: about 75–80 gsm;
  - KDP interior paper: 50–61 lb, which KDP gives as 74–90 gsm, for black ink on white or cream [KDP Print Options];
  - Leuchtturm1917 standard notebooks: 80–100 gsm [Leuchtturm1917 hardcover notebook];
  - Leuchtturm1917 Bullet Journal Edition 2: 120 g/m² [Leuchtturm1917 BuJo Ed. 2];
  - Tomoe River: very thin (commonly cited 52 gsm and 68 gsm versions **(unconfirmed from maker)**), yet ink-resistant because of its surface.
- Heavier paper ghosts less. Weight alone doesn't stop feathering; coating does.

### 5.9 Bleed, ghosting, feathering, show-through

Definitions from Goulet Pens and others:

- **Feathering:** ink spreads into the paper fibres, so lines look fuzzy with little "veins".
- **Bleed-through:** ink soaks through to the back.
- **Ghosting (show-through):** you can see writing from the other side, but no ink got through.
- **Causes:** absorbent fibres (feathering, bleed), thinness (ghosting), wet ink and broad nibs (all three).
- **Fixes:** coated or sized paper (Rhodia, Clairefontaine, Tomoe River), heavier paper, finer and drier pens, lighter inks. [Goulet, Why Paper Matters]

### 5.10 Tomoe River and the 2021 change

- **Original:** made by Tomoegawa, famous for being very thin yet showing ink "sheen" and "shading" with little feathering. Goulet calls it "thin yet highly ink-resistant".
- **The change:** Tomoegawa announced it would transfer the Tomoe River "product sales contract, trademark rights and inventory" to Sanzen Paper Manufacturing Co., Ltd. of Kanazawa, with the transfer planned for 28 November 2021, as part of a restructuring of its functional paper business. [Tomoegawa, 28 Oct 2021]
- **What changed in the paper:** one side-by-side test found Sanzen's paper "a little thicker and slightly toothier", with sheen and shading kept and no feathering in either, but "some" bleed-through on the Sanzen paper where the Tomoegawa had none. [Inkcredible Colours, 2023] Others report slightly longer dry times **(unconfirmed)**.
- **Now:** Sanzen refined the paper in 2024–2025 ("Tomoe River S"), and Hobonichi uses it in all planners from 2025 (Wikipedia; not confirmed on Hobonichi's own page).
- **Why it matters here:** KDP can't print on Tomoe River. It's a reference point for what "fountain-pen friendly" means, not an option for Journalwright Studio books.

### 5.11 Line weight and colour for print

KDP's rules for interiors [KDP Paperback Submission Guidelines]:

- lines at least **0.75 pt** (0.01 in, 0.3 mm) thick;
- grey fills at least **10%**;
- text at least **7 pt**;
- images at least **300 DPI**.

KDP prints black-and-white interiors on a toner or inkjet press, so very thin or very pale marks can break up, vanish or print unevenly between copies and printing plants ("paper weight varies based on printing location"). [KDP Print Options]

### 5.12 Ergonomics and handwriting size

- **Line pitch versus letter size:** a rough rule is that x-height (the height of "a", "e", "o") should be about a third to half of the pitch. At 7.1 mm pitch that's roughly 2.5–3.5 mm letters. **(Rule of thumb, not a standard.)**
- **Writing surface:** writing standing up, on a clipboard, or on a bus makes letters bigger and looser. Shift workers who write in odd places may want a wider pitch than they'd pick at a desk.
- **Dysgraphia:** Understood.org recommends "paper with different-colored or raised lines to help form letters in the right space" and "graph paper (or lined paper to be used sideways) to help line up math problems". [Understood]
- **Low vision:** bold-line paper uses thick black lines on bright white at 7/16 in (11.1 mm) or 9/16 in (14.3 mm), with a left margin. [APH]

### 5.13 How rulings survive phone scanning

Most scanning apps (Apple Notes, OneDrive, Rocketbook, Adobe Scan) crop the page, flatten perspective, and offer a "document" or black-and-white filter that boosts contrast and pushes pale greys to white. What that means for rulings **(from how these filters generally behave; test with your own phone)**:

- **Very pale rulings (under ~20% grey)** mostly vanish in black-and-white mode. That's good: the scan shows only your writing. Whitelines paper was designed for this: grey paper with white lines, so "guidelines don't show up when copied or scanned". [Well-Appointed Desk]
- **Mid-grey lines (~40%)** often survive as grey or broken lines. They help a reader but add noise for OCR.
- **Black lines** always survive.
- **Dots** survive worse than lines (they're small), which is one reason dot grid scans look clean.
- **Colour:** pale blue ("non-photo blue") was traditionally invisible to photocopiers. A B&W KDP book can't print colour, so grey is the only lever.

The Journalwright Studio approach follows this split: faint dots and grey lines for the hand, a solid 9 pt black frame, solid symbols and a black Data Matrix for the camera. See the [scan systems doc](rocketbook-and-scan-systems.md).

## 6. Setting it up

### First setup (one sitting, about 30 minutes)

1. **Measure your handwriting.** Write a normal sentence on blank paper. Measure the height of a lowercase "x" and of a "d" or "l". Note it.
2. **Pick a pitch.** Multiply x-height by about 2.5 for a starting line pitch. Try 6.4, 7.1 and 8.7 mm lines side by side.
3. **Pick a structure.** Mostly sentences → lines. Mixed → dots. Charts or Japanese → grid. Notes you'll study → Cornell. Drawing → blank.
4. **Test your pens.** Write each pen on the paper, including a filled square, and wait 30 seconds. Smudge test with a finger. Turn the page: note ghosting and bleed.
5. **Test your scanner** (if you scan). Photograph the page with your usual app in B&W mode. Check that the ruling disappears or stays faint, and your writing stays crisp.
6. **Decide on the back of the page.** If your pens ghost, plan to write on one side only, or use the back for low-stakes lists.

### First day

- Write a normal entry. Don't fight the ruling; note where it annoyed you.

### First week

- On day 3, look back: are letters touching lines above or below (pitch too tight)? Do lines look empty (pitch too wide)? Is the grid distracting?
- On day 7, swap one variable (pitch, ruling type or pen) if something bothered you more than twice.

## 7. Daily practice

Ruling isn't a daily ritual, but a few habits help:

1. **Before writing (5 seconds):** pick the pen that suits today's paper.
2. **While writing:** let the ruling carry your baseline; use dots or grid to line up lists and tables.
3. **When filling bubbles or boxes that will be scanned:** fill solidly with dark ink (see the scan doc).
4. **After writing (10 seconds):** close the book only when ink is dry, or blot. Wet ink on the facing page is a ghost you made yourself.
5. **Before scanning:** flatten the page and keep fingers off the frame.

---

## 8. Rhythms

- **Weekly:** none needed. If you tried a new pen, add a line to your pen test page.
- **Monthly:** with a new book, re-check the paper choice for each block in the editor. Was the grid or lined paper right for how the month went?
- **Quarterly:** do a fresh handwriting check. Stress, fatigue, pain and meds change handwriting size.
- **Yearly:** order a proof of any new layout and do a full pen and scan test on the real KDP paper (printing plants vary).
- **Archiving:** store finished books upright, away from damp. Gel and fountain inks can transfer in heat.

## 10. Worked examples

### 10.1 A day: choosing rulings for a day page

## 12. Community practice

What people actually do (from forums, reviews and planner communities):

- **Dot grid dominates journaling.** It's the default for Bullet Journal, planner art and "commonplace" notebooks.
- **Grid dominates Japanese planners.** Hobonichi users often write across two squares per line in English.
- **Paper reviews are a genre.** Reviewers test fountain pens, gel pens, highlighters and brush pens, photograph the back of the page, and grade feathering, bleed and ghosting. The Well-Appointed Desk's Whitelines review is typical: fountain pens showed "subtle voids in the strokes" on the waxy grey paper, and the reviewer "would not recommend the Whitelines Link to fountain pen enthusiasts".
- **Common customisations:**
  - printing your own dot grid or grid PDFs at a chosen pitch and grey;
  - putting a dark-lined guide sheet under blank paper;
  - drawing a margin line in pencil;
  - writing every other line on narrow rule;
  - cutting washi tape into column headers on grid paper.
- **Aesthetic camp:** fountain pens, Tomoe River, heavy 120–160 gsm paper for watercolour and markers, pastel inks, dot grid.
- **Minimalist camp:** any notebook, a ballpoint, lined paper, no decoration. Weight and ruling matter less than always having the book with you.
- **Paper grievances:** ghosting on 80 gsm is the most common complaint about mid-price notebooks; heavier 120 gsm paper answers it at the cost of fewer pages and a thicker book.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Pens for KDP paper (74–90 gsm, uncoated):**
  - best: ballpoint and hybrid ink (Uni Jetstream), fine gel (0.38–0.5), fineliners (0.3–0.4), pencil;
  - okay: medium gel, felt-tip; expect light ghosting;
  - avoid on day pages: wet fountain pens, broad markers, alcohol markers (bleed).
- **Pens for Tomoe River:** fountain pens shine, but dry time is long. Keep blotting paper handy.
- **Pens for scan bubbles:** a dark, solid ink (gel or felt-tip). Pencil scans grey.
- **Ruler:** 15 cm clear ruler with a 5 mm grid printed on it, for dot and grid pages.
- **Guide sheet:** a dark-lined sheet slipped under a page (works on thin paper only).
- **Blotting paper:** for fountain pens.
- **Stickers and washi:** stick on grid paper, not over scan zones (frame, strip, page code).
- **Stamps:** ink stamps can bleed on thin paper; test on the pen test page.
- **Paper weight cheatsheet:** 52–68 gsm Tomoe River (thin, coated); 74–90 gsm KDP; 80–100 gsm Leuchtturm1917; 120 gsm Bullet Journal Ed. 2; 160 gsm+ mixed-media.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Gained:** change ruling per page, no ghosting, infinite undo, zoom for large print.

## 16. Evidence and research

### Handwriting versus typing

| Study | Sample | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014), *Psychological Science* | Three studies with university students | Longhand note-takers did better on conceptual questions than laptop note-takers; laptop users transcribed more verbatim | Widely cited, small lab samples, lecture recall only. (Journal page blocked automated access; summarised from the replication below.) |
| Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson (2019), *Educational Psychology Review* | Direct replication plus eWriter and no-notes groups | "Performance did not consistently differ between any groups"; meta-analysis showed "small (nonsignificant) effects favoring longhand"; concluded it's premature to pick a winner | Stronger: replication plus meta-analysis. Still about lecture notes, not journaling |

### Ruling and handwriting

- **Dysgraphia:** coloured, raised and wider lines, and graph paper for maths, are standard classroom accommodations. [Understood] These are expert practice guidance, not tested in controlled trials that this research found.
- **Low vision:** bold-line paper is standard low-vision practice (APH sells it with federal quota funds). No trials found comparing pitches.
- **Dot grid versus lines:** no studies found. Claims that dot grid "boosts creativity" are anecdotal.

### Paper

- **Feathering, bleed and ghosting** are physical properties (absorbency, thickness, coating) and are easy to test yourself; no clinical claims involved.

### Claimed versus supported

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Handwriting helps memory more than typing | Mixed; replication found small, non-significant effects |
| The right line pitch makes writing neater | Supported by practice guidance (dysgraphia, low vision); not trial-tested for adults |
| Dot grid is more creative | Claimed only |
| Heavier paper ghosts less | Physical fact; easy to test |
| Faint rulings drop out of scans | True for B&W document filters in general; varies by app (test it) |

## 17. Benefits

- Neater, more even handwriting when pitch matches hand size. **Claimed** (supported by practice guidance for dysgraphia and low vision).
- Tables, trackers and charts line up on dots and grids. **Claimed** (obvious in use; no study needed or found).
- Less ghosting and bleed with the right paper and pen. **Evidenced** (physical property, testable).
- Cleaner scans with faint rulings. **Claimed** (consistent with how scan filters work; varies by app).
- Less "homework feeling" with dots. **Claimed**.
- Handwriting aids recall of what you wrote. **Evidenced but weak** (mixed studies).
- Cornell layout builds a review habit. **Claimed** (Cornell LSC recommends it; no journaling trial found).

## 18. Pitfalls

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Writing looks cramped | Wider pitch (college → wide), or write every other line |
| Writing looks shaky and small in big lines | Narrower pitch, or switch to dots |
| Grid feels busy | Use dots, or a dashed, paler grid |
| Dots too faint to follow | Darker grey dots (still under ~20% so they vanish in scans) or lines |
| Pen ghosts through KDP paper | Fine gel, ballpoint or hybrid ink; fill bubbles with a felt-tip only if tested |
| Fountain pen feathers | Use a finer, drier nib and a drier ink, or keep fountain pens for a separate Tomoe River notebook |
| Smudging (left-handed) | Quick-dry gel or hybrid ink; blot; write in the lower half first |
| Scan shows grey muddy lines | Use B&W or document filter; better light; pale rulings |
| Scan loses pencil | Use ink for anything you'll scan |
| Stickers cover scan zones | Keep stickers inside the writing space |
| Chasing the "perfect paper" | Pick one pen that works on your book paper and stop testing for a month |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** fewer choices is kinder. Keep one default ruling per block. Dots give structure without demanding neatness. A margin for symbols helps scanning your eye down a page.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** pale, even rulings; no busy grids; matte paper (glossy stock glares). Some people are bothered by the feel of toothy paper or the squeak of certain pens; test.
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** wider pitch means fewer, bigger strokes and less precision. Write fewer lines; use check-boxes and circles instead of sentences.
- **Depression and anxiety:** blank pages can feel like a demand. A few printed lines with a small label ("one line is enough") lowers the bar.
- **Trauma:** lined paper can recall school or forms; dots or blank may feel safer. Keep private pages free of scan codes if they shouldn't be scanned.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** coloured or raised lines, a ruling with a clear baseline, graph paper for numbers, wider pitch. Seyès-style sub-lines can help letter sizing. [Understood]
- **Low vision:** bold-line paper (7/16 or 9/16 in), black ink, felt-tip pens, high contrast, no faint dots. The 8.5 × 11 size gives 1.29× bigger everything.
- **Motor or hand pain:** wider pitch, thicker grip pens, ballpoint or gel that needs little pressure, less writing and more circling. A spoon-friendly day page uses bubbles and checkboxes.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** handwriting can be tied to identity; some people practise a new hand. A Seyès-like or guided ruling on a practice page helps. Name and signature practice pages are a gentle use.
- **Shift workers:** writing on clipboards and knees favours wider pitch and quick-dry pens. Books that lie flat help.
- **Low income:** any notebook works. Print your own dot grid at home. KDP paper plus a $2 ballpoint is plenty.

## 23. Glossary

- **Pitch:** distance from one line (or dot row) to the next.
- **Ruling:** the printed guide pattern on a page.
- **Wide / college / narrow rule:** US line pitches of 8.7, 7.1 and 6.4 mm.
- **Gregg / Pitman ruling:** shorthand rulings with a centre line.
- **Seyès:** French school ruling with 8 mm main lines and 2 mm sub-lines.
- **Quad ruled:** US graph paper, usually 4 squares per inch.
- **Dot grid (dotted):** dots at the crossings of an invisible grid.
- **Isometric paper:** triangular grid for 3D drawing.
- **Genkō yōshi:** Japanese manuscript paper, one box per character.
- **Cornell notes:** layout with a cue column, notes area and summary band.
- **Cue column:** the Cornell left column for questions and key words.
- **x-height:** height of lowercase letters without ascenders.
- **gsm (g/m²):** paper weight in grams per square metre.
- **Basis weight (lb):** US paper weight; depends on paper grade.
- **Feathering:** ink spreading along fibres, fuzzy edges.
- **Bleed-through:** ink reaching the back of the page.
- **Ghosting / show-through:** seeing writing through the page without ink passing through.
- **Sizing / coating:** surface treatment that stops ink soaking in.
- **Sheen:** a metallic glint some inks show on coated paper.
- **Shading:** light and dark variation within a stroke.
- **Tooth:** surface roughness.
- **Tomoe River / Tomoe River S:** thin coated Japanese paper; "S" is Sanzen's version.
- **Non-photo blue:** light blue that copiers traditionally ignore.
- **Engineering paper:** grid printed on the back, disappears in copies.
- **Bold-line paper:** thick, dark lines for low vision.
- **Raised-line paper:** lines you can feel.
- **Guide sheet:** dark-lined sheet placed under thin paper.
- **Grey level / fill %:** how dark a tint prints; KDP minimum 10%.
- **Point (pt):** 1/72 in (0.353 mm).
- **Bleed (print):** artwork running past the trim; Journalwright Studio books use no bleed.
- **Trim size:** finished page size (5.5 × 8.5 or 8.5 × 11 in).
- **Threshold (scanning):** the grey level above which a scan filter turns pixels white.

## 24. FAQ

1. **Dots, lines or grid?** Mostly sentences: lines. Mixed: dots. Tables, numbers or tiny handwriting: grid.
2. **What pitch should I use?** Start at college rule (7.1 mm). If letters touch lines, go wider; if lines look empty, go narrower.
3. **Why 5 mm for dot grid?** It's the common European squared-paper size and suits small-to-average handwriting; 1 cm squares for trackers are two dots.
4. **Can I use a fountain pen in a Journalwright Studio book?** A fine, dry nib with a well-behaved ink may be fine; test on the pen areas of the proof test sheet first. KDP's 74–90 gsm uncoated paper isn't made for wet nibs.
5. **Why does my writing ghost?** Thin paper plus dark, wet ink. Use a finer pen or a lighter ink, or write on one side.
6. **Is Tomoe River still the same paper?** No. Sanzen took over in 2021; testers find it slightly thicker and toothier, with a bit more bleed in some tests. The 2024–2025 "S" version is the current one.
7. **Why are the dots so pale?** So they guide your hand but drop out of scans and don't distract. KDP recommends at least 10% grey; the dots are about 14%, though tiny pale dots should still be checked on a proof.
8. **Will the rulings show when I scan?** Pale dots mostly vanish in B&W mode; grey lines may show faintly. Your ink and the black scan markers stay.
9. **What paper is best for low vision?** Bold-line paper with thick black lines at 7/16 or 9/16 in, and a felt-tip pen.
10. **Does handwriting beat typing?** The famous 2014 study said yes for lecture notes; a 2019 replication found no consistent difference. Write by hand because you like it.
11. **What's Cornell for in a journal?** Write first, reflect later: the cue column is for "what did this mean?"
12. **Can the 4 mm grid disappear in print?** Possibly. Its lines are thinner and paler than KDP's line guidance; check a proof (see section 26).

## 26. Open questions

- **Does the 4 mm grid print on KDP?** Its dashed lines were about 0.1 mm (≈ 0.28 pt) and 16% grey, thinner than KDP's 0.75 pt line guidance, and were changed to 0.75 pt at 22% grey (section 5 table). A proof copy should settle whether they print evenly or break up; if they break up, try crossing dots.
- **Is 5.6 mm too tight for block lines?** It's below US narrow rule. Worth testing with your own work handwriting.
- **Does the 0.26 in body line pitch suit clipboard writing?** Possibly too tight when standing.
- **Which greys drop out in your scanning app?** Scan-filter thresholds vary by app and lighting; a grey-ramp test page would answer it.
- **Tomoe River weights and current spec:** Sanzen's official numbers weren't confirmed from a primary source here.
- **Leuchtturm1917 dot spacing** wasn't stated on the official page opened.
- **No evidence** was found comparing dot grid, lines and grid for adult journaling outcomes.
