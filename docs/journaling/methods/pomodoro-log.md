# Pomodoro log (the Pomodoro Technique on paper)

Work in short, protected rounds, mark each one on paper, note what tried to interrupt you, and learn how long things really take.

## 2. Summary

The Pomodoro Technique cuts work into indivisible 25-minute rounds (a "Pomodoro") followed by a 3–5 minute break, with a longer 15–30 minute break after every four. Most people stop there, but Cirillo's original is a paper system with three sheets: an **Activity Inventory** of everything you might do, a **To Do Today** sheet where you list today's tasks, mark an X for each finished round, an apostrophe `'` for each internal interruption and a dash `-` for each external one, and an **Unplanned & Urgent** section for things that came up; and a **Records** sheet where you log, at day's end, how many rounds each task really took compared with your estimate. The rules are few and memorable: a Pomodoro is indivisible; if it begins, it has to ring; more than 5–7 rounds means break the task down; less than one means add small tasks together; and "the next Pomodoro will go better". Research supports short, planned breaks for mood and fatigue, but it doesn't show the 25-minute length is special, and a 2025 study found Pomodoro breaks no better than self-chosen ones on productivity. For a paper journal, the valuable parts are the tiny, forgiving marks: one box per round, a tick for a distraction noticed and parked.

## 3. History and origin

### Who created it

**Francesco Cirillo** is Italian. In the paper's preface he describes his first years at university in the late 1980s: after first-year exams he fell into "a slump, a time of low productivity and high confusion". He bet himself: "Can you study – really study – for 10 minutes?" He used a tomato-shaped kitchen timer as his "Time Tutor" (*pomodoro* is Italian for tomato). He later became a software development mentor and brought the method to programming teams, where it spread through the agile and Extreme Programming community (the acknowledgements thank the Milano XPUG among others).

### Cultural context

The method grew up next to agile software development: short iterations (time-boxing, which Cirillo credits to Steve McConnell's *Rapid Development*), incremental delivery (Tom Gilb) and continuous feedback. Its intellectual roots are unusual for a productivity system: Cirillo cites Henri Bergson and Eugène Minkowski on the experience of time, Hans-Georg Gadamer on play, and Tony Buzan on learning. The central idea is philosophical: time as *becoming* (abstract, slipping away, anxiety-making) versus time as a *succession of events* (concrete, calm). The Pomodoro swaps the first for the second.

### Key quotes from the creator (paper, 2006/2007)

- "For many people, time is an enemy."
- "A Pomodoro can't be interrupted; it marks 25 minutes of pure work… The atomic unit of time is a Pomodoro."
- "You're not allowed to keep on working 'just for a few more minutes'."
- "Stopping becomes synonymous with strength, not weakness."
- "The next Pomodoro will go better."
- "The first thing to learn with the Pomodoro Technique is that seeming fast isn't important, reaching the point of actually being fast is."
- "The Pomodoro Technique shouldn't be used for activities you do in your free time."
- From the official site (2026): "Your goal isn't to complete Pomodoros. Your goal is to become aware of what happens in your mind."

## 4. Philosophy and principles

**Stated goals** (paper §1.1). The technique aims to:

- alleviate anxiety linked to *becoming* (the sense of time running out);
- enhance focus and concentration by cutting down on interruptions;
- increase awareness of your decisions;
- boost motivation and keep it constant;
- bolster the determination to achieve your goals;
- refine estimation, both qualitative and quantitative;
- improve your work or study process;
- strengthen determination in the face of complex situations.

**Basic assumptions** (§1.2)

1. A different way of seeing time eases anxiety and makes you more effective.
2. Better use of the mind brings clearer thought, sharper focus and easier learning.
3. Simple, unobtrusive tools (paper, pencil, a timer) keep the method from adding complexity. "Many time management techniques fail because they subject the people who use them to a higher level of added complexity."

**Core beliefs**

- **Invert the dependency on time.** You no longer chase the clock; the clock serves a sequence of rounds.
- **Invert the dependency on interruptions.** Interruptions get scheduled into later rounds instead of taking over the current one.
- **Detachment is productive.** Breaks are where the mind integrates what it's learned.
- **Measure, don't judge.** Recording is for self-observation and improvement, not surveillance. Cirillo is blunt that it "mustn't be misconstrued as a form of this kind of external control".
- **Sustainable pace.** Respect the timetable; avoid the "Five More Minutes Syndrome"; overtime no more than five days in a row, followed by recovery.

**The problem it solves**

Procrastination driven by time anxiety, constant interruptions, poor estimates and overlong days. Cirillo's diagnosis in the preface: "the high number of distractions and interruptions and the low level of concentration and motivation".

### The Pomodoro (timer and unit)

"The traditional Pomodoro is 30 minutes long: 25 minutes of work plus a 5-minute break." The time left should always be visible. Cirillo prefers a mechanical kitchen timer for three reasons: winding it is "a declaration of your determination", it shows and ticks the time, and it rings clearly. He also wants explicit gestures to mark the end of a round or cross out a task: "it's better if these aren't automated". In shared spaces he lists softer options: vibrating watches, phone apps, muted timers, software.

On length (§3.2), he weighs two forces: the unit must be a fair measure of effort (too short, like 10 minutes, and tracking becomes intrusive), and it must suit attention. "We've come to consider the ideal Pomodoro as 20–35 minutes long, 40 minutes at the most." Teams he mentored tried an hour, two hours, 45 minutes and 10 minutes before settling on 30 (25 + 5).

### Breaks

- **Short break, 3–5 minutes:** stand up, walk, drink water, breathe, stretch. Not: work talk, important emails, calls. "During this break you shouldn't continue thinking about what you've done."
- **Long break after four, 15–30 minutes:** tidy the desk, coffee, voicemail, email, a quick walk. Still nothing complex.
- **When tired:** short breaks can stretch to 10 minutes, but "downtime between Pomodoros that consistently lasts more than 5–10 minutes risks breaking the rhythm". Better to lengthen breaks between sets first.
- **Don't shorten breaks under pressure:** "It would be a serious mistake."
- **Beginner tip:** set the timer for 25 minutes at the start of a long break so it doesn't run past 30.

### To Do Today sheet

Filled in each morning with: a heading (place, date, author), a list of today's tasks in priority order, and a section at the bottom labelled **Unplanned & Urgent Activities**. With estimation, each task gets a row of empty boxes, one per estimated Pomodoro, and the sheet shows **Available Pomodoros** for the day. Each finished round gets an X; interruptions go next to the task as `'` or `-`; finished tasks are crossed out.

### Activity Inventory sheet

A running list with a heading (author name) and lines for activities "as they come up". Each morning, estimate each item in Pomodoros and pick today's tasks from it. Unplanned items that arrive mid-round are added with a `U`, and a deadline in brackets if needed: `U [14 July] Call Carol`. Completed items are checked off (or deleted) at the end of the day. "Any changes to the Activity Inventory can be made with a good pencil and an excellent eraser."

### Records sheet

The raw data. Cirillo's first version has: **Date, Time, Type, Activity, Actual Pomodoros, Notes**. With estimation it becomes **Date, Time, Type of Activity, Description, Estimate, Real, Diff** (and Diff I / Diff II for second estimates). Start times don't need tracking during the day; reconstruct them at night "beginning with the most recent activity and moving backwards". Recording plus improvement should take no more than one Pomodoro.

### Interruption marks

- **Internal (`'`):** you feel the urge to do something else (snack, call, check email, look something up, re-plan). Mark `'`, then write the thing down, either under Unplanned & Urgent (if it can't wait) or in the Activity Inventory with a `U`. Go back to work. It should take "a few seconds at most".
- **External (`-`):** someone or something tries to interrupt. Mark `-`, apply **Inform, Negotiate, Call back**: tell them you're busy, agree when you'll get back to them, then really do it. Write it under Unplanned & Urgent with the promised time in brackets in the margin, or in the Inventory with a `U`.
- **If the round really breaks:** it's void, "even if it's about to ring". Record the interruption mark, don't mark an X, take a break, start a new round. "The Next Pomodoro Will Go Better."

### Estimation marks

- One empty box per estimated round.
- If a task isn't done when the boxes run out: either keep adding X marks past the boxes, or make a **second estimate** drawn in "a different color or shape" to the right. Rarely a third. "All the activities that require a third estimate have to be carefully reconsidered."
- Estimates are whole Pomodoros only ("figures like 5½ Pomodoro aren't allowed").

### Five stages

| Stage | When | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Planning | Start of the day | Decide the day's activities |
| Tracking | Throughout the day | Gather raw data on effort and other metrics |
| Recording | End of the day | Archive the day's observations |
| Processing | End of the day | Turn raw data into information |
| Visualizing | End of the day | Show it in a way that points to improvements |

### Six objectives (the learning path, in order)

1. **Find out how much effort an activity requires.** Just count rounds per task.
2. **Cut down on interruptions.** Mark `'` and `-`; schedule or delete what comes up.
3. **Estimate the effort for activities.** Boxes, second estimates, Records with Diff.
4. **Make the Pomodoro more effective.** Use the first 3–5 minutes to recap and the last 3–5 to review; use the first and last rounds of a set the same way.
5. **Set up a timetable.** Fixed start and end; sets like `[1+3],[4]:[4],[1+1]`.
6. **Other objectives.** Track only the metrics that serve a chosen improvement.

### Rules (Appendix 1 of the paper)

- A Pomodoro consists of 25 minutes plus a five-minute break.
- After every four Pomodoros comes a 15–30 minute break.
- The Pomodoro is indivisible. There are no half or quarter Pomodoros.
- If a Pomodoro begins, it has to ring. (If it's broken, it's void. If you finish the task early, review until it rings. Exception: done in the first five minutes and review isn't worth it, so it doesn't count.)
- Protect the Pomodoro: inform, negotiate, call back.
- If it lasts more than 5–7 Pomodoros, break it down.
- If it lasts less than one Pomodoro, add it up.
- Results are achieved Pomodoro after Pomodoro.
- The next Pomodoro will go better.

## 6. Setting it up

### First setup (15–20 minutes)

1. **Get a timer** you can see. A wind-up kitchen timer is the original; a silent visual timer is gentler (see section 19).
2. **Make three sheets.** Official eSheets or hand-ruled:
   - To Do Today: heading, task lines with space for boxes on the right, a ruled-off **Unplanned & Urgent** area at the bottom.
   - Activity Inventory: heading, many ruled lines, a narrow left margin for `U` and deadlines, a right column for estimates.
   - Records: columns Date, Time, Type, Activity, Est, Real, Diff, Notes.
3. **Fill the Activity Inventory** with everything on your mind.
4. **Choose a timetable.** Start and end times you will really keep. Cirillo's example: 8:30–1:00 and 2:00–5:30.
5. **Decide what counts as "urgent"** for Unplanned & Urgent (hint: less than you think).

### First day

- Objective I only. No estimates yet.
- First round: plan. Copy today's tasks from the Inventory to To Do Today, in priority order.
- Set 25 minutes. Work. At the ring, X next to the task. Break 3–5 minutes.
- Every four, a long break.
- Finish a task? Cross it out. If the round hasn't rung, review or polish until it does.
- Last round: fill in the Records sheet (task and how many X's).

### First week

- **Days 1–2:** count rounds. Even one uninterrupted round is "an excellent result".
- **Days 3–4:** add interruption marks (`'` and `-`). Park, don't act.
- **Days 5–7:** try estimating: empty boxes for each task. Record estimate vs real.
- **End of week:** look at the Records. How many rounds did you really do per day? Use that number as tomorrow's Available Pomodoros.

## 7. Daily practice

**Morning (first round, about 25 minutes)**

1. Look at yesterday's To Do Today and Records.
2. Update estimates in the Activity Inventory.
3. Write Available Pomodoros for today (based on real past numbers, not hopes).
4. Pick tasks that fit, combining small ones ("add it up"). Write them on To Do Today with one empty box per estimated round.
5. Tidy the desk.

**Each round (25 minutes)**

1. Wind the timer. First 3–5 minutes (once you're practised): recap what you've done on this task so far.
2. Work on only this task.
3. Urge to do something else? `'`, write it down, keep going.
4. Someone interrupts? `-`, inform / negotiate / call back, write it down, keep going.
5. Last 3–5 minutes (once practised): review what you did.
6. Ring: X in the next box. Stop, even mid-sentence.

**Each break (3–5 minutes)**

- Stand, move, water, breathe. No work thinking. Glance at Unplanned & Urgent: anything truly urgent?

**Every fourth round**

- Long break, 15–30 minutes. Now's the time for email and voicemail.

**End of day (last round)**

1. Cross out finished tasks; move unfinished ones back to the Inventory.
2. Copy to Records: date, start time (reconstruct backwards), type, activity, estimate, real, difference, interruption counts, a note.
3. One question: how could the same result take fewer rounds?
4. Note tomorrow's first task.
5. Stop at the timetable's end.

## 8. Rhythms

| Rhythm | What to do | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **Per round** | 25 min work; X; interruption marks | 25 min |
| **Per set** | 4 rounds then a long break; optionally first round of a set recaps and last reviews | ~2 h |
| **Daily** | Planning round, recording round | 2 rounds |
| **Weekly** | Sum rounds per day and per type (work vs exploration); count `'` and `-`; compare estimates. Ask: which set is my most productive? | 15–30 min |
| **Monthly** | Look at estimate accuracy trend: goal is "the number of cases of underestimation is equal to the number of cases of overestimation"; drop metrics you don't use | 20 min |
| **Seasonal** | "When the seasons change, your timetable needs to change too." Re-plan sets and start/end times | 15 min |
| **Yearly** | Archive Records sheets; note average rounds per day and main interruption sources | 30 min |
| **After a break (holiday, illness)** | Restart gently: one or two rounds a day, building back to 10–12 | — |

**Migration:** unfinished To Do Today tasks go back to the Activity Inventory at the end of each day. Items that "lose their purpose over time" are deleted.

## 9. Page anatomy

### A. To Do Today (Cirillo's layout, with marks)

```
+------------------------------------------------------+
| Place ________   Date ________   Name ________       |
|                  TO DO TODAY                         |
| Available rounds: [ 8 ]                              |
+------------------------------------------------------+
| Task (in priority order)          | ' - | rounds     |
|-----------------------------------+-----+------------|
| Answer ch. 4 questions            |     | [X][X]     |
| Repeat laws out loud              | '   | [X][X][ ]  |
| Summarize laws in writing         | ''- | [X][X][X]X |
|                                   |     |            |
|                                   |     |            |
+------------------------------------------------------+
|              UNPLANNED & URGENT                      |
| [15:40] Email draft to Luke       |     | [X]        |
| Order food                        |     |            |
+------------------------------------------------------+
```

- Task column ~60% of width; interruption column ~10%; boxes ~30%.
- Boxes about 5 mm square, 1 mm apart. Up to 7 per row (the break-down rule caps it).
- Unplanned & Urgent takes the bottom quarter.

### B. Activity Inventory

```
+------------------------------------------------------+
| ACTIVITY INVENTORY              Name ________         |
+------+-------------------------------------+----+----+
| U/dl | Activity                            |Est | ✓  |
+------+-------------------------------------+----+----+
|      | Answer ch. 4 questions              | 2  |    |
|      | Summarize laws in writing           | 3  |    |

|      | Call Mark: laptop back              |  ↑ |    |
| U    | Call Carol: next concert?           |    |    |
| U[14]| Book interview with Maestro Neri    | 1  |    |
+------+-------------------------------------+----+----+
```

- Left margin ~12% for `U` and bracketed deadline.
- Right: estimate and done tick. "+1" with an arrow groups small tasks into one round.

```

```


- Fits in a 5.5×8.5 day page in about 30 mm of height.
- Tally spots for `'` and `-` are optional.

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Who | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| **Original Pomodoro Technique** | Francesco Cirillo, paper 2006/2007, book 2018 | Full paper system: three sheets, marks, estimation, timetable |
| **"Just the timer" Pomodoro** | Popular apps and culture | 25/5 timer only. No sheets, no marks, no estimation. What most people mean today |
| **Flowtime Technique** | Zoë Read-Bivens (Medium, as "UrgentPigeon"), around 2015–2016 **(date varies by source)** | No fixed work length: work until you naturally stop, log start, end, interruptions and duration, then take a break sized to the work (Entrepreneur's summary: 5 min for under 25 min of work, 8 for 25–50, 10 for 50–90, 15 for over 90). Read-Bivens: "The timer is a tyrant." |
| **Short sprints (10/2, 15/3, 15/5)** | ADHD coaches and apps | Lowers the start barrier. Cirillo himself started with 10 minutes and suggests building up the non-stop time bit by bit |
| **Long rounds (50/10, 52/17, 90-minute blocks)** | Various; "52/17" came from a time-tracking app's data blog **(unverified)** | Longer focus for deep work. Cirillo's own ceiling is 40 minutes |
| **Pomodoro in pairs and teams** | Cirillo's companion paper *Applying the Pomodoro Technique in Teams*; the 2018 book | Shared timer; Person, Pair and Team Pomodoros counted separately |
| **Study-with-me / body doubling** | Online study communities | Someone else's visible timer and presence; video streams or co-working calls |
| **Pomodoro + interstitial journaling** | Community practice | Write a timestamped line in each break (see [interstitial-journaling.md](interstitial-journaling.md)) |
| **Tomato bullet journal spread** | Bullet journal community | A page of tomato icons or circles to colour per round; decorative |
| **Time-boxing** | Software practice (McConnell, 1996) | The parent idea: fix the time, flex the scope. Not tied to 25 minutes |

## 12. Community practice

What people actually do (from blogs, study communities, ADHD creators and app culture; observational):

- **The timer is everything.** Almost nobody uses the three sheets. The Records sheet and estimation are the least-used parts, though they're the parts Cirillo cares about most.
- **Length tinkering.** 25/5 is the default, but many people settle on 45/15 or 50/10 for deep work and 10–15 minutes for dreaded tasks.
- **Apps and extensions.** Timer apps with site blockers, statistics and streaks dominate. Some add "forest" or pet mechanics.
- **Body doubling and "study with me".** Long videos with visible Pomodoro timers and lo-fi music; co-working calls with shared breaks.
- **ADHD adaptations.** Shorter rounds to start, a visual (silent) timer, movement breaks, permission to keep going when in flow or stop early when fried. The standard "no working through the ring" rule is often dropped.
- **Paper trackers.** Bullet journal users draw rows of circles or tomatoes and colour them in. Minimalists use tally marks.
- **Aesthetic vs minimalist.** Aesthetic camp: tomato stickers, coloured-in trackers, cute timers. Minimalist camp: a dot per round in the margin. Cirillo sits with the minimalists: simple tools, explicit gestures, pencil and eraser.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Timer:** wind-up kitchen timer (original), a silent visual countdown (disc that shrinks), a watch with a vibrating timer, or an app. Cirillo's requirements: you start it on purpose, it shows time left, it signals the end clearly.
- **Pencil and eraser:** Cirillo's own recommendation, because estimates change.
- **Paper:** any. 80–100 gsm handles pencil and ballpoint. Grid or dot paper makes the boxes easy.
- **Ruler:** for ruling the sheets once, or print them.
- **Stamps:** a small square or circle stamp makes rows of boxes quickly (optional).
- **Stickers:** tomato stickers are a community habit, not part of the method.
- **Highlighter:** for crossing out finished tasks, if you like.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

**Apps and templates**

- Official web, Windows and PWA timers.
- Countless third-party timers: most offer 25/5 presets, custom lengths, stats and site blocking. Some integrate with task managers or calendars.
- Spreadsheet Records sheets (Cirillo suggests a spreadsheet only when paper gets too complex).
- Printable To Do Today and Activity Inventory templates.

**E-ink**

- reMarkable, Kindle Scribe, Boox: handwritten To Do Today templates with box columns; use a separate timer.
- An e-ink device can show a countdown only if it refreshes; frequent refresh on e-ink is slow and can flash. A once-a-minute partial refresh is the usual compromise. The X4 redraws rarely by design, so it suits *recording* rounds better than timing them (section 25b).

**Scanning**

- Boxes with X marks are very machine-readable. `'` and `-` tallies are readable if they sit in a fixed spot.

**Lost or gained vs paper**

| Paper | Digital |
|---|---|
| Explicit gestures (wind, mark, cross out) that Cirillo says matter | Automatic counts, charts, history |
| Pencil and eraser for changing estimates | Easy to change lengths |
| No screen during breaks | Phone timers pull you into the phone |
| Interruptions recorded by hand make you notice them | Site blockers can stop some interruptions |
| No streaks or badges | Gamification can motivate or add pressure |

## 16. Evidence and research

There's no trial of Cirillo's full system (sheets, marks, estimation). The research tests break schedules, mostly in students.

| Study | Sample and design | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Biwer, Wiradhany, Oude Egbrink and De Bruin (2023)**, *British Journal of Educational Psychology* 93(S2):353–367 | 87 Dutch university students, one real self-study session online. Self-regulated breaks (n = 35) vs 24 min work / 6 min break (n = 25) vs 12/3 (n = 27) | Self-regulated breakers studied longer between breaks and reported **more fatigue and distraction, less concentration and motivation**. **No difference in mental effort or task completion.** Authors: systematic breaks gave mood benefits and similar completion in less time | One day, small groups, self-report. Supports planned breaks for how studying *feels* |
| **Smits, Wenzel and De Bruin (2025)**, *Behavioral Sciences* 15(7):861 | 94 university students, one 2-hour real study session. Self-regulated (n = 25) vs Pomodoro 25/5 (n = 36) vs Flowtime (n = 33) | Pomodoro breaks led to a **faster rise in fatigue**; Pomodoro and Flowtime a faster drop in motivation than self-regulated breaks, but **no overall differences** in fatigue, motivation, productivity, task completion or flow | Contradicts part of Biwer (2023). Small, single session. Suggests no break style clearly wins |
| **Ogut (2025)**, *BMC Medical Education* (scoping review) | 32 studies, total N = 5,270: 3 RCTs, 5 quasi-experiments, 24 observational/comparative, some app-based | Across the 3 RCTs (n = 87), structured intervals had ~20% lower fatigue, better distractibility and motivation than self-paced breaks. 88% of studies positive; 57% used validated measures | A scoping review, not a meta-analysis. The "3 RCTs, n = 87" matches the Biwer study's conditions, so the RCT evidence may be one study **(checked against numbers; unverified in full text)**. Many included studies are low quality |
| **Albulescu et al. (2022)**, *PLOS ONE* 17(8):e0272460 | Meta-analysis of micro-breaks (≤10 min), 22 samples, N = 2,335 | Vigor up (d = .36), fatigue down (d = .35); performance effect not significant overall (d = .16); longer breaks linked to better performance; less benefit on highly demanding cognitive tasks | Good-quality synthesis. Supports the short break; doesn't test 25 minutes |
| **Ariga and Lleras (2011)**, *Cognition* 118(3):439–443 | 84 participants, 50-minute vigilance task | Two brief task switches prevented the usual performance drop | Supports brief breaks against "goal habituation". Lab vigilance task |
| **Mark, Gudith and Klocke (2008)**, CHI '08 | 48 participants, interrupted email task | Interrupted people worked faster with no loss of quality but reported more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort | Supports Cirillo's focus on interruptions as costly even when output looks fine |
| **Barkley (fact sheet)** | Clinical model of ADHD | ADHD is to time "what nearsightedness is to spatial vision"; help by "making time itself more externally represented" and breaking long projects into small daily work periods with immediate feedback | Theory and guidance. Supports visible timers and small chunks for ADHD; not a Pomodoro trial |

**ADHD-specific evidence:** a 2025 study of "Smart-Pomodoro", a wearable and app for children with ADHD (*International Journal of Human–Computer Interaction*), exists but its full text couldn't be opened for this doc **(unverified)**. No adult ADHD trials of the Pomodoro Technique were found. Clinician-reviewed advice (ChoosingTherapy, 2025/2026) recommends shorter intervals to start, silent visual timers if ticking is stressful, movement breaks and body doubling.

**Claimed vs supported**

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Planned short breaks reduce fatigue | **Supported** (micro-break meta-analysis; Biwer 2023) |
| Pomodoro breaks beat self-chosen breaks | **Mixed** (Biwer yes on mood; Smits no difference) |
| 25 minutes is the right length | **Not supported or tested**; Cirillo himself says 20–35 |
| Better focus and concentration | Supported for self-report in some studies |
| Higher productivity or task completion | **Not supported** in the two best trials (no difference) |
| Better estimation over time | Claimed by Cirillo; untested |
| Less procrastination | Claimed; widely reported; untested directly |
| Helps ADHD | Claimed; fits Barkley's model; no adult trials found |

## 17. Benefits

- Short planned breaks lower fatigue and raise vigor — **evidenced** (micro-breaks)
- Planned breaks feel better than pushing through (concentration, motivation) — **evidenced, mixed** (Biwer yes, Smits no difference overall)
- Easier to start dreaded tasks ("just one round") — **claimed**
- Visible, countable progress (a row of X's) — **claimed**; fits the progress principle
- Interruptions become visible and schedulable — **claimed**
- Better time estimates with practice — **claimed** (Cirillo)
- A sustainable end to the day; no heroic overtime — **claimed**
- Less time anxiety ("becoming") — **claimed**
- External, physical time for time-blind brains — **claimed**; consistent with Barkley's model
- Cheap and simple — true by design

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The ring breaks flow | 25 minutes is arbitrary for deep work | Use longer rounds (up to Cirillo's 40), or Flowtime: work until you stop, then break for a set fraction |
| Ring anxiety | Feeling watched or rushed; results-oriented people hear "am I fast enough?" | Silent visual timer; remember the goal is observation, not speed; Cirillo: "the first objective… is simply to mark down the Pomodoros you've done" |
| Can't finish a single round | Constant internal interruptions | Cirillo's own fix: set 25, but aim only to work non-stop a little longer each time: "no less than 10 minutes, maybe even just one minute more" |
| Voiding rounds feels punishing | The indivisible rule | On paper, mark a void round with `/` instead of nothing, and keep going. "The next Pomodoro will go better" |
| Breaks turn into phone time | The phone is the timer | Use a separate timer; keep the phone in another room; break = move or drink water |
| Breaks run long | Nothing ends the break | Set the timer for the break too (Cirillo's beginner tip for long breaks) |
| Over-planning | Planning more rounds than exist | Available Pomodoros = last week's real average |
| Streak pressure | App gamification | Paper; no streaks; zero-round days are just days |
| Work that can't be timed | Care, outdoor shifts, customer-facing | Use rounds only for the desk or admin part of the day, or count "rounds" loosely as task chunks |
| Only using the timer | The sheets feel like homework | Keep just one mark: an X per round. Add interruption ticks when curious |
| Estimation feels like judgment | Diff numbers look like failure | Aim for balance, not zero: equal over- and underestimates is Cirillo's sign of improvement |
| Working past the timetable | "Five More Minutes Syndrome", guilt | A hard end time; overtime no more than five days with recovery after (Cirillo) |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** Start with 10–15 minute rounds (Cirillo started with 10). Use a silent visual timer so time is *visible* (Barkley's "externalize time"). Allow "flowtime" rounds: if you're in flow at the ring, you may continue and take a longer break after, logged as one long round. Movement breaks. Body doubling (a friend on a call, a study-with-me video). Keep the rule "the next one will go better" and drop the voiding rule if it causes shame. Never track streaks. Hyperfocus warning: set a hard stop timer for meals and meds.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** Ticking and ringing can be painful: use a silent timer, vibration or a slowly shrinking visual disc. Predictable round length is often a relief. Transitions at the ring can be hard: allow a 1-minute wind-down to finish a thought and write a "resume" note.
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** Shorter rounds, longer breaks, smaller sets (Cirillo: when "especially tired… organize shorter sets (for example three Pomodoros) and take longer breaks"). Count a round as a spoon or half a spoon. Lying-down breaks count. Flare days: one round is a full day.
- **Depression:** One round is enough. Pick the smallest possible task. Mark every round, even short ones. Don't compare to other days.
- **Anxiety:** The method was designed against time anxiety, but a countdown can also feed it. Try counting *up* (a stopwatch) instead of down, or cover the timer and use a gentle chime. Use Unplanned & Urgent to park worries.
- **Trauma:** Sudden alarms can startle. Use a soft chime or vibration. Keep interruption tallies private and non-judgmental.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** Boxes and ticks instead of words. Short task names or icons. Records sheet optional; a dot per round on a month page is enough.
- **Low vision:** Large-format visual timer; bold boxes; tactile timers (wind-up dials can be felt); a talking timer.
- **Motor or hand pain:** Stamps or pre-printed boxes; one tap to mark; skip the Records sheet; a physical button device to count rounds (see X4).
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** Useful for admin that's emotionally heavy (name-change forms, insurance appeals, HRT paperwork): one round at a time, with a comforting break planned. Keep sensitive task names vague on scanned pages.
- **Shift workers:** Use rounds for off-shift admin and study only. Build the timetable around the shift, not a 9-to-5. After a night shift, shorter sets.
- **Low income:** Free: any clock and scrap paper. The official eSheets and timers are free; no app needed.

## 21. Combining

- **Interstitial journaling** ([interstitial-journaling.md](interstitial-journaling.md)): a line in each break (what I did, how I feel, what's next). The two together make a complete paper record: rounds for effort, entries for context.
- **Bullet Journal** ([bullet-journal.md](bullet-journal.md)): add boxes after tasks in the daily log; `'` and `-` fit alongside BuJo signifiers without clashing (BuJo uses `•`, `-` for notes, so use a different interruption mark, for example a small tick above the line).
- **Time blocking** ([time-block-planner.md](time-block-planner.md)): block the day into sets; count rounds inside each block.
- **Emergent Task Planner** ([emergent-task-planner.md](emergent-task-planner.md)): its time bubbles are close to estimation boxes.
- **Ivy Lee / Top 3** ([ivy-lee-method.md](ivy-lee-method.md)): the six (or three) tasks become To Do Today in priority order.
- **Eisenhower Matrix** ([eisenhower-matrix.md](eisenhower-matrix.md)): sort the Unplanned & Urgent list at the long break; most of it isn't urgent.
- **Getting Things Done** ([getting-things-done-paper.md](getting-things-done-paper.md)): the Activity Inventory is close to a next-actions list; the Unplanned mark is a quick capture.
- **Full Focus Planner** ([full-focus-planner.md](full-focus-planner.md)): rounds for the Big 3.
- **Hobonichi / Jibun Techo** ([hobonichi-techo.md](hobonichi-techo.md), [jibun-techo.md](jibun-techo.md)): shade half-hour slots on their time axes as rounds.
- **Morning Pages** ([morning-pages.md](morning-pages.md)): a brain dump before the first round clears internal interruptions.
- **Five Minute Journal** ([five-minute-journal.md](five-minute-journal.md)): morning intent sets the day's tasks; evening prompts sit beside the Records.

## 23. Glossary

- **Pomodoro:** Italian for tomato; Cirillo's word for his kitchen timer and for one 25-minute round of work (plus its break). Registered trademark.
- **Round / focus round:** neutral word for one Pomodoro.
- **Set:** a group of (usually) four rounds, followed by a long break.
- **Short break:** 3–5 minutes between rounds.
- **Long break:** 15–30 minutes after a set.
- **To Do Today sheet:** the daily plan with marks.
- **Unplanned & Urgent:** the section of To Do Today for things that came up and can't wait.
- **Activity Inventory:** the master list of possible activities, with estimates.
- **Records sheet:** the end-of-day log of effort and estimates.
- **Internal interruption (`'`):** you interrupt yourself.
- **External interruption (`-`):** someone or something else interrupts you.
- **Inform, Negotiate, Call back:** Cirillo's script for external interruptions.
- **Protect the Pomodoro:** handle interruptions without breaking the round.
- **Void:** a round that was broken and doesn't count.
- **Available Pomodoros:** the number of rounds you can really work today.
- **Estimate:** predicted rounds for a task, drawn as empty boxes.
- **Second / third estimate:** a re-estimate drawn in a different color or shape.
- **Quantitative estimation error:** estimate higher (overestimation) or lower (underestimation) than actual effort.
- **Qualitative estimation error:** missing activities in the plan (shown by many Unplanned items).
- **Time-boxing:** fixing the time for a set of activities; unfinished work moves to the next box.
- **Becoming:** Cirillo's (after Bergson) term for abstract, passing time that causes anxiety.
- **Succession of events:** concrete, ordered time, which feels calmer.
- **Five More Minutes Syndrome:** overrunning the timetable out of guilt.
- **Overlearning:** reviewing a finished task until the round rings.
- **Flowtime:** Zoë Read-Bivens's variant with no fixed work length and breaks sized to the work.
- **Micro-break:** a break of 10 minutes or less.
- **Body doubling:** working alongside someone for gentle accountability.

## 24. FAQ

**1. Do I have to use 25 minutes?**
No. Cirillo's own range is 20–35 minutes, 40 at most, with 25+5 as the tested default. For hard starts, 10–15 minutes is fine; he began with 10.

**2. What if I'm in flow when the timer rings?**
Cirillo says stop, because the break helps you integrate and the next round goes better. Many people (and the Flowtime variant) keep going and take a longer break after. Research doesn't settle it.

**3. What if someone interrupts me?**
Mark `-`, tell them you're busy, agree when you'll get back to them, write it down, and keep going. If you must stop, the round is void; take a break and start a new one.

**4. Is it proven to work?**
Short planned breaks help fatigue and mood. The best student trials found Pomodoro breaks about equal to self-chosen breaks on output. The full paper system hasn't been studied.

**5. Do I need the three sheets?**
No, but they're the actual technique. At minimum, keep a mark per round. Add interruption ticks and estimates when you want to learn more about how you work.

**6. Is it good for ADHD?**
Many ADHD people find short, visible rounds helpful for starting. Adjust length, use a silent visual timer, use movement breaks, and drop rules that cause shame. No adult ADHD trials were found.

**7. What should I do in a break?**
Move, drink water, breathe, look outside. Avoid work talk, email and phones in short breaks; save email for long breaks.

**8. Can I use it at a job without a desk?**
Mostly for the desk or admin parts of life: forms, study, planning, bills. Outdoor or care work runs on its own clock.

**9. Why paper instead of an app?**
Cirillo says explicit gestures (winding, marking, crossing out) matter and paper keeps complexity low. Apps are fine if they don't pull you into the phone.

**10. What does "add it up" mean?**
Tasks under one round (quick emails, calls) get grouped until they fill one round.

### e. Don't adopt

- **Ticking or ringing** on the X4 or in any Keeping Watch tool: sound is a notification in practice and hard on sensory needs.
- **Tomato imagery**: decorative, adds ornament, and leans on the trademark.
- **The word "Pomodoro" as a block name**: registered trademark; use "Focus rounds".
- **The "void round" rule as printed instruction**: it can turn one interruption into shame. The book should say any round counts.
- **Streaks, targets and daily totals shown on the sleep screen**: badges and pressure.
- **The full Records sheet in every month**: too dense for a calm book; offer only in back matter if at all.
- **Estimation as default**: estimates are for people who want them. Boxes can be read as "planned rounds" or ignored.
- **"No working past the ring" as a hard rule**: many ADHD users do better with flexible stops.

## 26. Open questions

- Is 25 minutes better than other lengths for anyone in particular (for example ADHD adults, autistic adults, people with chronic fatigue)? No direct trials found.
- Do the **sheets** (interruption marks, estimation, records) add benefit beyond the timer? Never tested.
- Why did Biwer (2023) find mood benefits for systematic breaks but Smits (2025) didn't? Different lengths (24/6 vs 25/5), different samples, one session each.
- Does Cirillo's 2018 book change any rules or symbols from the 2007 paper (for example the second and third estimate shapes)? Not checked in the full book.
- Full text of the Smart-Pomodoro ADHD study (2025) couldn't be opened.
- Could a silent round timer work on the X4 without breaking its battery life and calm redraw rhythm? Needs a hardware test.
- Where the "52/17" rule came from and whether its data holds up.
