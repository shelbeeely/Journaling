# The Five Minute Journal

Intelligent Change's guided gratitude journal (2013), created by Alex Ikonn and UJ Ramdas: five short prompts, three in the morning and two at night, plus a daily quote and weekly challenges. Also covers the app and the research on gratitude interventions, with honest effect sizes.

Sources checked 2026-09-28. Uncertain points are marked **(unsure)**. Prompt wording has changed between printings; the classic wording is used here and noted where the current edition differs.

## 2. Summary

The Five Minute Journal (5MJ) is a book of pre-printed daily pages with five prompts. In the morning you list three things you're grateful for, three things that would make today great, and a daily affirmation ("I am…"). At night you list three amazing things that happened and one way today could have been better. Each page carries an inspirational quote, and each week adds a small challenge. UJ Ramdas and Alex Ikonn launched it in Toronto in 2013; after Tim Ferriss ordered 2,500 copies it became the best-known "gratitude journal," with 3+ million copies sold (Intelligent Change's figure). It's sold as "science-backed." That's partly fair: gratitude interventions have been tested in well over a hundred studies, and the best recent meta-analysis (Choi et al. 2025, 145 papers, 24,804 people) finds a small overall boost in well-being, Hedges' g = 0.19. Against depression and anxiety the effect is small (g ≈ −0.29 at post-test) and shrinks against good comparison activities. Positive "I am" affirmations have less support and can backfire for people with low self-esteem. The format is a well-designed, low-effort habit; it isn't a treatment.

### Cultural context

The 5MJ arrived as positive psychology hit the mainstream: Seligman's "three good things" (2005), Emmons's gratitude studies (2003), and the "lifehacking" and morning-routine culture around Tim Ferriss. It's credited (by Intelligent Change) with starting the "guided journaling movement": pre-printed prompt books that remove the blank page. It led to many imitators (The Six-Minute Diary, countless gratitude journals and printable templates). Its critics come from two sides: people who find it too positive and shallow, and researchers who note that "science-backed" overstates small effects.

## 4. Philosophy and principles

**Stated goals:** be happier, more grateful and more intentional in five minutes a day. The company's mission is to help 1% of the world's population "create positive change."

**Core beliefs:**

1. **Structure beats willpower.** A prompt removes the blank-page problem. You don't decide what to write, only how to answer.
2. **Bookend the day.** A morning section sets attention; an evening section reviews it.
3. **Small and specific.** Tiny great things and tiny improvements, not big goals.
4. **Gratitude trains attention.** Looking for good things each day makes you notice more of them.
5. **Identity.** "I am…" affirmations are meant to help you become who you want to be.
6. **Growth mindset.** "How could today have been better?" is about learning, not blame.

**Problem it solves:** people who want the benefits of journaling but won't write pages. The 5MJ asks for about 12 short lines a day.

## 5. Core components

| Element | When | Classic wording | Count |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily quote | Top of the day | A printed quote (different each day) | 1 |
| Gratitude | Morning | "I am grateful for…" | 3 |
| Intentions | Morning | "What would make today great?" | 3 |
| Affirmation | Morning | "Daily affirmations. I am…" | 1–2 |
| Amazing things | Evening | "3 amazing things that happened today…" | 3 |
| Reflection | Evening | "How could I have made today even better?" | 1–2 |
| Weekly challenge | Once a week | A small task or reflection | 1 |
| Introduction | Front of book | How and why to use it; the "science" | Once |

The current product page describes the evening as "highlights of the day" and a "lesson learned" **(the exact printed wording in current editions is unsure; the product pages checked didn't quote it)**.

### Daily quote

A short quote from a well-known figure at the top of each day's spread. It's a prompt to think, not a task.

### Gratitude (3)

Three things you're grateful for. Intelligent Change's tips: be **specific** ("the warm coffee Sam made me" rather than "coffee") and **vary** your answers to keep them fresh. When stuck, use Tim Ferriss's categories: a relationship, an opportunity you have today, something great from yesterday, a simple object nearby.

### What would make today great? (3)

Three things within your control that would make the day good. The guidance is "tiny great things": actions ("take a real lunch break"), not outcomes you can't control ("get the promotion"). This is close to setting an intention or implementation plan.

### Daily affirmation

"I am…" statements about who you want to be. Intelligent Change suggests two styles: the **Hammer** (the same affirmation every day) and the **Butterfly** (a new one each day), and writing at "the edge of your current reality," not a fantasy. See section 16 for the risk with low self-esteem.

### 3 amazing things

Three good things from the day. The tips say to look past the obvious, even on bad days, and be specific.

### How could I have made today even better?

One or two small, actionable changes. Framed as a growth mindset: "I'd have gone to bed at 10," not "I was lazy."

### Weekly challenges

Short challenges that "encourage you to reflect on your values, enjoy simple pleasures of life, and take actions beyond your comfort zone" (product page). They're printed between weeks.

## 6. Setting it up

### First setup

1. **Get a journal** (official or any notebook) and put it where you'll see it on waking and at bedtime. Intelligent Change's first tip: near the bed or near the toothbrush.
2. **Pen clipped to it.**
3. **Read the introduction** (if you have the book), or the five prompts in section 5.
4. **Write your "why"** on the first page: one sentence on why you're doing this (their tip 10).
5. **Pick an affirmation style:** Hammer or Butterfly.
6. **Link each half to an existing habit:** morning after coffee or teeth; evening after brushing teeth.

### First day

- Morning (2–3 min): date, read the quote, three gratitudes, three "great" items, one "I am."
- Evening (2–3 min): three amazing things, one "even better."
- Check the morning list at night: did any "great" item happen? No scoring.

### First week

- Expect repeat gratitudes by day 3 ("family, health, coffee"). Switch to specific: *which* moment.
- Keep "great" items tiny. If none happen, they were too big.
- Do the weekly challenge at the weekend.
- If you miss a half, leave it blank and carry on.

## 7. Daily practice

| Step | Time | Prompt | Tips |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Morning, 0:00 | Date; read the quote | Don't answer it, just read |
| 2 | 0:20 | I am grateful for… (3) | Specific; one person, one small thing, one about you |
| 3 | 1:00 | What would make today great? (3) | Actions you control; could all be done by 3 p.m. |
| 4 | 1:45 | Daily affirmation: I am… | Present tense, believable |
| 5 | Evening, 0:00 | 3 amazing things that happened today | Scan the day hour by hour if stuck |
| 6 | 1:00 | How could I have made today even better? | One behaviour, no blame |
| 7 | 1:30 | Optional: tick which "great" items happened | Not in the official format; a common custom |

**Order of operations:** gratitude comes first to set a positive frame before planning. The evening section is meant to be the last writing of the day.

## 8. Rhythms

| Rhythm | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Daily** | Morning three prompts; evening two prompts |
| **Weekly** | A printed weekly challenge. Common custom: reread the week's "amazing things" and "even better" lines, notice repeats |
| **Monthly** | Nothing official. Common: pick the best "amazing thing" of the month; review affirmations |
| **6 months** | The book runs out (undated, about 6 months). Start a new one; some people reread the old one first |
| **Yearly** | Nothing official. People keep old volumes as a record |

**Archiving:** the books are the archive. The app has "On this day" look-backs (premium).

**No migration or index:** each day stands alone.

## 10. Worked examples

### Sample day

```
Quote: "The best way out is always through." - Robert Frost
Tue 14 Oct

I am grateful for...
1. Mara answering my text in five minutes with a heart
2. The heater in the work truck actually working
3. That my hip only aches instead of hurts

What would make today great?
1. Eat a real lunch sitting down
2. Leave the site by 3:40 and catch the 4:05 bus
3. Put 15 min into the zine drawings tonight

Daily affirmation. I am...
someone who keeps small promises to herself.

---

3 amazing things that happened today
1. Crew lead said the fence line looked clean
2. Rain held off until I was on the bus
3. Drew two bus stops; one is good

How could I have made today even better?
Packed lunch last night so I wasn't stuck with gas-station food.
```

## 12. Community practice

**Where people talk about it:** r/Journaling, r/productivity, r/getdisciplined, YouTube reviews ("I used the Five Minute Journal for a year"), Tim Ferriss's podcast and blog audiences, Amazon reviews.

**What people actually do:**

- Many do the morning half and skip the evening, or the other way round.
- Many copy the prompts into a cheaper notebook or bullet journal instead of buying the book.
- Common drift: gratitudes become the same three words every day. Fix: specificity, or Ferriss's four categories.
- Affirmations are the most skipped and most disliked prompt. Some replace them with an intention ("Today I'll…") or a values line.
- Some add a mood or sleep line, or a tick for which "great" items happened.
- Reported benefits: noticing more good things, a calmer start, a feeling of closure at night. Reported downsides: feels shallow or forced after a few months, guilt about gaps, "toxic positivity" on bad days.
- App users like reminders and photos; some long-time users dislike the move of older features behind a subscription (App Store reviews).

**Aesthetic vs minimalist:** the book itself is minimalist (linen, one typeface, lots of white space). The aesthetic camp decorates DIY versions with stickers and lettering in bullet journals; the minimalist camp uses a one-line-per-prompt version in any notebook.

## 16. Evidence and research

### Gratitude interventions

| Study | Sample | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emmons & McCullough 2003, *J Personality and Social Psychology* | 3 studies: students (weekly lists, then daily lists) and adults with neuromuscular disease (21 days) **(durations from the paper, unsure)** | Gratitude listing vs hassles or neutral/social comparison: higher well-being on "several, though not all" outcomes. Positive affect was the most robust finding | Classic and well designed for its time; small samples; some outcomes self-reported; comparison groups (hassles) may make gratitude look better |
| Seligman, Steen, Park & Peterson 2005, *American Psychologist* | Internet study, 6 groups incl. placebo | 3 of 5 exercises lastingly increased happiness and cut depressive symptoms; "three good things" (with why) was one **(which three: from the paper, unsure)** | Randomised, placebo-controlled; self-selected online sample; high dropout typical of such studies |
| Froh, Sefick & Emmons 2008, *J School Psychology* | 221 early adolescents, 11 classes | Counting blessings raised gratitude, optimism, life satisfaction; lowered negative affect; strong link to school satisfaction | Class-level randomisation; short follow-up (3 weeks) |
| Wood, Froh & Geraghty 2010, *Clinical Psychology Review* | Review | Gratitude strongly related to well-being; interventions "promising" but the field "may have neglected current limitations," especially weak control groups | Influential critical review |
| Davis et al. 2016, *J Counseling Psychology* | Meta-analysis, k = 4–18 per comparison | vs measurement-only: well-being d = .31. vs alternative activity: well-being d = .17, gratitude d = .46, anxiety d = .11 (n.s.). vs a psychologically active comparison: d = −.03 (no better) | Careful about control types; small k for some comparisons |
| Dickens 2017, *Basic and Applied Social Psychology* | 38 studies, 282 effect sizes | Small to medium effects on happiness, life satisfaction and positive affect; small effect on depression (d ≈ 0.13 vs waitlist) **(figures from secondary summaries; the paper's page could not be opened)** | Broad; not checked directly |
| Cregg & Cheavens 2021, *J Happiness Studies* | 27 studies, N = 3,675 | Depression and anxiety: g = −0.29 at post-test, −0.23 at follow-up. Larger vs waitlist than vs active controls. Authors: effects are "relatively modest"; people seeking relief from depression or anxiety should use treatments with stronger evidence | Strongest test for mental health outcomes; clear honest conclusion |
| Diniz et al. 2023, *Einstein (São Paulo)* | 64 RCTs | Gratitude interventions: more gratitude, better mental health, fewer anxiety and depression symptoms, more positive mood | Broad; the abstract gives no pooled effect sizes; smaller journal **(quality of included trials unsure)** |
| Choi, Cha, McCullough, Coles & Oishi 2025, *PNAS* | Preregistered; 145 papers, 163 samples, 727 effects, 24,804 people, 28 countries | Small overall increase in well-being, g = 0.19 (95% CI 0.15–0.22). Varies by country, no clear cultural explanation. Larger for positive affect, for combined gratitude activities, and in RCTs. Robust to publication bias | The best current estimate |

### Affirmations

| Study | Finding |
|---|---|
| Wood, Perunovic & Lee 2009, *Psychological Science* | Repeating "I'm a lovable person" made people **with low self-esteem feel worse**; high self-esteem people felt slightly better. Positive self-statements "backfire for the very people who 'need' them the most." |
| Cohen & Sherman 2014, *Annual Review of Psychology* | Self-affirmation research that works is **writing about your core values**, not repeating positive statements about yourself. Timely values affirmations improved some education, health and relationship outcomes |

### Intentions and reflection

"What would make today great?" resembles setting implementation intentions (specific if-then plans), which have good evidence for goal follow-through in general **(not tested in the 5MJ format; source not opened for this doc)**. The evening "even better" line resembles upward counterfactual thinking, which can help planning or feed self-criticism depending on tone **(unsure; general literature)**.

### Claimed vs supported

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| "Science-backed" | Partly. Gratitude lists are well studied; the 5MJ's exact mix (plus affirmations, plus intentions) is not |
| Makes you happier | Supported, small: g ≈ 0.19 on well-being overall |
| Reduces depression and anxiety | Small, and not better than other active activities; not a treatment |
| Affirmations help you become who you want to be | Weak; can backfire for low self-esteem |
| Writing down commitments makes you follow through | Plausible from goal-setting research; not tested here |
| Five minutes is enough | The gratitude studies used short tasks too; plausible |
| Daily is best | Unclear. Some research suggests doing it less often can help avoid habituation **(unsure; not checked)** |

### Limits

Many gratitude studies use students, short follow-ups and self-report. Effects look larger against waitlists than against real alternative activities, which suggests some of the benefit is from doing *any* structured reflective activity. The best evidence says: it helps a little, on average, for mood and well-being.

## 17. Benefits

- Small increases in well-being and positive mood (**evidenced**; g ≈ 0.19 overall).
- Small reductions in depression and anxiety symptoms (**evidenced**, small, not better than active alternatives).
- More optimism and life satisfaction in some studies, including teens (**evidenced**, some studies).
- A reliable habit because it's so short (**claimed**; plausible).
- More intentional days through the "great" list (**claimed**).
- Better sleep or health (**claimed**; one of Emmons's samples reported better sleep, not well replicated **(unsure)**).
- Felt sense of closure at night (**claimed**).
- Noticing patterns over a month (repeat gratitudes, repeat "even better" lines) (**claimed**).

## 18. Pitfalls

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Same three gratitudes every day | Be specific (a moment, not a category); use Ferriss's four categories; allow one or two instead of three |
| Feels fake on bad days | Allow neutral gratitudes ("the day is over"); let the evening say "hard day" |
| Affirmations feel like lies | Replace with a values line ("Today I care about…") or an intention; follow Wood et al.: don't force it |
| "Great" items never happen | They're too big. Make them 10-minute actions |
| "Even better" turns into self-criticism | Write one behaviour, not a trait. "Go to bed at 10," not "I'm lazy" |
| Missing the evening | Put the book on the pillow or next to the toothbrush |
| Guilt about gaps | Leave blanks; no catch-up |
| Gets boring after months | Change the prompts for a week (tip 9), skip days on purpose, or switch to weekly (see open questions) |
| The book costs $32 every six months | Copy the five prompts into any notebook |
| App privacy | Use paper, or check the app's privacy settings and what syncs |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** A great fit: tiny, structured, done fast. Keep the book in a place you can't miss; use a sticky note or the paper as the reminder. Allow one-word answers.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** Predictable structure helps. Literal prompts may confuse ("amazing" is strong; try "good" or "OK" things). Replace affirmations with facts ("I did X").
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** Do only one half; lower counts to one each. Gratitude can include rest and body small wins. Avoid "What would make today great?" on flare days; change it to "What's one kind thing I can do for myself?"
- **Depression and anxiety:** Gratitude helps a little on average, but forced positivity can make people feel worse or guilty. Cregg & Cheavens's advice stands: it isn't a replacement for treatment. Drop affirmations if you have low self-esteem (Wood et al. 2009). Allow "neutral" or "survived" entries.
- **Trauma:** "How could today have been better?" can become self-blame. Rephrase as "What would I like more of tomorrow?" Avoid gratitude that minimises harm ("grateful it wasn't worse").
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** Very little writing; one-word answers work; the app offers voice notes.
- **Low vision:** Large-print DIY page with thick lines; app with system text size; voice notes.
- **Motor or hand pain:** About 12 short lines a day; lower counts; use the app or voice.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** "I am…" affirmations can be powerful when they're about identity you're living ("I am a woman who…") and painful when they feel out of reach. Keep them believable, or use a gender-euphoria log ("a moment I felt like me") instead, which Journalwright Studio already has as a preset.
- **Shift workers:** "Morning" and "evening" mean start and end of *your* day. On night shifts, do the morning half before work.
- **Low income:** Free with any notebook; the official book is $32 per 6 months. The app's free tier covers the prompts.

## 21. Combining

- **With [Morning Pages](morning-pages.md):** pages to empty the head, then the three morning prompts to point the day. Or the 5MJ on busy days.
- **With the [Bullet Journal](bullet-journal.md):** put "what would make today great" at the top of the daily log as tasks; put "amazing things" as notes with a symbol.
- **With time blocking ([time-block-planner.md](time-block-planner.md)):** the three "great" items become the first blocks of the day.
- **With a [five-year diary](five-year-diary.md):** use the best "amazing thing" as the one line in the five-year book.
- **With the [Hobonichi Techo](hobonichi-techo.md) or [Jibun Techo](jibun-techo.md):** the five prompts fit in the daily page's top half.
- **With Journalwright Studio day pages:** most of the 5MJ already exists as blocks (Small good things, Top priorities, Lined notes, Went well). See section 25.

## 24. FAQ

**1. Do I need the official book?**
No. The five prompts work in any notebook. The book adds quotes, weekly challenges and a pleasant object.

**2. Does it really take five minutes?**
Roughly: 2–3 minutes each half.

**3. Is it science-backed?**
The gratitude parts are well studied and show small benefits. The affirmation part is weak. The exact 5MJ format hasn't been tested.

**4. How big is the effect?**
Small: about g = 0.19 on well-being in the largest meta-analysis. That's a real but modest change, similar to many light-touch activities.

**5. Will it help my depression?**
It may help a little, but it's not a treatment. The main meta-analysis on this recommends treatments with stronger evidence.

**6. What if affirmations make me feel worse?**
Drop them. Research shows they can backfire for people with low self-esteem. Try a values line or "a moment I felt like me."

**7. What if I miss a day or half a day?**
Leave it blank and carry on.

**8. My gratitudes are always the same. Is that a problem?**
It makes the exercise go stale. Be specific about moments, or rotate categories.

**9. Morning or evening: which half matters more?**
Unknown. Most gratitude research used evening or weekly lists. If you can only do one, pick the one you'll keep.

**10. Can I do it weekly instead?**
Yes. Some research suggests less frequent gratitude lists can work as well or better **(unsure)**.

**11. Is the app private?**
The App Store privacy label says it collects identifiers for tracking and links contact info and user content to you. Paper is private by default.

2. `good`: n 3 (Grateful for)

This is the target shape. ### e. Don't adopt

| Don't | Why |
|---|---|
| Printed "I am…" affirmation lines | Can backfire for low self-esteem (Wood et al. 2009); use a values line instead |
| "Amazing" as a label | Too strong for hard days; "good things" is calmer and more honest |
| A different quote every day | Visual noise; feed-like; On this day already exists |
| Streaks, badges or reminders on the e-ink companion | Out of scope; guilt on missed days |
| "Science-backed" claims in the book | Effects are small; say nothing rather than overclaim |
| "How could today have been better?" as the only evening question | Can tip into self-blame; pair it with Went well / Was hard |
| Photos, cloud sync, subscriptions | Privacy rules; nothing leaves the device except over its own hotspot or an explicit sync the user starts |

## 26. Open questions

- The exact printed wording of current editions' evening prompts ("highlights of the day," "lesson learned"?) couldn't be confirmed from the product pages.
- No study has tested the 5MJ format itself (gratitude + intentions + affirmation + review).
- Is daily gratitude better or worse than weekly for long-term users (habituation)? Research is thin.
- Why do effects vary by country (Choi et al. 2025 found variation but no explanation)?
- How much of the benefit is gratitude and how much is any short daily reflection (active-control results suggest a lot is the latter)?
- Sales numbers conflict between sources (2M, 3M+); both are company claims.
