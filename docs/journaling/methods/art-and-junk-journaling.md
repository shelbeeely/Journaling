# Art journaling and junk journaling

Art journals (also called visual journals) use paint, drawing, collage and handwriting on the same page. Junk journals are handmade books built from found paper and ephemera: envelopes, receipts, tickets, packaging. Sketchbook diaries record days in drawings. Sticker and washi tape culture overlaps with all three. This doc covers what each one is, where it came from, how people do it, what the research on art-making and art therapy does and doesn't show, and how it can sit next to Keeping Watch's structured, scanned pages.

Uncertain points are marked **(unverified)** or **(uncertain)**. All sources were checked on 2026-09-28.

## 2. Summary

Art journaling means making pages that mix pictures, colour and words about your life or feelings, without needing to be "good at art." Junk journaling is its thrift cousin: you build or fill a book from paper other people throw away, and the book fills up with the stuff of your days. Sketchbook diaries draw the day instead of writing it. Washi tape and stickers are the glue of the culture. The research on art therapy delivered by trained therapists shows moderate benefits for depression and anxiety in pooled trials, though trials are small, unblinded and varied, and a large UK trial for schizophrenia found no benefit. Studies on art-making outside therapy are smaller still: 45 minutes of art lowered cortisol in most people in one uncontrolled study, and colouring a structured pattern lowered anxiety more than colouring a blank page. The honest claim: making something with your hands can calm you and give you a way to record days when words won't come. It isn't treatment, and it doesn't need to be.

## 3. History and origin

### Timeline

| Year | Event |

| 14th–17th c. | Commonplace books: educated Europeans copy and collect "scraps" of information in bound blank books (see [commonplace book](./commonplace-book.md)) |
| 19th c. | Scrapbooks of mementos become a common household practice |

### Who's who

- **Teesha Moore**: mixed-media artist known for bright collage journal pages with "zetti" figures (stoic faces, pointy hats, striped legs), heavy black-and-white doodling and watercolour backgrounds. She sold stamps and collage sheets under the Zettiology name and, with Tracy Moore, ran Artfest retreats at Fort Worden near Port Townsend, Washington (from community write-ups; her own site was down when checked). A widely shared video tutorial shows her folding a 16-page journal from a single large sheet of watercolour paper **(unverified; video not opened)**.
- **Dina Wakley**: mixed-media artist and teacher, and a Ranger signature designer (paints, brushes, stamps). *Art Journal Freedom* teaches colour and composition (dominance, repetition, symmetry, contrast, "the power of black and white") so you can use or break the rules on purpose. A follow-up, *Art Journal Courage*, exists **(not opened)**.
- **Danny Gregory**: a New York writer and illustrator (his day job in advertising is widely reported but **unverified** here) who began drawing his days after a family crisis. He wrote that drawing had "a power to change my life and the world around me so profoundly." He later co-founded the Sketchbook Skool courses **(not opened)**.
- **Keri Smith**: author-illustrator of interactive books. *Wreck This Journal* asks you to poke holes, paint with coffee and deface pages, to get past the fear of ruining a nice book.
- **Kamoi Kakoshi**: an industrial tape maker in Kurashiki, Japan, that turned masking tape into decorative washi tape (mt), after three designers showed what it could do. Its goal: "a roll in every home."

### Cultural context

Art journaling grew from the mixed-media and rubber-stamp scene, craft magazines and retreats, then online classes and blogs. Junk journaling grew later, mostly on YouTube, with a strong thrift, vintage and "use what you have" ethic, though it also became a big market for printable ephemera and kits. Sketchbook diaries come from the urban-sketching and illustrated-journal tradition. Washi and stickers came from Japanese stationery culture and spread through planner communities. All of them treat the journal as an object, not just a record.

### Key quotes

- Junk journaling's common motto (quoted on Wikipedia): "there's only one rule in junk journalling: there are no rules!"
- Danny Gregory on drawing: it had "a power to change my life and the world around me so profoundly."
- Girija Kaimal (art therapy researcher) on her cortisol study: "that's the core idea in art therapy: Everyone is creative."
- mt's founding goal: "a roll in every home."

## 4. Philosophy and principles

**Stated goals**
- Express what words can't reach.
- Play without a goal; make "bad art" on purpose.
- Keep the physical traces of a life: tickets, wrappers, notes.
- Reuse and slow down.

**Core beliefs**
1. Everyone can make marks. Skill is optional.
2. Process over product. The page doesn't need to be good.
3. There are no rules, but knowing a few (Wakley's colour and composition) gives you more freedom, not less.
4. Mess is allowed. *Wreck This Journal* exists to break the fear of the precious page.
5. Materials carry memory. A bus transfer says more than "took the bus."

**The problem it solves**: "I can't find the words," "I want to make something that isn't work," "I have a drawer of tickets and cards I want to keep," and "writing journals feel like homework."

## 5. Core components

| Element | What it is |
|---|---|
| Background layer | Paint, gesso, tea stain, patterned paper, or a scrap of printed text |
| Collage | Cut or torn images and paper glued down |
| Ephemera | Found paper objects: tickets, receipts, labels, envelopes, seed packets |
| Focal image | One thing the page is "about": a drawing, photo, or cut-out |
| Journaling | Words: a few lines, a list, a word written large |
| Mark-making | Doodles, stamps, stencils, lines, outlining |
| Pockets and tuck spots | Envelopes, belly bands, flaps for loose items |
| Tape and stickers | Washi, sticker sheets, labels |
| Signatures | Folded stacks of paper sewn into a book (junk journals) |
| Prompts | A word, a theme, or a challenge to start from |
| Sketch | A drawing from life: your meal, your boots, the view from the bus |

### Background layer
Removes the blank-page fear. Wipe on leftover paint, glue down a page of an old book, or scribble with a crayon. Black-and-white option: pencil hatching, newspaper text, or plain collage.

### Collage
Tear rather than cut for a softer look and less fine motor effort. A glue stick is enough for paper; heavier items need matte medium or tape.

### Ephemera
The core of junk journaling. Wikipedia lists letters, packaging and junk mail; community lists add book pages, tags, envelopes, tea bags and library cards. Privacy: remove names, addresses, barcodes and account numbers from receipts and envelopes before gluing them in.

### Focal image
Wakley's composition idea of dominance: one thing should be biggest, darkest or most central. It gives the eye somewhere to land.

### Journaling
Short is fine: one word, a list of three, a date and a place. Some people hide writing under a flap or write it and paint over it (private journaling).

### Mark-making
Teesha Moore's pages use lots of black-and-white outlining and doodling to unify mixed pieces. For low-energy days, one line drawn around the edge of everything counts.

### Pockets
An envelope glued in half makes a pocket. Belly bands (a strip around a signature) hold loose tags.

### Tape and stickers
Washi is low-tack paper tape: it can be lifted and moved, and it's easy to tear by hand. Stickers are the fastest decoration there is.

### Signatures
A junk journal is often 3–6 signatures of 4–6 folded sheets, sewn with a pamphlet stitch into a cover of cardboard or cloth.

### Prompts
See the bank in section 22.

### Sketch
Draw what's in front of you for 5 minutes. Gregory's approach: draw the ordinary things (dishes, the street) and the day becomes worth noticing.

## 6. Setting it up

**First setup (20–30 minutes)**
1. Pick a book. Any sketchbook with paper at least 120 gsm (heavier if you'll paint). Or make one: fold 5 sheets of printer paper in half, nest them, and staple or stitch the fold.
2. Gather a "go kit" small enough to carry: glue stick, small scissors, a black pen, a pencil, one roll of tape.
3. Start a paper stash: an envelope or box for tickets, wrappers, cards and anything with a nice pattern.
4. Ruin the first page on purpose: scribble, spill tea, glue something crooked. Now nothing is precious.

**First day**
- Glue one thing from today onto a page. Write the date and one line. Done.

**First week**
- Day 2: a background only (paint, paper, or pencil hatching).
- Day 3: add one image on top of a background.
- Day 4: a 5-minute sketch of something on the table.
- Day 5: a list page: three things you saw.
- Day 6: a pocket page for the week's tickets.
- Day 7: look back. Which one felt best to make? Do more of that.

## 7. Daily practice

A realistic practice is 3–5 short touches a week, plus an occasional long session.

**Short touch (2–10 minutes)**
1. Pick up one thing from today (receipt, leaf, wrapper).
2. Glue or tape it down.
3. Write the date and 1–3 words.
4. Optional: draw a box around it or outline it.

**Long session (30–60 minutes)**
1. Background (5–10 min). Let it dry.
2. Collage layer (10–15 min): pick from your stash.
3. Focal image (5–10 min).
4. Words (5 min).
5. Marks and outlining (5–10 min).
6. Stop before it feels "done." Leave room.

**Prompts to start**
- What colour was today?
- What did I touch most today?
- One thing I want to keep from this week.

## 8. Rhythms

- **Weekly:** a pocket page or envelope for the week's paper. Once a week, glue in the best one or two.
- **Monthly:** a cover or title page for the month; flip back through; write one line on what the pages show.
- **Quarterly:** clear the stash box; recycle what didn't make it in. Sew or bind a new signature if you're making your own.
- **Yearly:** date the spine; store finished books flat and dry. Photograph favourite pages if you want a digital backup.
- **Archiving:** glue sticks can fail over years; PVA or matte medium lasts longer. Acidic paper yellows. Keep journals out of sun and damp.

## 9. Page anatomy

### A. Art journal spread (two pages)

```

|  background: wash or paper  |                             |
|   +---------+               |      +------------------+   |
|   | collage |   ~~~~~~~     |      |  FOCAL IMAGE     |   |
|   | scrap   |   marks       |      |  (drawing/photo) |   |
|   +---------+               |      +------------------+   |
|                             |   ONE BIG WORD              |
|   journaling lines:         |                             |
```

```
+------------------------------------------+
| DATE ___  TITLE ___________  TAGS ___    |  <- fixed scan header
|------------------------------------------|
| moon/sun | events | care check-in        |
|------------------------------------------|

```
The art stays inside the frame. Scanning still works because the header, frame, strip and code stay clear. Keep tape and glue off those zones.

## 10. Worked examples

### Sample day: short touch (5 minutes)

```
Page: Sketch box on today's day page
- Taped in: bus transfer (route 33), corner of a granola wrapper.
- Drew: a thumbnail of the storm clouds from the site at lunch.
- Words: "cold hands, good crew."
- Outline around all of it in black pen.
```

### Sample week (junk journal, 6 sessions)

```
MON  tea-stained background on 2 pages (3 min)
TUE  glued pharmacy bag strip (name cut off) + "refill day" (4 min)
WED  nothing (tired)
THU  sketch of lunch, 5 min
FRI  envelope pocket for the week's receipts (names/numbers blacked out)
SAT  long session: collage of a seed catalog + a walk map; wrote 6 lines (45 min)
SUN  looked back; taped a leaf from Saturday; wrote "more walks"
```

### Sample month (one art journal)

```
Week 1  Month title page: "OCTOBER" in torn letters from magazines
Week 2  Two spreads: first frost; a hard day painted solid grey with one yellow line
Week 3  Glue-book rows for 7 days; sticker for each day I went outside
Week 4  A "what I kept" pocket page; a sketch of the new jacket
End     One line on the last page: "Mostly grey, some yellow. The yellow was outside."
```

## 12. Community practice

- **YouTube "flip-throughs" and "journal with me"** videos are the main teaching form for junk journals (general observation).
- **Kits and printables:** many junk journalers buy printable ephemera sheets from Etsy sellers instead of collecting their own. This is convenient, and it also turns a thrift practice into a shopping one.
- **Swaps and pen pals:** people trade ephemera by post.
- **Common customisations:** tea- or coffee-dyed paper, inked edges for an aged look, pockets, belly bands, tuck spots, fabric covers, die cuts.
- **Aesthetic camps:** "vintage" (sepia, lace, old maps), "cottagecore," "grunge," bright mixed media (Moore, Wakley), and clean minimalist sketchbooks.
- **Minimalist camp:** a black pen, one sketch a day, nothing else.
- **Tension:** supply culture ("stash") versus use-what-you-have. Many people say buying supplies started to replace using them.

## 14. Tools and supplies

**Minimum kit ($0–$10)**
- Glue stick (acid-free if possible), scissors, one black pen, pencil.
- Found paper: envelopes (the patterned insides are "security patterns"), old books, maps, packaging.

**Add as you go**
- Washi tape: low-tack, hand-tearable, repositionable.
- Stickers and labels.
- Gel pens (white shows up on dark paper).
- Watercolour pan set or cheap acrylics; gesso to prep thin paper.
- Stamps and an ink pad.
- Matte medium or PVA for heavy pieces.

**Paper weight and bleed**
- 70–90 gsm printer paper: fine for collage and pen, buckles with paint.
- 120–160 gsm: sketch paper; light wash OK.
- 180–300 gsm: mixed media and watercolour.
- Keeping Watch's printed books are thin KDP paper: dry media only (pencil, pen, crayon), tape and glue stick. No paint or heavy layers on day pages; they'll show through and cockle.

**Rulers and cutters**
- A metal ruler and a craft knife for straight edges; a cutting mat.
- Hand-tearing needs no tools and hurts less.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps:** Procreate and other drawing apps; digital scrapbook kits (PNG stickers, washi). Instagram and Pinterest are where many people share pages.
- **E-ink:** reMarkable, Kindle Scribe and Boox handle line drawing and sketches well, but not colour or collage. Good for a sketchbook diary; poor for junk journals.
- **Scanning:** flat pages scan well; bumpy collage casts shadows. Photograph at an angle with soft light instead.
- **Keeping Watch's X4:** a 1-bit reader with buttons; it can't draw. It could show a scanned sketch from your book on the sleep screen, dithered, if you load it over the hotspot (optional, and see RAM notes below).
- **What's lost on screen:** texture, touch, the object itself, and the pleasure of glue. **What's gained:** undo, no mess, no supply cost after the device.

## 16. Evidence and research

Two things get mixed up online: **art therapy** (a credentialed therapist, a therapeutic relationship, treatment goals) and **art-making or art journaling on your own**. The American Art Therapy Association says art activities like colouring books and apps "are not equivalent to professional art therapy services."

| Study | Sample | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guo, Liu, Zhu, He (2026), *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 17:1877502 | 12 RCTs, adults | Visual art therapy reduced depressive symptoms, SMD −0.81 (95% CI −1.16 to −0.46); anxiety SMD −0.69 in a subgroup of 6 studies | Moderate-to-large pooled effect, but blinding of participants and assessors was often unclear or high-risk, and heterogeneity was noted |
| Joschko, Roll, Grabowska, Willich, Berghöfer (2026), *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 17:1789633 | 26 studies; 19 (997 patients) in the meta-analysis | Art therapy for depression, SMD 0.53 (95% CI 0.30–0.76) favouring art therapy | Moderate effect; I² about 66%; mixed controls (waitlist, usual care, other treatments) |
| Quinn, Millard, Jones (2025), *Nature Mental Health* | 39 controlled studies; depression n = 3,360 (36 studies); anxiety n = 949 (10 studies), older adults, group arts | Depression d = 0.70, falling to 0.42 after adjusting for publication bias; anxiety d = 0.76. Bigger effects in care homes (d = 1.07) than community (d = 0.51) | Publication bias found; I² = 80.5%; mostly white women participants. All arts, not only visual |
| Crawford et al. (2012), *BMJ* 344:e846 (MATISSE) | 417 people with schizophrenia, UK, 3 arms | Group art therapy did not improve global functioning or symptoms compared with an activity group or usual care at 12 and 24 months. Only 61% of the art therapy arm attended at least one session | Large, pragmatic, well-designed RCT with a null result. Shows art therapy isn't a universal fix |
| Kaimal, Ray, Muniz (2016), *Art Therapy* 33(2):74–80 | 39 adults, 18–59 | After 45 minutes of free art-making (markers, clay, collage), about 75% had lower cortisol; no difference by prior art experience | Small, no control group; cortisol also falls naturally over the day |
| Curry & Kasser (2005), *Art Therapy* 22(2):81–85 | 84 undergraduates after an anxiety induction | Colouring a mandala or a plaid pattern lowered anxiety more than free colouring on blank paper | Small, students, short-term. Suggests structure helps |
| van der Vennet & Serice (2012), *Art Therapy* 29(2):87–92 | College students | Replication: mandala colouring lowered anxiety more than plaid or blank | Small; this time mandala beat plaid, so the "pattern" versus "mandala" question is unsettled |
| Stuckey & Nobel (2010), *Am. J. Public Health* 100(2):254–263 | Narrative review | Visual art linked to better wellbeing, less distress, better emotional expression in illness | Many included studies lacked controls, used small convenience samples and varied measures |
| Fancourt & Finn (2019), WHO Europe HEN report 67 | Scoping review, 3,000+ studies | Arts play a role in prevention, promotion and treatment across the lifespan; "multi-modal, low-cost interventions" | Breadth over depth; mixed study types, so evidence quality varies |

**Claimed versus supported**

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Art therapy with a therapist reduces depression and anxiety | Supported, moderate effect, with real bias and quality limits |
| Art therapy helps every condition | Not supported (MATISSE null for schizophrenia) |
| Making art lowers stress | Some support (small, mostly uncontrolled or short-term studies) |
| Structured colouring calms more than a blank page | Some support (two small RCTs; which pattern is unclear) |
| Art journaling on your own treats depression | Not studied directly; don't claim it |
| You need talent for it to help | Not supported; Kaimal found no effect of experience |
| Junk journaling is therapeutic | Claimed by the community (Wikipedia notes practitioners link it to stress relief); no studies found |

## 17. Benefits

- Moderate reduction in depression and anxiety when delivered as art therapy. **Evidenced** (with limits)
- Short-term drop in stress after making art, regardless of skill. **Evidenced** (weakly)
- Short-term drop in anxiety from colouring a structured pattern. **Evidenced** (weakly)
- A way to record days without many words. **Claimed**
- Keeps memories as objects. **Claimed**
- Play and flow outside of work. **Claimed**
- Cheap and sustainable when you use found paper. **Claimed**, obviously true when you don't buy kits
- Social connection through swaps and groups. **Claimed**; group arts had evidence for older adults (Quinn 2025)

## 18. Pitfalls

| Problem | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Blank-page freeze | Fear of ruining a nice book | Ruin page one on purpose; use cheap paper; start with a background |
| Comparing with Instagram | Polished pages online | Keep a "just for me" book; don't photograph it |
| Supply spiral | Buying feels like making | One-in-one-out rule; a stash box limit; use found paper first |
| Never "finishing" a page | Perfectionism | Timer: 10 minutes, then stop |
| Too tired to set up paints | Setup cost | Keep a dry kit (glue stick, tape, pen) open on the table |
| Pages too thick, book won't close | Layers | Fewer layers; build a junk journal with a wider spine; remove some pages before binding |
| Private info in ephemera | Receipts, envelopes, labels | Cut off names, addresses, barcodes, account numbers |
| Using it instead of care | Art as the only coping tool | Keep the safety plan and support list nearby; art is a companion to care, not a replacement |
| Mess and sensory overload | Paint, glue, smells | Dry media only; tape instead of glue |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** Short touches beat long sessions. Keep a kit in one visible box. Novelty helps: rotate materials. Watch for supply-buying as a dopamine hit.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** Sticky, wet or smelly materials can be too much. Use tape, stickers and dry pens. Structured tasks (fill a grid, colour a pattern) may calm more than open blank pages (Curry & Kasser 2005).
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** The "one thing taped in" day counts. Sit or lie down; use a clipboard. Pre-cut scraps on good days for bad days.
- **Depression and anxiety:** A page can be one colour. Colouring a pattern is a known low-energy calmer. Don't force meaning.
- **Trauma:** Imagery can bring up more than words. Keep it light and present-focused (what's on the table), stop if distressed, and keep hard material for work with a therapist. Art therapy exists for that.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** Images and single words carry the page. Stamps and sticker letters replace handwriting.
- **Low vision:** High-contrast materials, thick black markers, large collage pieces, tactile items (fabric, string).
- **Motor or hand pain:** Tear, don't cut. Tape instead of glue sticks (which need pressure). Large tools with thick grips. Stickers are the lowest-effort option.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** Collage lets you build images of yourself you haven't seen elsewhere (cut-out clothes, hair, words). Keep books private if safety at home is an issue. Community ephemera swaps can be affirming.
- **Shift workers:** Keep a pocket kit (a small notebook, a glue stick, tape) for breaks; collect ephemera on the route; do long sessions on days off.
- **Low income:** Junk journaling is built for this: envelopes, cereal boxes, library book sale pages, free printables, and a single glue stick.

## 21. Combining

- **With structured day pages:** the Sketch box is the art zone. Everything scannable stays outside it.
- **With [time logging](./time-logging-lifelog.md):** the hour ribbon is the dry record; the art is the colour on top. A ticket stub taped next to the hour you rode the bus is both.
- **With [a commonplace book](./commonplace-book.md):** collage a quote with an image.
- **With [expressive writing](./expressive-writing.md):** write, then paint over it for privacy.
- **With [a dream journal](./dream-journal.md):** sketch the dream's main image before you write it.
- **With [a DBT diary card](./dbt-diary-card.md):** art-making is a distraction or self-soothe skill in many DBT skill lists (not checked in this doc's sources).
- **With [Stoic journaling](./stoic-journaling.md):** a sketch of one thing you can control.
- **With a weekly review:** glue in the week's best scrap.

## 23. Glossary

- **Altered book:** an existing book painted and collaged over.
- **Art therapy:** a mental health profession using art-making within a therapeutic relationship, practised by credentialed therapists.
- **Belly band:** a paper strip around a signature or page to hold loose items.
- **Collage:** an image made by gluing pieces of paper or other material.
- **Die cut:** a shape cut by a machine and a metal die.
- **Ephemera:** everyday paper meant to be thrown away (tickets, receipts, tags).
- **Focal point:** where the eye lands first on a page.
- **Gesso:** a primer that makes thin paper take paint.
- **Glue book:** a quick daily collage book.
- **Junk journal:** a handmade book from recycled or found paper.
- **Matte medium:** an acrylic glue and sealer.
- **Mixed media:** using several materials on one page.
- **Pamphlet stitch:** a simple 3- or 5-hole binding stitch.
- **Signature:** a set of folded sheets nested together.
- **Smash book:** K&Company's pre-printed stick-it-in journal (2011).
- **Stash:** your supply of paper and materials.
- **Tuck spot:** a small slot or flap to hold a tag.
- **Visual journal:** another name for an art journal.
- **Washi tape:** decorative, low-tack paper tape.
- **Zetti:** Teesha Moore's figure style (stoic faces, pointy hats, stripes).

## 24. FAQ

**1. I can't draw. Can I still do this?**
Yes. Collage, tape and stickers need no drawing. Kaimal's study found stress dropped the same whatever people's art experience.

**2. Is art journaling "art therapy"?**
No. Art therapy is a profession with trained therapists. Art journaling is self-care and making. Both can be good; they aren't the same.

**3. Does it actually help mental health?**
Art therapy with a therapist has moderate pooled effects on depression and anxiety, from studies with real weaknesses. Solo art journaling isn't well studied. Short art-making and structured colouring have small studies showing lower stress and anxiety in the short term.

**4. What's the cheapest way to start?**
A stapled booklet of printer paper, a glue stick, and your recycling.

**5. How do I keep the book from getting too fat?**
Fewer layers, thinner paper, a wider spine, or remove a page for every two you add stuff to.

**6. Can I do this in my printed Keeping Watch book?**
Yes, in the Sketch box, with dry media, tape and glue stick. Keep the header, frame, SEND TO strip and page code clear.

**7. What should I never glue in?**
Anything with your full name, address, account numbers, barcodes or medical details visible. Cut or black those out first.

**8. Pages look bad. Should I tear them out?**
Leave them. Messy pages often read better later. If you must, glue two pages together and start again on the outside.

**9. Mandala or blank page?**
If you're anxious, a pattern to colour seems to calm more than a blank page in two small studies.

**10. Is washi tape archival?**
It's low-tack and can lift over years. For keepsakes, add a dot of glue under it (general craft advice, not tested here).

### e. Don't adopt

- **Decorative borders, textures or vintage ornament on printed pages.** Busy, against the calm design, and they fight the scan zones.
- **Colour-dependent prompts** ("use red for anger"). Print is black and white.
- **Instructions to paint on day pages.** KDP paper is thin; wet media will cockle and bleed through.
- **Calling any of it "art therapy."** AATA is clear that products and apps aren't; claims would mislead.
- **Supply shopping lists or brand pushes.** Against the low-cost ethic.
- **Generated images or AI prompts on the X4.** Out of scope.
- **Ephemera printed with real names, addresses or barcodes** as sample content. The editor and samples use generic data only.

## 26. Open questions

- No trial found of self-directed art journaling (outside therapy) on mood.
- Mandala versus plaid: the 2005 study found them equal; the 2012 replication found mandala better. Which pattern works best for a printed "colour me" block is unsettled.
- Exact mt launch year (mt says the first collection came in 2007 and the brand was founded in 2008).
- Teesha Moore's own site was down; Artfest dates and the 16-page journal details come from community pages and video listings, not primary sources.
- How much glue and tape a KDP paperback can take before the binding strains. Needs a physical test.
- Whether a collaged Sketch box affects page-code scanning if collage overlaps the frame. Needs a scan test.
