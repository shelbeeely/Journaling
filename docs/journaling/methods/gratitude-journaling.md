# Gratitude journaling

Writing down, on a schedule, things you are glad about and where they came from. This doc covers the research practice
and its offshoots. The branded Five Minute Journal has its own doc: [five-minute-journal.md](five-minute-journal.md).

## 1. At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Creator** | No single creator. The research form comes from Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough (2003). "Three good things" comes from Martin Seligman's group (2005). Older roots are religious thanksgiving and "count your blessings". |
| **Year** | 2003 (first randomized trial of a gratitude list); 2005 (three good things) |
| **Type** | Writing practice; a positive psychology intervention (PPI) |
| **Time per day** | 2–5 minutes for a list; 10–15 minutes for one item in depth |
| **Time per week** | 5–15 minutes if weekly (the research-favoured rhythm); 15–35 minutes if daily |
| **Cost** | Free (any paper). Branded gratitude journals run about US$10–35. |
| **Formats and sizes** | Any notebook; a block on a day page; an index card; an app; a jar of slips |
| **Best for** | People with a stable baseline who want a small lift in mood; noticing good things during a hard stretch; people who like short, finishable tasks |
| **Not ideal for** | Treating clinical depression or anxiety on its own; people in active crisis, grief or unsafe situations; people who feel judged by "should be grateful" |
| **Learning curve** | None to start; the skill is making entries specific and varied |
| **Supplies** | A pen and 3–5 short lines |
| **Official site** | None. The Greater Good Science Center hosts a free research-based version: <https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/gratitude_journal> |
| **Evidence level** | **Some studies, many trials, small effects.** Dozens of randomized trials and several meta-analyses. Reliable small boost to positive mood and well-being; weak and control-dependent effect on depression and anxiety. |

## 2. Summary

A gratitude journal is a list of things you are glad about, written on a schedule. Researchers have tested it more than
almost any other self-help exercise: across 145 papers and about 25,000 people in 28 countries it raises well-being by a
small amount (Hedges' g about 0.19; Choi et al. 2025). It works best when entries are specific, varied, about people, and
not so frequent that they turn into a chore; one study found once a week beat three times a week. Against a fair
comparison task, like writing about your day, the advantage shrinks to almost nothing for anxiety and depression (Davis
et al. 2016; Cregg & Cheavens 2021). So it is a pleasant, cheap, low-risk habit, not a treatment. For Journalwright Studio this
means a small, optional "Small good things" block (already built), a weekly rather than daily default, and wording that
never tells a person how to feel.

## 3. History and origin

### Who created it

There is no inventor. Three strands meet:

- **Religious and moral practice.** Thanksgiving prayers, grace before meals, the Jewish morning blessings and the
  Christian "examen" all ask people to name what they received. The hymn "Count Your Blessings" (Johnson Oatman Jr.,
  1897) gave English its phrase.
- **Popular self-help.** Sarah Ban Breathnach's *Simple Abundance* (1995) told readers to write five things they were
  grateful for each night, and Oprah Winfrey promoted it on air in the late 1990s. This made the "gratitude journal" a
  consumer product before the science arrived. *(Dates from memory of the book's publication history; the Oprah link is
  widely reported but I did not open a primary source for it.)*
- **Research psychology.** Robert A. Emmons (UC Davis) and Michael E. McCullough (then University of Miami) ran the first
  randomized trials, published in 2003. Martin Seligman (University of Pennsylvania), who launched "positive
  psychology" as president of the American Psychological Association in 1998, added "three good things" in 2005.

### Cultural context

Gratitude journaling rode two waves: 1990s self-help (abundance, simplicity) and 2000s positive psychology (happiness
as a skill). Both are American and individualist. The 2025 cross-cultural meta-analysis found the effect is not the same
everywhere, and some cultures experience gratitude as mixed with indebtedness. Social media turned the list into a
performance ("blessed"), which fed the later "toxic positivity" backlash.

### Key quotes (short)

- Emmons & McCullough (2003): "a conscious focus on blessings may have emotional and interpersonal benefits." Note the
  word *may*.
- Lyubomirsky et al. (2005): counting blessings several times a week may have led people "to become bored with the
  practice, finding it less fresh and meaningful over time."
- Cregg & Cheavens (2021): "we recommend individuals seeking to reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety engage in
  interventions with stronger evidence of efficacy."

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- **Attention is trainable.** Negative events grab attention on their own; good ones fade (hedonic adaptation).
  Writing them down gives them a second look.
- **Gratitude has two parts** (Emmons): affirming that something good happened, and seeing that its source is at least
  partly outside yourself (a person, luck, the world). That is why "why it happened" or "who made it happen" matters.
- **Small and specific beats big and vague.** "The bus driver waited when I ran" does more than "my health".
- **Freshness matters.** Variety and not-too-often keep it meaningful (Lyubomirsky's "refractory period").
- **It supplements, it doesn't replace.** It sits beside care, not instead of it.

**Problem it solves:** a day that was mostly hard still had something worth keeping, and without a note it is lost.

## 5. Core components

| Element | What it is | Required? |
|---|---|---|
| The list | 1–5 short lines of good things | Yes |
| The "because" | Why it happened or who caused it | Strongly recommended |
| The schedule | Daily, several times a week, or weekly | Yes, pick one |
| Depth item | One entry expanded to a few sentences | Optional |
| Gratitude letter | A letter to a person, sent or not | Optional, periodic |
| Mental subtraction | Imagine life without one good thing | Optional variant |
| Review | Rereading old entries | Optional |

### The list
Short lines, one good thing each. Research used 1 to 5 items; Seligman used 3; Emmons used up to 5. Fewer is fine.

### The "because"
Seligman's instruction was to write why each good thing happened. It moves attention from the thing to its source and
makes the entry specific.

### The schedule
The best-known frequency finding is Lyubomirsky's: once a week beat three times a week over six weeks, among
students. It is one small study; the GGSC practice suggests at least three times a week for two weeks. Treat both as
starting points and adjust for boredom.

### Depth item
The GGSC practice says: "prioritize depth". One item explored beats five skimmed.

### Gratitude letter
Write to a person who helped you. Seligman's "gratitude visit" delivered it in person and had the largest short-term
effect. Wong et al. (2016) found letters helped therapy clients even when not sent.

### Mental subtraction
Imagine how a day or your life would be without one good thing (Koo, Algoe, Wilson & Gilbert, 2008, is the usual
citation; I did not reopen that paper for this doc). Some people find it less saccharine than listing.

### Review
Reading back entries on a bad day. Not well studied; people report it helps and sometimes that it hurts ("I used to be
happier").

## 6. Setting it up

1. **Pick the slot.** One line on the day page, or a weekly box on the week spread. The `good` block
   works as-is.
2. **Pick the count.** Start at 1 line. Research does not show that five beats one.
3. **Pick the rhythm.** Default to weekly (Sunday or the weekly review). Go daily only if it stays fresh.
4. **Write the prompt in neutral words.** "Small good things" rather than "I am grateful for". It asks what happened,
   not how you should feel.
5. **Decide what "skip" means.** A blank line is allowed. No streaks.

**First day:** one line, one good thing, one "because". Under a minute.

**First week:** do it on 3 days, or just once at the weekly review. At the end of the week, reread and circle the one
that still feels true.

## 7. Daily practice

Evening, 2–5 minutes, after the care check-in:

1. **Pause** (10 s). Look at the day's events or the time blocks.
2. **Find one good thing** (30 s). Small counts: warm socks, a quiet bus, a text back.
3. **Make it specific** (30 s). Who, where, what exactly.
4. **Add the because** (30 s). "because Sam saved me a seat".
5. **Optional second and third** (1 min).
6. **Optional depth** (2–5 min). Pick one and write a few sentences in the writing space.
7. **Stop.** Don't grade it.

**If nothing comes:** write "nothing today" or leave it blank. A true blank is healthier than a forced line.

## 8. Rhythms

- **Weekly:** the research-favoured default. At the weekly review, list 3 good things from the week. Circle one to
  carry to the month.
- **Monthly:** on Looking back, copy the circled ones. Consider one gratitude letter (sent or not).
- **Quarterly:** change the angle to fight boredom: people this season, places, your body, small tools, weather.
- **Yearly:** a "good things of the year" list from the monthly ones. Archive the rest; don't reread a whole year on a
  bad night.
- **Resets:** if it has gone stale or feels like a chore for 2 weeks, drop to weekly or pause for a month.

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variant | Who | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| Counting blessings | Emmons & McCullough 2003 | Up to five things, weekly or daily |
| Three good things | Seligman et al. 2005 | Three things that went well, and why; nightly for a week |
| Gratitude visit / letter | Seligman et al. 2005 | A letter delivered in person; one-off |
| Gratitude letter (unsent) | Wong et al. 2016 in therapy | Letters not necessarily delivered |
| Mental subtraction | Koo et al. 2008 | Imagine the good thing absent |
| Savoring log | Fred Bryant (savoring research) | Stay with one good moment longer |
| Gratitude jar | Popular | Slips of paper in a jar, read at year end |
| Five Minute Journal | Intelligent Change, 2013 | Morning and evening prompts; see [five-minute-journal.md](five-minute-journal.md) |
| "Small good things" / glimmers | Deb Dana (polyvagal-informed therapy) uses "glimmers" | Tiny moments of safety or ease rather than "blessings" |
| "One good thing" apps | Many | One entry a day, often with a photo |
| Awe walk log | Sturm et al. 2020 | Note moments of awe during a walk *(not reopened for this doc)* |

## 12. Community practice

- **What people do:** Reddit threads (r/Journaling, r/getdisciplined, r/bulletjournal) show most people keep a 3-line
  daily list, drift into repeating the same items ("family, cat, coffee"), and quit after a few weeks. The common fix is
  switching to "one specific thing" or "three good things that happened today" rather than things you "have".
- **Customisations:** colour-coded gratitude logs in bullet journals; "gratitude + 1 hard thing" pairs; photo-a-day
  gratitude; couple's gratitude jar; kids' "rose, bud, thorn".
- **Aesthetic camp:** decorated monthly gratitude spreads with one box per day, stickers and lettering.
- **Minimalist camp:** a single line at the bottom of a daily log, or a weekly list only.
- **Backlash camp:** "I'm not doing gratitude anymore" posts from people with depression, chronic illness or trauma
  who felt pressured to be thankful. They often switch to "what happened" logs or "glimmers".

## 13. Official products and formats

Not applicable as a method; there is no official product. The market is large and generic: undated gratitude journals
($10–35) from many publishers, the Five Minute Journal (see its doc), and apps. None has research behind the product
itself; the research used plain forms.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- Any pen. A fine pen (0.3–0.5 mm) fits short lines.
- Paper: any; 7 mm ruled lines fit 3 short entries in under an inch.
- No stickers or stamps needed; a single heart icon marks the block.
- A slip jar if you like physical collections.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps:** Presently, Gratitude (Happyfeed and others), Day One templates. Many push daily reminders and streaks, which
  the research on boredom suggests can backfire.
- **E-ink:** reMarkable and Boox templates of 3 lines; Kindle Scribe notebooks. Fine for handwriting; review is harder.
- **Scanning:** a short line block scans and OCRs well; the `good` zone would map to a text field.
- **Lost vs paper:** apps make review and search easy but add pings, streaks and ads. Paper keeps it quiet and private.
  There is no strong evidence for either format; the GGSC page stresses writing over just thinking.

## 16. Evidence and research

### Key studies

| Study | Sample | Design | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Emmons & McCullough 2003, *JPSP* | 3 studies: undergraduates (weekly, then daily) and adults with neuromuscular disease | Random assignment to gratitude vs hassles vs neutral or social comparison | Gratitude groups better on several, not all, outcomes; positive affect most robust | Foundational; small samples; self-report |
| Seligman et al. 2005, *Am Psychol* | Online volunteers (about 577 at baseline) | 6-arm RCT with placebo (early memories) | Three good things: happiness up, depression down for 6 months; gratitude visit: large 1-month boost | Self-selected happiness seekers; large dropout |
| Lyubomirsky, Sheldon & Schkade 2005, *Rev Gen Psychol* | Students | 6 weeks; once vs three times a week vs control | Only the once-a-week group rose | Small; described briefly in a review paper |
| Sheldon & Lyubomirsky 2006, *J Posit Psychol* | 67 undergraduates | 4 weeks; gratitude vs best possible self vs control | Both raised mood; BPS slightly better; motivation predicted adherence | Small |
| Froh, Sefick & Emmons 2008, *J Sch Psychol* | Early adolescents in classrooms | Gratitude vs hassles vs control, 2 weeks | Less negative affect, more school satisfaction in gratitude group | Classroom-level |
| Mongrain & Anselmo-Matthews 2012, *J Clin Psychol* | Online adults | Replication of Seligman with stronger controls | Happiness rose, but equally with a positive placebo; no depression edge | Key replication |
| Wong et al. 2016, *Psychother Res* | 293 therapy clients | Therapy only vs +expressive writing vs +gratitude letters | Gratitude better mental health at 4 and 12 weeks | Adjunct to therapy, not alone |
| Sin & Lyubomirsky 2009 | 51 PPIs, 4,266 people | Meta-analysis | Well-being r = .29, depression r = .31 | Broad PPIs; weak controls inflate |
| Bolier et al. 2013, *BMC Public Health* | 39 RCTs, 6,139 people | Meta-analysis | SWB 0.34, PWB 0.20, depression 0.23 | Publication bias and low quality noted |
| Davis et al. 2016, *J Couns Psychol* | k = 4–18 per test, N up to 1,755 | Meta-analysis by control type | vs activity-matched d = .14; vs psychologically active d = −.03; anxiety n.s. | Careful control coding |
| Dickens 2017, *Basic Appl Soc Psychol* | 38 studies, 282 effect sizes | 56 meta-analyses | Helps some outcomes incl. happiness, not others; "may be overemphasized"; depression about d = .13 (as cited by Cregg & Cheavens) | Broad |
| Cregg & Cheavens 2021, *J Happiness Stud* | 27 studies, N = 3,675 | Meta-analysis on depression and anxiety | g = −0.29 post, −0.23 follow-up; −0.18 vs active; −0.51 vs waitlist | Clear recommendation: use better-evidenced care for symptoms |
| Choi et al. 2025, *PNAS* | 145 papers, 163 samples, 24,804 people, 28 countries | Preregistered meta-analysis | g = 0.19 on well-being; varies by country; larger for positive affect, combined methods, RCTs | Largest to date |

### Critiques: control groups and toxic positivity

- **The control-group problem.** Early trials compared gratitude with "list your hassles" or with doing nothing. Both
  flatter gratitude. Against a fair task, like listing the day's events, the gap shrinks (Davis 2016: d = .14) or
  vanishes (d = −.03 against a psychologically active task). Mongrain's replication found a "positive placebo" worked
  as well, which suggests the active ingredient may be any positive, self-relevant writing, not gratitude as such.
- **Toxic positivity.** A 2026 scoping review defines it by four parts: emotional suppression, emotional invalidation,
  unrealistic optimism and disingenuous happiness (Premlal et al.). Gratitude practice is not toxic in itself, but a
  rule that you *must* feel thankful fits all four.
- **Accepting bad feelings helps.** Ford et al. (2018) found, across lab, diary and longitudinal studies, that people
  who accept their negative emotions rather than judge them have better psychological health. That argues for pairing
  good things with an honest "was hard".
- **Chasing happiness can backfire.** Mauss et al. (2011) found that valuing happiness highly predicted *less*
  happiness, especially in good circumstances. A practice framed as "become happier" may add pressure.
- **Self-selection.** Many samples are volunteers seeking happiness, students or online users who stayed to the end.

### Claimed vs supported

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Makes you happier | **Supported, small** (g ≈ 0.2) |
| Improves positive mood | **Supported**, the most reliable outcome |
| Reduces depression | **Weak.** Small vs waitlist, near zero vs fair comparison |
| Reduces anxiety | **Not supported** vs fair comparisons |
| Better sleep | **Some studies** (Emmons 2003 neuromuscular study), not firmly replicated here |
| Better physical health, more exercise | **Early, self-report** |
| Better relationships | **Plausible**, stronger for letters to people |
| Works everywhere the same | **No**, varies across countries (Choi 2025) |
| More is better | **No**; once a week beat three times in one study |

## 17. Benefits

- Small lift in positive mood. **Evidenced.**
- Small lift in life satisfaction and well-being. **Evidenced.**
- Something to write on a day you don't want to write. **Claimed.**
- Records good moments you'd forget; useful on Looking back. **Claimed.**
- Gratitude letters strengthen ties and help therapy clients. **Evidenced (one RCT) / claimed.**
- Cheap, fast, low-risk. **Evidenced** (no harms reported in trials; harms not well measured).
- Better sleep. **Claimed / limited evidence.**

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---|---|
| Same items every day ("coffee, cat") | Require one specific moment and a "because"; rotate an angle each month |
| Becomes a chore | Drop to weekly; the research supports it |
| Guilt: "others have it worse" | Use "what went okay" wording; no comparisons |
| Forced gratitude on a terrible day | Allow blank and "nothing today"; pair with Was hard |
| Using it instead of care for depression | Keep it beside the care plan and Support page, not as treatment |
| Streak anxiety | No streak counters; blanks are neutral |
| Gratitude that covers harm (grateful to an abuser, to a job that hurts you) | Gratitude is for real goods; it is not a duty to people who harm you |
| Performing it for others (social media) | Keep it private |
| Rereading on a bad night makes it worse | Reread on neutral days; skip the archive in crisis |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** one line, not five; weekly default; tie it to an existing step (after brushing teeth). Novelty helps:
  rotate angles.
- **Autism and sensory needs:** concrete prompts ("a sound you liked") beat abstract ones ("what blessings"); allow
  sensory items (a quiet room, a soft shirt).
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** a single circled word or icon counts. "Things that were easier than expected".
- **Depression:** the evidence says don't rely on it. Use neutral wording ("small good things", "what happened that was
  okay"), allow blanks, cap at one line, and never pair it with guilt. Behavioral activation and therapy have stronger
  evidence. If listing makes you feel worse, stop; that is a known reaction, not a failure.
- **Anxiety:** helps some by shifting attention; for others it becomes another thing to get right. Keep it optional.
- **Trauma:** avoid "grateful for everything that happened"; gratitude is never owed to harm. "Glimmers" (small moments
  of safety) can feel safer.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** single words, icons, a sketch, or a voice note are fine.
- **Low vision:** larger line spacing; 8.5×11 trim; high contrast.
- **Motor or hand pain:** one word or a tick next to a pre-printed list (warmth, food, rest, people, outside).
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** "a moment I felt like me" (a Journalwright Studio preset) is gratitude aimed at
  euphoria rather than at circumstances. Never frame as "be grateful for what you have" about medical access.
- **Shift workers:** "today" is a shift, not a date. Write after the shift, not at bedtime.
- **Low income:** free by nature. Watch wording that equates good things with purchases.

## 21. Combining

- **With Went well / Was hard / Tomorrow:** the balanced version. Gratitude alone can feel like denial; paired with
  "Was hard" it feels honest.
- **With the [Bullet Journal](bullet-journal.md):** a weekly gratitude collection; not a daily requirement.
- **With [Five-year diaries](five-year-diary.md):** one good line per day reads well across years.
- **With [Stoic journaling](stoic-journaling.md):** the Stoic "negative visualisation" is close to mental subtraction.
- **With [Morning pages](morning-pages.md):** morning pages dump; a good-things line closes the day.
- **With prompt journaling:** gratitude prompts are one family in [prompt-journaling.md](prompt-journaling.md).
- **With exchange pages:** a gratitude letter is a natural exchange prompt ("Who are you thankful for, and why?" is
  already in the year's list).

## 23. Glossary

- **Gratitude intervention:** any structured exercise meant to raise gratitude (lists, letters, visits).
- **Counting blessings:** listing things one is grateful for (Emmons & McCullough).
- **Three good things:** nightly list of three things that went well and why (Seligman).
- **Gratitude visit:** writing and delivering a thank-you letter in person.
- **Mental subtraction:** imagining a good thing absent.
- **Positive psychology intervention (PPI):** exercises aimed at raising positive feelings or strengths.
- **Hedonic adaptation:** getting used to good things so they stop registering.
- **Refractory period:** Lyubomirsky's term for the rest time before an activity regains its effect.
- **Effect size (d, g):** difference between groups in standard deviations; 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.
- **Waitlist control:** a comparison group that does nothing but fill in measures; inflates effects.
- **Active control:** a comparison group that does a similar non-gratitude task (e.g., list daily events).
- **Positive placebo:** a pleasant control task (e.g., early memories) used to test whether any positive writing works.
- **Meta-analysis:** a study that pools many studies' results.
- **Toxic positivity:** pressure to stay positive that suppresses or invalidates real negative feelings.
- **Glimmer:** a small cue of safety or ease (term from polyvagal-informed therapy).
- **Savoring:** deliberately staying with a good experience.

## 24. FAQ

**How many things should I write?** One to three. No study shows five beats one; specifics matter more than count.

**Daily or weekly?** Start weekly. One six-week student study found once a week worked and three times did not. If
daily stays fresh for you, fine.

**Morning or night?** Most research used evening. Choose the time you'll actually do it.

**Will it help my depression?** Probably not much on its own. Against fair comparisons the effect on depression is
small or near zero (Cregg & Cheavens 2021). It can sit beside treatment. If it makes you feel worse, stop.

**What if I can't think of anything?** Leave it blank or write "got through it". Forcing it is where the harm is.

**Is it toxic positivity?** Not by itself. It turns toxic when it becomes a rule that you must feel thankful, or when
it replaces feeling or fixing what's wrong. Pair it with a "was hard" line.

**Do I have to be grateful *to* someone?** No. "Good things that happened" works. Entries about people seem to do a
little more, and letters to people have good evidence.

**Should I reread old entries?** On neutral days, sometimes. Not as a rescue on the worst night.

**Does writing matter, or can I just think it?** The GGSC practice stresses writing; most studies used writing. Thinking
it is better than nothing.

**Does it work in every culture?** Effects vary between countries (Choi et al. 2025). Where gratitude carries debt or
obligation, "good things" wording may fit better.

### e. Don't adopt

- **Streaks, counters or "days grateful in a row".** Adds pressure; the research favours not-too-often.
- **"I am grateful for" as the fixed label.** Tells the person how to feel; use neutral wording.
- **Daily five-line lists as the default.** Evidence doesn't support more lines; it costs page space.
- **Quote-of-the-day gratitude affirmations.** Busy, and feed-like.
- **Claims that gratitude treats depression or anxiety.** The meta-analyses don't support it; keep support pages and any care plan central.
- **Social sharing of entries.** Privacy rules and performance pressure.

## 26. Open questions

- The once-a-week finding rests on one small student study described in a review paper. No large trial has tested
  frequency head to head.
- Whether gratitude *lists* versus *letters* differ much; letters look stronger but comparisons are few.
- Harms: few trials measured worsening. Anecdotes (depression, chronic illness) suggest some people feel worse.
- How it works for trans people, disabled people and shift workers specifically: no studies found.
- Whether paper beats apps: no direct comparison found.
- Long-term (over a year) effects: follow-ups rarely exceed six months.
