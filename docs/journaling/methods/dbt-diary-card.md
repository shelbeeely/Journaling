# DBT diary card (Dialectical Behavior Therapy self-monitoring)

> **Not medical advice.** This is a research summary to help design journal pages. It is not therapy. The diary card
> was built to be used with a DBT therapist and a skills group, and this doc does not replace either.

## 2. Summary

The DBT diary card is a one-page weekly grid that a person fills in every day during Dialectical Behavior Therapy. It
records, in numbers and ticks, how strong a handful of emotions were (usually 0–5), how strong the urges were for the
behaviours they are working on (0–5), whether they acted on those urges (yes/no or a count), what they took
(medication, alcohol, drugs), and whether and how well they used DBT skills (a 0–7 scale), with a back page for ticking
which skills they practised each day. Its job is to give the person and their therapist an honest, quick record of the
week so the session can start from facts, and so any self-harm or suicidal behaviour gets addressed first. It's short
by design, private, and non-judgemental: a missed day or a hard day is data, not failure. The card works best inside
DBT; used alone it can still be a calm daily check-in and skills reminder, but it isn't a treatment by itself.

## 3. History and origin

### Who created it

Marsha M. Linehan, an American psychologist, created DBT and its diary card. Her memoir, *Building a Life Worth Living*
(Random House, 2020), describes a severe crisis at age 18 that led to years in a psychiatric hospital, and how that
experience later shaped DBT, which "combines self-acceptance with actionable change strategies". She earned her PhD,
joined the UW Department of Psychology, and ran the Behavioral Research and Therapy Clinics (BRTC) there for over four
decades. She said publicly that she had kept her own history private for years and chose to share it after a patient
asked whether she was "one of us" (reported in coverage of the memoir; **uncertain** on exact wording).

### Cultural context

DBT arrived when BPD was widely seen as untreatable and people who self-harmed were often turned away. It brought
behaviourism (track the behaviour, analyse it, change the contingencies) together with Zen-influenced acceptance and
mindfulness, which Linehan practised. The diary card is the behavioural half made visible: count what happens, don't
guess. The phrase "a life worth living" became DBT's stated goal and the title of her memoir.

### Key quotes

- DBT's aim, in Linehan's framing: building "a life worth living" (memoir title and a standard DBT phrase).
- From the 1999 card itself, the skills scale is written in plain words, from "0 = Not thought about or used" to
  "7 = Didn't try, used them, helped". The card's own language is the clearest statement of its non-judgemental tone:
  thinking about a skill and not using it still counts as a 1 or 2.

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- **Dialectics: acceptance and change together.** You accept what you felt and did today, exactly as it was, and you
  also work to change it. The card asks for honest numbers without commentary.
- **Behaviour is tracked, not guessed.** Memory of a week is shaped by the worst day. A daily card corrects that.
- **The target hierarchy.** In DBT, life-threatening behaviour comes first, then behaviour that gets in the way of
  therapy, then problems that damage quality of life, then building skills. The card is laid out so the therapist can
  see at a glance whether anything in the first tier happened, and put it first on the agenda.
- **Skills replace problem behaviour.** DBT assumes many problem behaviours are attempts to cope that work in the short
  term. The skills column shows whether new coping is being tried and whether it helped.
- **No moral scoring.** Urges are rated separately from actions. A high urge with no action is a success, and the card
  makes that visible.
- **Filling it in is itself a behaviour.** Not completing the card is treated in DBT as something to understand and
  solve (was it shame? forgetting? no time?), not punished.
- **Problem it solves:** sessions that drift into the latest crisis without data; patients and therapists who can't
  see progress; skills that are learned in group but never practised.

## 5. Core components

|---|---|---|---|

| Before/after session ratings | Urge to use, urge to quit therapy, urge to harm, and "belief in control of" emotions, behaviours, thoughts | 0–5 | Front, bottom |

### Header

The 1999 BRTC card asks "Filled out in session? Y N" and "How often did you fill out this side? Daily / 2–3x / Once".
That's a built-in honesty check: a card completed from memory in the waiting room is less reliable, and the question
makes that easy to say without shame.

### Urges (0–5)

Urges are rated separately from actions. On the BRTC card the front starts with "Use", "Suicide" and "S-H"
(self-harm), each 0–5. Clinics add others, such as urge to binge, to quit therapy, or to isolate. 0 means none; 5 means
the strongest. Rating an urge doesn't mean acting on it.

### Emotions (0–5)

The BRTC card lists Pain, Sad, Shame, Anger and Fear, plus Joy near the end. "Pain" here means emotional misery. Joy is
there on purpose; a 2025 study of 95 adults in DBT found daily joy ratings rose during treatment, fastest in the first
months, and rises in joy predicted later drops in BPD severity (Bitran et al.). Some clinics use 0–10 or 0–100 instead
of 0–5 **(common variation)**.

### Target behaviours

The BRTC card has number-and-specify columns for illicit drugs, alcohol ("ETOH"), prescription meds and
over-the-counter meds, a Y/N for self-harm, and a count for lying. "Prescription" here means how much was taken, so
that misuse and missed doses are both visible. Many modern cards add "Took meds as prescribed Y/N".

### Skills used (0–7)

This is the column people remember. Verbatim from the 1999 BRTC card:

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0 | Not thought about or used |
| 1 | Thought about, not used, didn't want to |
| 2 | Thought about, not used, wanted to |
| 3 | Tried but couldn't use them |
| 4 | Tried, could do them but they didn't help |
| 5 | Tried, could use them, helped |
| 6 | Didn't try, used them, didn't help |
| 7 | Didn't try, used them, helped |

"Didn't try, used them" means the skill happened automatically, which is the goal. A German inpatient study grouped
these as no use (0–3), unsuccessful use (4–5) and successful use (6–7) (Probst et al., 2018). Note this grouping puts 5
("tried, could use them, helped") with unsuccessful use; that's the study's choice, not Linehan's wording.

### Before/after session ratings

Bottom of the front: urge to use, urge to quit therapy and urge to harm, rated before and after the session, and
"belief in control of" emotions, behaviours and thoughts. These show whether the session itself helped.

### Skills checklist (back side)

A list of every skill with Mon–Sun beside each; circle the days you practised it. The shared 1999 sample lists 23 lines:
wise mind; observe; describe; participate; nonjudgmental stance; one-mindfully; effectiveness; DEAR MAN; GIVE; FAST;
PLEASE; build mastery; build positive experiences; opposite action; distract; self-soothe; improve the moment; pros and
cons; radical acceptance; and four "building structure" lines (work, love, time, place). That sample also adds "urge
surfing", "alternate rebellion" and "adaptive denial" from the substance-use adaptation. Cards built on the 2014 manual
list more skills (see below).

### The four skills modules (2014 manual)

| Module | What it's for | Examples of skills |
|---|---|---|
| Mindfulness | Noticing without judging; the base for everything else | Wise mind; "what" skills (observe, describe, participate); "how" skills (nonjudgmentally, one-mindfully, effectively) |
| Distress tolerance | Getting through a crisis without making it worse; accepting what can't change | STOP, pros and cons, TIPP, distract (ACCEPTS), self-soothe, IMPROVE the moment, radical acceptance, turning the mind, willingness |
| Emotion regulation | Understanding and changing emotions; lowering vulnerability | Check the facts, opposite action, problem solving, ABC PLEASE (accumulate positives, build mastery, cope ahead, PLEASE self-care) |
| Interpersonal effectiveness | Asking, saying no, keeping relationships and self-respect | DEAR MAN, GIVE, FAST; finding and ending relationships; walking the middle path |

Acronym expansions (DEAR MAN = Describe, Express, Assert, Reinforce, stay Mindful, Appear confident, Negotiate; GIVE =
Gentle, Interested, Validate, Easy manner; FAST = Fair, no Apologies, Stick to values, Truthful; PLEASE = treat
PhysicaL illness, balanced Eating, avoid mood-Altering substances, balanced Sleep, get Exercise; TIPP = Temperature,
Intense exercise, Paced breathing, Paired muscle relaxation; STOP = Stop, Take a step back, Observe, Proceed mindfully)
are the standard ones taught in DBT groups **(uncertain in exact wording between editions)**.

### DBT's four treatment modes

The 2015 JAMA Psychiatry trial describes standard DBT as four parts: weekly individual therapy, weekly group skills
training, as-needed phone coaching between sessions, and a therapist consultation team. The card links them: filled in
daily (between sessions), reviewed in individual therapy, and its skills column comes from group.

### Review in session

In DBT, the individual session usually starts with the therapist reading the card. The order follows the target
hierarchy: any self-harm or suicidal behaviour or high urges first, then therapy-interfering behaviour (including a
missing card), then quality-of-life targets. A marked behaviour usually leads to a **chain analysis**: a step-by-step
reconstruction of vulnerability, prompting event, links (thoughts, feelings, body sensations, actions), the behaviour and
its consequences, followed by "where could a skill have fit?" Therapists are also taught to validate and reinforce the
effort of filling it in. (A Wiley DBT book has a whole chapter titled "Self-Monitoring with the Diary Card"; its publisher page blocked
automated reading, so details here come from the card itself and the studies below **(uncertain in detail)**.)

## 6. Setting it up

### Inside DBT

1. Your therapist gives you their clinic's card, or you agree on one together.
2. Together you choose targets: the behaviours you want less of (the "actions" columns) and the urges linked to them.
3. You pick emotions to rate (the standard five plus joy is common).
4. You get the skills list, often in the order your group will teach them.
5. You agree when you'll fill it in (e.g. before bed) and where it lives (bag, bedside, phone).
6. You bring it to every session. If it isn't filled in, you fill it in together at the start.

### Self-guided (no therapist)

1. Read the limits in section 18 first. If self-harm or suicide urges are frequent, find a clinician; this is not a
   substitute.
2. Use a free card (UW BRTC or DBT Self-Help) or rule your own: 7 rows, 8–12 columns.
3. Choose **3–5 emotions**, **1–3 urges** and **1–3 actions**. Fewer columns means you'll keep doing it.
4. Choose 6–10 skills you're actually learning. Put them on the back.
5. Pick one time and one cue (brushing teeth, the X4 sleep screen, the last bus home).
6. Write a one-line safety note on the card: "If an urge is 4–5: my plan is on p. __" (see
   [safety-planning](safety-planning.md)).

### First day

Fill in one row in two minutes. Don't try to be accurate to the decimal. Rate each item by the **highest** it got
today, unless you agreed with your therapist on "average" **(both conventions exist; decide once)**.

### First week

Keep going even with gaps. At the end of the week, look at the whole card for five minutes: which day was hardest, and
was there a skill that day? Write one sentence. That's the review.

## 7. Daily practice

Order of operations (2–5 minutes, evening):

1. **Date and a quick breath.** Look at the row, not the whole week.
2. **Urges first** (0–5 each). Highest point today.
3. **Actions** (Y/N or number). Just the facts.
4. **Emotions** (0–5 each), including joy.
5. **Meds and substances** (what and how much).
6. **Skills used** (0–7), then circle today on the back for each skill you practised.
7. **Optional one line:** what helped, or what set things off.
8. **Check:** if any urge is 4–5, or you acted on a harmful urge, follow your safety plan or contact your therapist's
   coaching line if you have one. If you're in danger, call your local emergency number (in the US, call or text 988, or call 911).

Prompts for the optional line:
- What was the prompting event for the highest number today?
- Which skill did I think about but not use? What got in the way?
- One thing I did that I'm glad about.

## 8. Rhythms

| Rhythm | What happens |
|---|---|
| Daily | Fill one row (2–5 min) |
| Weekly | Card reviewed at the start of individual therapy; a new card starts. Self-guided: 10-minute review of highs, lows, skills that worked. Chain analysis for any marked target behaviour. |
| Every skills module (about 6 weeks per module in the standard group, **uncertain**) | New skills added to the back; old ones stay |
| Monthly | Compare averages or count "days with any action" per week. Look at joy and skills trends, not just distress. |
| Stage change (months) | In DBT, targets change as stage 1 goals are met; the card is rewritten |
| Yearly | Standard DBT for adults is often a one-year programme; many people keep a lighter card after it ends **(common practice)** |
| Archiving | Cards are private health information. Keep old ones somewhere safe; many clinics keep a copy. |

## 12. Community practice

- **What people do:** peer sites (DBT Self-Help, subreddits on DBT and BPD) share modified cards: fewer columns,
  bigger boxes, colour-coded modules, joy and gratitude columns, sleep and pain columns. People often talk about the
  card feeling like homework at first, then becoming a way to "see" a week.
- **Common customisations:** swapping "self-harm" for other targets; adding "dissociation", "sleep hours", "pain",
  "therapy-interfering"; 0–10 scales; a notes column; putting the skills list on a bookmark.
- **Aesthetic vs minimalist:** some keep it in a decorated bullet journal with a weekly spread; others use a bare
  spreadsheet or app. DBT itself doesn't care about looks, only that it's done daily and honestly.
- **Honesty and shame:** a common theme is fear of writing a high number. Clinicians teach that the card is for
  noticing, and high numbers get help, not blame.
- **Caution:** community forms vary in quality and some drop the safety parts. Stick to a card your therapist
  agrees with.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- **Paper:** letter or A4, landscape; or a half-letter card folded into a wallet. 90–100 gsm stops pen show-through if
  printed double-sided.
- **Pen:** anything. A pencil lets you fill in during the day and ink at night.
- **Holder:** a clipboard or plastic sleeve so it survives a bag; a bookmark-sized skills list.
- **Optional:** a small timer or phone alarm for the nightly fill-in.
- No stickers, stamps or colour needed. Colour coding of modules is optional; black-and-white works.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

- **Apps:** many DBT diary card apps offer reminders, charts and PDF export for a therapist. Check privacy: this is
  sensitive health data; prefer apps that store locally and don't sell or share data.
- **Spreadsheets:** easy trend charts; DBT Self-Help offers a spreadsheet-style card.
- **E-ink:** a PDF card on a reMarkable, Kindle Scribe or Boox works well; handwriting stays private and offline.
- **Scanning:** a photo each week for the therapist; don't upload to general cloud folders without thinking about who
  can see it.
- **Gained digitally:** reminders, charts, no lost cards. **Lost:** the calm of paper, the "hand it over" ritual, and
  privacy if the app syncs to servers.

## 16. Evidence and research

| Study | Sample | Finding | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linehan et al., 1991, *Arch Gen Psychiatry* | Women with BPD and repeated self-harm; 1 year DBT vs usual care | Fewer and less severe self-harm episodes, better retention, fewer inpatient days | First RCT; small; women only |
| Linehan et al., 2006, *Arch Gen Psychiatry* | 101 women with BPD and recent suicidal behaviour; DBT vs community experts, 2 years | DBT group about half as likely to attempt suicide; less hospital use | Strong design; women only |
| Neacsiu, Rizvi and Linehan, 2010, *Behav Res Ther* | 108 women across three RCTs | DBT participants used about three times more skills by the end; skills use fully mediated fewer suicide attempts and less depression, and more anger control | Skills measured by a checklist (DBT-WCCL), not only diary cards |
| Linehan et al., 2015, *JAMA Psychiatry* | 99 women at high suicide risk | All three DBT versions cut suicide attempts similarly; skills-training versions better on self-injury, depression, anxiety | Good RCT; supports the skills side |
| Storebø et al., 2020, Cochrane | 75 RCTs, 4,507 people, all therapies for BPD | DBT better than usual care on BPD severity, self-harm and functioning | **Low-certainty** evidence; only severity reached a clinically meaningful cut-off |
| Klein, Skinner and Hawley, 2013, *Psychotherapy* | Women with binge eating disorder or bulimia; 16 weeks | Supported diary-card self-monitoring and group DBT both gave large improvements in bingeing; group better on body image; diary-card arm kept people in treatment better | The only trial found that tests the card as the main intervention; small; supported by a clinician, not alone |
| Probst et al., 2018, *Front Psychiatry* | 44 inpatients with BPD, 5 weeks | Suicidal ideation lower on days with successful skill use | Naturalistic; single diary items |
| McCool et al., 2023, *Subst Use Misuse* | 48 people in a DBT partial hospital | Mindfulness skills on the diary card predicted lower urges to use the same and next day; 71.5% of urge items completed | Small; single site; one skill mark per day |
| Bitran et al., 2025, *Cogn Ther Res* | 95 adults with BPD, 6 months DBT | Daily joy rose; rises predicted later drops in BPD severity | Diary-card data; observational |

**Claimed vs supported:**
- *Supported:* DBT (with the card) reduces self-harm and suicidal behaviour in BPD; skills use is a mechanism;
  diary-card data track skill use and daily states well enough for research.
- *Partly supported:* the card as a self-help tool (only one small clinician-supported trial).
- *Claimed, not isolated in trials:* that the card by itself improves outcomes, strengthens alliance, or reduces
  shame. These are clinical rationale **(uncertain)**.
- Completion is a known issue: in McCool et al. about 28% of possible urge ratings were missing.

## 17. Benefits

- Fewer self-harm and suicidal behaviours when used within DBT: **evidenced** (for DBT as a package).
- Makes skills use visible and trackable: **evidenced** (used as a research measure).
- Links skill use to lower same-day distress: **evidenced** (observational).
- Supported diary-card self-monitoring helps binge eating: **evidenced** (one small trial).
- Joy ratings show progress that distress numbers miss: **evidenced** (one study).
- Faster, more focused therapy sessions: **claimed**.
- Notices patterns (shifts, sleep, pain) that trigger urges: **claimed**, widely reported.
- Separating urges from actions reduces shame: **claimed**.
- A 3-minute, low-writing habit that works on bad days: **claimed**.

## 18. Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---|---|
| Too many columns; stops after a week | Start with 3 emotions, 2 urges, 1–2 actions, skills 0–7 |
| Filling in the whole week from memory | Tie it to a nightly cue; tick "how often" honestly; a partial card is still useful |
| Shame about high numbers or actions | Remember the card's purpose; tell your therapist that writing it is hard (that itself gets worked on) |
| Using it alone while urges to self-harm are high | Get support: a DBT programme, your doctor, 988; keep a safety plan at hand |
| Dwelling: rating distress makes it louder | Do it quickly, include joy, add one "what helped" line, stop there |
| Not knowing the skills names | Keep a skills bookmark; use circle-the-word lists |
| Card found by others | Use initials and your own code words for urges; keep it in a private place |
| Only tracking bad things | Add joy, skills that helped, one good thing |
| Numbers feel meaningless | Write anchors: what does "3" mean for you? Keep them the same all month |
| Perfectionism about which skill "counts" | Any honest attempt counts; the scale has room for "tried, didn't help" |

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **ADHD:** fewest possible columns; same time daily tied to an existing habit; bubbles instead of writing; a
  visible place (X4 sleep screen). See [adhd-journaling](adhd-journaling.md).
- **Autism and sensory needs:** explicit written anchors for each number; allow "can't tell" as a valid mark; add a
  sensory load column; emotion words can be hard, so use body cues ("tight chest") too. See
  [autistic-sensory-journaling](autistic-sensory-journaling.md).
- **Chronic illness and low spoons:** mark spoons and pain; a minimum version (one urge, one feeling, skills 0–7) for
  bad days; pain here is physical, distinct from the card's emotional "pain" column (label clearly).
- **Depression and anxiety:** keep joy and "what helped"; don't reread the week on a low night; review with someone.
- **Trauma:** don't write details; use code words; chain analysis should be done with a therapist when it involves
  trauma triggers.
- **Dyslexia and dysgraphia:** bubbles and circle-the-word lists, no required writing; large print skills list.
- **Low vision:** large-print card, 14 pt+, thick lines, high contrast; or voice notes; one item per row.
- **Motor or hand pain:** bubbles or ticks only; a bigger card; digital with large buttons (the X4 buttons).
- **Trans and gender-diverse people:** add dysphoria as an emotion if useful; misgendering and minority stress can be
  prompting events, and the card should allow naming them without judgement; find a DBT therapist who is affirming;
  Trans Lifeline is peer-run and does not call emergency services without explicit request.
- **Shift workers:** rate by "waking day" not calendar day; mark the shift type; fill in after the last sleep of the
  day rather than at midnight.
- **Low income:** cards are free from UW BRTC and peer sites; handwritten grids work; many community mental health
  centres offer DBT skills groups on sliding scales **(varies by area)**.

## 21. Combining

- **Safety plan:** the card notices urges; the plan says what to do when they're high. Put a pointer to the plan on
  the card. See [safety-planning](safety-planning.md).
- **CBT thought record:** use a thought record for the one moment behind the day's highest number. See
  [cbt-thought-record](cbt-thought-record.md).
- **Mood tracking:** a year-in-pixels colour for the day can be derived from the card's feelings. See
  [mood-tracking-year-in-pixels](mood-tracking-year-in-pixels.md).
- **Habit tracking:** PLEASE skills (eat, sleep, move, meds) are habits; habit dots cover them. See
  [habit-tracking](habit-tracking.md).
- **Symptom tracking:** pain, sleep and medication overlap; keep one place for each number. See
  [symptom-tracking](symptom-tracking.md).
- **Stoic journaling:** evening review has a similar "what did I do, what next" shape, without the scales. See
  [stoic-journaling](stoic-journaling.md).
- **Expressive writing:** avoid pairing long trauma writing with a high-urge day; see
  [expressive-writing](expressive-writing.md) for its own cautions.
- **ADHD and sensory journaling:** see [adhd-journaling](adhd-journaling.md) and
  [autistic-sensory-journaling](autistic-sensory-journaling.md).

## 24. FAQ

**Do I need a therapist to use a diary card?**
It was designed for DBT with a therapist, and the evidence is for that. Using it alone as a check-in is reasonable, but
if urges to self-harm or suicide are part of your life, pair it with professional help and a safety plan.

**Is 0–5 or 0–10 better?**
Neither is proven better. 0–5 is the classic BRTC card and quicker to circle. Pick one and keep it.

**Highest of the day or average?**
Both are used. Most people find "highest" easier to remember. Decide once and note it on the card.

**What if I didn't use any skills?**
That's a 0, 1 or 2. Those are useful: 2 ("wanted to") tells you what got in the way.

**What if I don't want to write down that I self-harmed?**
Talk to your therapist about that fear; it's common and it's workable. You can use a code word or a symbol you both
understand. If you're not in therapy, a doctor or 988 can help you find care.

**Isn't writing about urges going to make them worse?**
There's no good evidence that rating urges increases them, but some people find it makes them dwell. Keep it fast,
include joy, and stop after the row **(uncertain: not directly studied)**.

**Can I use it for things other than BPD?**
Yes. DBT skills are taught widely for emotion dysregulation, substance use, eating problems and more. The card adapts:
change the targets.

**Should the X4 or my journal warn anyone if I rate high?**
No. Journalwright Studio never sends alerts. Your plan and your people are the response; you choose when to reach out.

**How long do people keep cards?**
Through DBT (often a year) and sometimes long after in a lighter form.

**Can I share it with my doctor or psychiatrist?**
Yes; a week's card is a good summary of meds taken and hard days.

### e. Don't adopt

- **Printing "suicide" or "self-harm" as default labels.** Let the person name urges; the page may be seen by others.
- **Alerts, scores, streaks or colours for "good" and "bad" days.** Out of scope and shaming.
- **Risk ratings or "danger levels".** That's clinical assessment, not a journal's job.
- **Copying the BRTC card verbatim** as a product page. It's free for clinical use but not commercial; KDP books are
  commercial. Use original layout and wording, and point to UW BRTC for the original.
- **Scan uploads of urge and action data by default.** Route to a private destination only.
- **Generative AI "coaching" on the e-ink companion.** Out of scope.
- **Detailed behaviour fields** (method, amount of harm). Not needed and not safe to prompt for.

## 26. Open questions

- Does the diary card itself, apart from DBT, improve outcomes? Only one small supported trial (binge eating) tests it.
- Is 0–5 or 0–10 more reliable for daily emotion ratings? No head-to-head data found.
- "Highest" versus "average" daily ratings: which predicts outcomes better? Not settled.
- Exact date the diary card first appeared in DBT: not confirmed.
- Whether the 2025 revised handouts change the diary card layout: not confirmed (publisher page doesn't say).
- Behavioral Tech's founding year and evidence page could not be opened (blocked); marked uncertain.
- Whether rating urges daily raises distress for some people: not directly studied.
- Best way to show a 0–7 scale with meanings on a 1-bit screen without clutter: design question for the X4.
